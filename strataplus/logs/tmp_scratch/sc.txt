Abstract. How many semiprimes N ≤ X have, for some modulus M ≥ 2, no Fermat witness modulo M ,
and what is the shape of their count? Write G(N ) for the gcd of r − 1 over the nonzero Fermat residues
r = aN −1 mod N of N , over all bases 0 ≤ a < N : a modulus M has no witness at N exactly when
M | G(N ), and the count is the number of pairs of primes 2 < p < q with pq ≤ X√and G(pq) > 1. From
the published Bourgain–Glibichuk–Konyagin bound alone the count is at most (⌊ X⌋ + 1)(Q + ⌈X γ ⌉)2 ,
for a threshold Q that depends on γ only and comes from that bound, that is ≪γ X 1/2+2γ for every
γ > 0. If the Bateman–Horn conjecture holds for the pairs of primes p, qj (p) = 1 + j(p − 1),   √ and a fixed
positive proportion of those pairs have G(p qj (p)) > 1 (a density hypothesis), it is at least c X/(log X)2 .
At j = 5 that proportion is expected to be δ5 = 31/4096 ≈ 0.00757, against 0.00771 measured on the
45 126 family members with p < 6 · 106 (experiments/delta5_geometric.py), and it is carried by the
single class p ≡ 7 (mod 8). The exponent 1/2 is conditional in one direction only: the upper side rests on
a published theorem, the lower side on open conjectures.




                                          1. Introduction
    How many semiprimes below X have, for some modulus M ≥ 2, no Fermat witness
modulo M , and what shape does that count take? Here a base a is a Fermat witness modulo
M for an odd N when its residue r = aN −1 mod N is nonzero and r ̸≡ 1 (mod M ); at a
prime N no modulus has one, since every nonzero residue is 1. Over the 138 233 258 odd
composites N ≤ 3.1 · 108 with at least two prime factors, an exhaustive computation (the
census of Section 3, script experiments/blind_modulus_census_c) leaves 22 composites
at which some modulus has no witness, and 21 of those 22 are Carmichael numbers. The
census gives the count of composites at which some modulus M ≥ 2 has no witness, that
is of the composites with G(N ) > 1, at that one scale. The shape is a two-sided statement
about the semiprimes: an upper end ≪γ X 1/2+2γ  √     for every γ > 0, from one published
exponential-sum estimate, and a lower end c X/(log X)2 , from a prime-pair count for
one family of pairs together with the density of that family at which a modulus has no
witness.
    Section 2 defines the vocabulary and proves what is used of it. For an odd number
N = 2x − 1 the invariant G(N ) is the gcd of r − 1 over the nonzero Fermat residues r
of N , at all bases, and a modulus M has no witness at N exactly when M | G(N ). The
count is taken over the pairs of primes 2 < p < q with pq ≤ X and G(pq) > 1, so that
each such pair carries a modulus with no witness. The second count of this article is for a
class-level rule, taken from [18]: a base is a witness for it when its residue r is nonzero
and r mod 4 is 3 or (N − 1) mod 4. After the census, one composite is not Carmichael:
N = 300 979 369, with G(N ) = 2, recorded as blind_modulus_off_carmichael[L] and
cited to the same article; no two of its nonzero residues r, r′ have r − 1 and r′ − 1 coprime
(not_hasCoprimeAttainableOffsetPair_off_carmichael[L] ), since such a pair would
force G(N ) = 1. The row, and the anatomy behind it, are Section 3. The markers [L]
and [L*] record, respectively, a statement machine-checked with no sorry anywhere in its
dependency graph in this repository’s Lean development, and one machine-checked from a
named hypothesis written into its statement.

    The tests are not a practical primality test: they read all N bases, at Θ(N ) exponenti-
ations. That these tests can detect Carmichael numbers is not evidence of depth: there
every witness is a base sharing a factor with N . With those constraints, the exponent 1/2
is conditional in one direction only: the upper side rests on a published theorem not yet
in Mathlib, and only the lower side on open conjectures.
    The route is the count, then the shape, then where the count sits. Section 2 fixes the
invariant and proves the facts about it that the rest uses. Section 3 records the census.
Section 4 fixes the sets that are counted. Section 5 turns the size of one local image into
an unconditional upper bound. Section 6 puts the published estimate into that bound.
Section 7 supplies the matching lower shape, and with it the exponent. An appendix,
Section A, carries the same estimate over to the pairs at which the class-level rule has no
witness: a degree-eleven minorant of the Fejér kernel puts a coset element in every class
modulo 4, and the missed semiprimes are counted. Section 8 asks which members of the
family can carry a modulus with no witness at all, and states the quantitative form of the
count.
                            2. Preliminaries on the invariant
    Every count in this article counts composites with G(N ) > 1, and all of them are read
off one invariant of N . This section defines it and proves the facts about it that the later
sections use: the equivalence that turns a filter into a divisibility, the divisibility of N − 1
by it, the local picture at a prime factor, the criterion that one large local image settles,
and the collapse that a 2-adic separation forces. The invariant and these facts are [17]’s,
and are proved here again.

                   r = (m − 1)N −1 mod N ∈ {0, 1, . . . , N − 1}      (resid),
    and the invariant is the greatest common divisor of r − 1 over the nonzero residues:

Lemma 2.2 (dvd_residueGcd_iff_forall_attainable[L] ). Let N ≥ 3 be odd. For
every modulus M , no base is a witness at M if and only if M | G(N ). In particular G(N )
divides r − 1 for every nonzero Fermat residue r of N .
Proof. In the gcd defining G(N ), the base m contributes the term r − 1 when its residue
is nonzero and the term 0 when that residue is 0. If M | G(N ) then M divides each term,
so M | r − 1 wherever r ̸= 0, and no base is a witness. Conversely, if no base is a witness
then each term is a multiple of M — r − 1 by hypothesis, and 0 because every modulus
divides it — hence M divides the gcd. The last clause is the case M = G(N ).             □
Lemma 2.3 (residueGcd_dvd_sub_one[L] ). Let N ≥ 3 be odd, and let N = de with
d, e > 1 coprime. Then G(N ) | N − 1.
Proof. By the Chinese remainder theorem there is E with 0 ≤ E < N , E ≡ 1 (mod d)
and E ≡ 0 (mod e). Since d > 1 the congruence modulo d excludes E = 0, and a positive
multiple of e is at least e ≥ 2; so 2 ≤ E ≤ N − 1. An idempotent is its own Fermat
residue: E 2 ≡ E (mod N ) gives E k ≡ E for every k ≥ 1, so the base E has residue E
(resid_of_idem). Its complement E ′ = N + 1 − E lies in [2, N − 1] as well, is idempotent
modulo N , and is 0 modulo d and 1 modulo e. Both E and E ′ are nonzero residues, so
Lemma 2.2 gives G(N ) | E − 1 and G(N ) | E ′ − 1; their sum is N − 1.                 □
Lemma 2.4 (side_resid_pin[L] ). Let N ≥ 3 be odd, let p be a prime with N = pc and
gcd(p, c) = 1, let u be an inverse of c modulo p, and let b be a base prime to p. Then the
base that is b modulo p and 0 modulo c has residue
                           cX,      X = u bN −1 mod p ∈ (0, p).
Proof. Take w ∈ {0, . . . , N − 1} with w ≡ b (mod p) and w ≡ 0 (mod c), which exists
because p and c are coprime. Its residue is bN −1 modulo p and 0 modulo c. Put X =
ubN −1 mod p, so that 0 ≤ X < p; then cX is 0 modulo c, and cX ≡ cu bN −1 ≡ bN −1
(mod p). Two integers in [0, N ) that agree modulo p and modulo c agree modulo their
product pc = N , hence are equal, and cX < cp = N ; so the residue is cX. It is nonzero:
were p | ubN −1 , the prime p would divide u or b, and both are units modulo p.       □
Lemma 2.5 (dvd_prime_sub_side[L] ). In the setting of Lemma 2.4, with c > 1, let
M | G(N ). Then M | p − X for every X ∈ u Sp : read as integers in (0, p), the whole coset
u Sp lies in the class of p modulo M .
Proof. The integer X has the form u bN −1 mod p for a base b prime to p, so cX is a nonzero
residue (Lemma 2.4) and M | cX − 1 (Lemma 2.2). At the split N = p · c the invariant
divides N − 1, so M | pc − 1 (Lemma 2.3). Subtracting, M | c (p − X). The divisibility
M | cX − 1 makes c a unit modulo M , since a common divisor of M and c divides cX
and cX − 1; hence M | p − X.                                                             □
Lemma 2.6 (mul_card_powRange_le[L] ). In the setting of Lemma 2.4, with c > 1, let
M | G(N ) and M > 0. Then
                            M · #Sp ≤ p − 1.
Proof. Multiplication by the unit u maps Sp bijectively onto the coset u Sp , so the two
sets have the same number of elements. Each X ∈ u Sp satisfies 0 < X < p and X ≡ p
(mod M ) (Lemma 2.5), so p − X is a positive multiple of M not exceeding p − 1: thus
X = p − M k with 1 ≤ k ≤ (p − 1)/M . The integer k = (p − X)/M is determined by X,
so distinct elements of the coset give distinct k, and #Sp ≤ (p − 1)/M . Multiplying by M
gives the claim.                                                                        □

Proposition 2.12 (residueGcd_eq_one_of_padicValNat_lt[L] ). Let N ≥ 3 be odd, let
p be a prime dividing N , and suppose that N is not a power of p. If
                                  v2 (N − 1) < v2 (p − 1),
then G(N ) = 1.
Proof. Write N = pk e with k = vp (N ), so that e > 1 and gcd(pk , e) = 1. Put a = v2 (p − 1):
then 2a | p − 1 and 2a ∤ N − 1.
     A local −1. The group (Z/pk )× is cyclic of order φ = pk−1 (p − 1), which 2a divides.
Let g = gcd(N − 1, φ) and write φ = gL. If L were odd, then 2a , being coprime to L,
would divide g, hence N − 1, against the hypothesis; so L is even and 2g | φ. In a cyclic
group of order φ the element −1 is the unique element of order 2, and the image of the
(N − 1)-st power map is the subgroup of order φ/g = L; that order being even, the image
contains −1. There is thus a base b with bN −1 ≡ −1 (mod pk ).
     A pair of residues summing to N . By the Chinese remainder theorem there are bases
w, w′ ∈ {0, . . . , N − 1} whose residues are, at w, −1 modulo pk and 0 modulo e, and, at
w′ , 1 modulo pk and 0 modulo e. Both residues are nonzero, and rw + rw′ is divisible by
pk and by e, hence by their product N ; lying in (0, 2N ), it equals N .
     Then G(N ) divides (rw −1)+(rw′ −1) = N −2 (Lemma 2.2) and, at the split N = pk ·e,
also N − 1 (Lemma 2.3). Hence G(N ) | 1, which is to say G(N ) = 1.                        □

                                     4. Counting sets
    The census counts composites at which a modulus has no witness, one at a time. To
ask how many there are — and with what shape — one needs the two finite sets the count
is taken over: a family of semiprimes, indexed by a prime, whose count can be taken prime
by prime, and the global set of semiprimes below a bound at which a modulus has no
witness at all. This section fixes both, states the growth shape against which the later
bounds are read, and records the injection that turns every count taken on the family into
a count in the global set.

    Definition 4.1. For j, n ∈ N the second linear form of the family is
                                     qj (n) = 1 + j(n − 1)
    (familyQ). For j ≥ 2 and X ∈ N let the family pairs at scale X be the odd primes
    p whose partner qj (p) is again prime and satisfies p qj (p) ≤ X (familyPairs). The
    family-exceptional primes are those family pairs whose semiprime p qj (p) has a modulus
    with no witness at it (familyBlind), and the family-missed primes are the same pairs
    with the class-level rule’s filter in place of that one (familyMiss).
     The definition unfolds to familyQ_eq[L] . The partner strictly exceeds its prime,
p < qj (p) for j ≥ 2 and p ≥ 3 (lt_familyQ[L] ), and it is bounded by the product of the
two parameters, qj (n) ≤ jn for j, n ≥ 1 (familyQ_le_mul[L] ): the two facts the injection
below uses, the first for the order of the pair and the second wherever the scale of the count
has to be compared. At j = 3 the partner is q3 (p) = 3p−2 for p ≥ 1 (familyQ_three[L] ), so

Theorem 4.4 (card_familyBlind_le_blindSemiprimes[L] ). Let j ≥ 2 and X ∈ N. The
map p ↦→ (p, qj (p)) sends every family-exceptional prime for (j, X) to an exceptional
semiprime below X, and it is injective on that set. Consequently the number of family-
exceptional primes for (j, X) is at most the number of exceptional semiprimes below
X.
Proof. Let p be a family-exceptional prime. Unpacking the two filters, p is an odd prime,
qj (p) is prime, p qj (p) ≤ X, and G(p qj (p)) > 1. Since p ≥ 3, the product bounds the
partner as well: qj (p) ≤ p qj (p) ≤ X. The partner exceeds its prime, p < qj (p), because
qj (p) − p = (j − 1)(p − 1) > 0 for j ≥ 2 and p ≥ 3. The pair (p, qj (p)) therefore satisfies
every clause of an exceptional semiprime below X, so the assignment is a map into the
exceptional semiprimes (familyBlind_mem_blindSemiprimes[L] ). Two family-exceptional
primes with the same image have equal first coordinates, so the assignment is injective.
A finite set that injects into another has at most that other’s cardinality, and the claim
follows.                                                                                   □
    Exchanging the filter G(p qj (p)) > 1 for that of the class-level rule leaves the argument
unchanged: a family-missed prime has its pair among the missed semiprimes below
X (familyMiss_mem_missedSemiprimes[L] ), and hence the family-missed count is at
most the global missed count (card_familyMiss_le_missedSemiprimes[L] ). The global
exceptional count is bounded in Section 5, and the global missed count in Theorem A.5.
    The exceptional composite of the census (Section 3) is a member of the family at

the only one below 3.1 · 108 . The first coordinate of the member is the prime 7759 ≡ 7

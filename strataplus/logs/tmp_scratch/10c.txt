
\section{The Face on the Roots of Unity}
\label{sec:face}

The previous section left the expression of 2009 as a function of one point of the
unit circle, $(-\zeta)^{A(x,m)}$, folded by the outer power $^{N/2}$, which makes its value a sign. This section does the reading without folding anything down. At rung $M$
the reading of a residue is the $\mu_M$-valued \emph{face}
\[
  W_{M}(r) \;=\; \exp\Bigl(\frac{2\pi i\,(r-1)}{M}\Bigr),
\]
a continuous function of the same point, and the question the Part has been asking
--- which moduli see $N$ --- becomes the question of which $M$ make the face equal
$1$ at every nonzero residue. The same expression is studied for its own sake in
\cite{Companion-complex-formula}; the reading below asks what it settles.

The section settles two things and bounds a third. The face of the invariant is an identity: the moduli with
trivial face are exactly the divisors of $G(N)$, so the reduction of Part~V is a
statement about an annihilator on the circle (\S\ref{sec:face-invariant}).
Read at $M = 4$, the same identity says what the criterion of 2009 was reading and
where the two fibres the criterion names sit among the four (\S\ref{sec:face-fibres}). What is
bounded is the other direction, from a formula back to a modulus: one member of the
coefficient family reads exactly one bit (\S\ref{sec:face-member}), products of
members settle a congruence class of $N$ and nothing more (\S\ref{sec:face-products}),
and the $2$-adic tower's fire vector leaves exactly the parity of the residue
undetermined
(\S\ref{sec:face-tower}). The section therefore says what the complex presentation
of 2009 was reading and what no product of its members can settle; it claims
neither to have found a faster filter nor to have identified a new invariant.

\subsection{The face of a rung}
\label{sec:face-rung}

Throughout $x \ge 2$, $N = N(x) = 2x-1$, and $1 \le m \le N$ is a base of the
window of Definition~\ref{def:rung-filter} of \S\ref{sec:definitions}, with Fermat
residue $\Res{x}{m}$ (Definition~\ref{def:residue}). The modulus of a rung is a
natural number $M$, and the residue is an integer, so the face is defined on
$\Z$ and not on a range; the theorems that read it as the value of a filter carry
the range hypotheses explicitly.

\begin{definition}[The face of a rung]
\label{def:face}
For $M \in \N$ and $r \in \Z$ the \emph{face of the rung $M$} at the residue $r$ is
\[
  W_{M}(r) \;=\; \exp\Bigl(\frac{2\pi i\,(r-1)}{M}\Bigr)
  \qquad (\lean{Wface}).
\]
At $M = 0$ the quotient is read as $0$, so the face is $1$ there; every statement
below that uses the congruence it encodes carries $M \ne 0$.
\end{definition}

The face is the congruence $r \equiv 1 \pmod M$ written in another coordinate, and
the identity is exact.

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item $W_{M}(r) = 1$ if and only if $M \mid (r-1)$, for $M \ne 0$
  ($\lv$ \lean{Wface\_eq\_one\_iff}).
\item $W_{M}(r) = W_{M}(s)$ if and only if $M \mid (r-s)$, for $M \ne 0$
  ($\lv$ \lean{Wface\_eq\_Wface\_iff}); in particular the face of a residue depends
  on the residue modulo $M$ and on nothing else.
\item $W_{M}(r) = W_{M}(s)\cdot W_{M}(r-s+1)$ ($\lv$ \lean{Wface\_mul}), since the
  exponents $(s-1) + (r-s)$ add to $r-1$.
\end{enumerate}

The second item is what makes the face a function of the class, and the third is
what makes it multiplicative in the argument. Together they give the pairing law of
Part~II on the circle: for complementary residues $e$ and $N+1-e$ the two faces
multiply to a constant,
\[
  W_{M}(e)\cdot W_{M}(N+1-e) \;=\; W_{M}(N)
  \qquad (\lv\ \lean{Wface\_pairing}),
\]
because $(e-1) + (N-e) = N-1$. Read on a complementary pair of idempotents --- the
complement of an idempotent is again one (\lean{idem\_compl},
\S\ref{sec:pairing}) --- the product is $W_{M}(N)$, independent of which idempotent
was chosen ($\lv$ \lean{Wface\_idem\_pairing}). The arithmetic pairing of
\S\ref{sec:pairing} and this product are the same law: what the sum $e + e' = N+1$
does to the filtered value, the product of the faces does to the reading.

Three analytic facts about the face are used later and are collected here once.
The face is an exponential of a real multiple of $i$, so its real and imaginary
parts are the cosine and the sine of the angle ($\lv$ \lean{Wface\_re},
$\lv$ \lean{Wface\_im}), and it is a unit vector for every $M$ and every $r$
($\lv$ \lean{Wface\_norm\_one}); the exponential form
$\exp\bigl(2\pi i (r-1)/M\bigr)$ is the definition read with real argument
($\lv$ \lean{Wface\_eq\_exp\_ofReal}). Two consequences of unit modulus are the
ones the section actually spends. The real part never exceeds $1$
($\lv$ \lean{Wface\_re\_le\_one}), by the triangle inequality or by $\cos \le 1$;
and a face whose real part \emph{is} $1$ is $1$ ($\lv$
\lean{Wface\_eq\_one\_of\_re\_one}), because a unit vector with vanishing imaginary
part is real and positive. That equality case is what turns a sum of faces into a
criterion in \S\ref{sec:face-invariant}, and it is the only place where analysis,
rather than arithmetic, is used in the section.

\subsection{Where the expression of 2009 sits}
\label{sec:face-base}

The base of the previous section's expression is a point of order $N$, and the
order is the first thing the face can see. Since $-\zeta = \exp(2\pi i\,x/N)$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:zeta_base} of \S\ref{sec:origin}) and $x$ is coprime to
$N = 2x-1$ ($\lv$ \lean{coprime\_x\_N}) --- a common divisor of $x$ and $2x-1$
divides $1$ --- the base is a primitive $N$-th root of unity ($\lv$
\lean{neg\_zeta\_isPrimitiveRoot}). Its order is odd, and a primitive root of odd
order never reaches $-1$: $(-1)$ has order $2$, which divides no odd number, so
$(-\zeta)^{k} \ne -1$ for every integer $k$
($\lv$ \lean{isPrimitiveRoot\_pow\_ne\_neg\_one\_of\_odd}, $\lv$
\lean{neg\_zeta\_pow\_ne\_neg\_one}).

So the $\pm 1$ the expression reports is not the base's. It is the fold's:
multiplying the angle of an $N$-th root by $N/2$ lands in $\{\pm1\}$ and produces
$(-1)^{k}$,
\[
  \exp\Bigl(\frac{2\pi i\,k}{N}\cdot\frac{N}{2}\Bigr) \;=\; (-1)^{k}
  \qquad (\lv\ \lean{Nth\_root\_half\_fold}),
\]
whose square is $1$ ($\lv$ \lean{Nth\_root\_half\_fold\_sq}). This is the sharpening
of \S\ref{sec:origin-four} that the face makes visible: the outer power is where
torsion of order two is manufactured, the base cannot supply it, and the two
factors of the modulus $4$ --- the fold and the parity of the filtered value ---
are one bookkeeping fact about a primitive root of odd order and one arithmetic
fact about the coefficient. On a complementary pair of residues summing to $N$ the
manufacture is visible in the sign itself: with both residues nonzero, the two
values $\sigma$ are opposite ($\lv$ \lean{monsterSign\_neg\_of\_sum\_eq\_N}), which
is the pair law of \S\ref{sec:pairing} at the two-valued face.

The prefactor of the expression is of the same kind, and it is worth one sentence
because the notebook treated it as decoration. It is a quadratic character:
$\operatorname{sgn}(x) = -(-1)^{(N-1)/2}$ for every odd $N$ ($\lv$
\lean{sgn\_eq\_neg\_pow\_N\_sub\_one}), which at an odd prime is the negated
Legendre symbol of $-1$, and in general the negated Jacobi symbol ($\lv$
\lean{sgn\_eq\_neg\_jacobiSym}). Nothing in the expression is an independent
geometric choice.

Which orders the base itself can reach is a divisibility. If a power of a primitive
$N$-th root is primitive of order $M$, then $M \mid N$ ($\lv$
\lean{isPrimitiveRoot\_pow\_dvd\_order}); applied to the base of 2009 this reads
$M \mid N$ ($\lv$ \lean{neg\_zeta\_pow\_primitive\_dvd}), and conversely no power of
it is primitive of order $M$ when $M \nmid N$ ($\lv$
\lean{not\_isPrimitiveRoot\_neg\_zeta\_pow\_of\_not\_dvd}). So some power of the
base is a primitive $M$-th root exactly when $M \mid N$ ($\lv$
\lean{exists\_neg\_zeta\_pow\_primitive\_iff}). Every $2^{j}$ and every prime $p$
not dividing $N$ --- $3$ among them, unless $3 \mid N$ --- is outside that set, so a
reading of the residue valued in $\mu_M$ for such an $M$ cannot be got from a power
of the base alone: it would have to change the generator of the root of unity, not
only its exponent. The family of \S\ref{sec:face-member} changes the exponent alone,
and what it reads is the sign the outer fold produces, which is why its values lie
in $\mu_2$ and not in $\mu_M$ for an $M \nmid N$.

Two readings that the complex presentation invites are closed by one fact about
additive homomorphisms, and the fact is the reason the section can say what the
formula was reading rather than what it might have been. For odd $N$ there is no
nontrivial additive homomorphism $\Z/N \to \Z/2^{j}$ ($\lv$ \lean{no\_add\_hom\_odd}),
and more generally none when the two moduli are coprime ($\lv$
\lean{no\_add\_hom\_coprime}), nor when both are odd and coprime ($\lv$
\lean{no\_add\_hom\_odd\_odd}); the shape the section uses is the composite
statement that covers all the $2$-power rungs at once ($\lv$
\lean{no\_add\_hom\_odd\_of\_coprime}). The argument is the order of the image: the
image of $1$ has order dividing $N$ and dividing $M$, hence dividing
$\gcd(N,M) = 1$, so it vanishes. Consequently the branch of the value $\sigma$ of
\eqref{eq:value} --- whether the argument lies below or above $N$, the second
factor of the expression --- cannot be produced by reading the low bits of a
canonical representative through a group homomorphism. It is forced by the choice
of representative, and the book records it as a branch because it is one.

\begin{definition}[Two faces that are also homomorphisms]
\label{def:linear}
For $a \in \Z/(N)$ the \emph{additive character} of the rung $N$ is
\[
  \psi_{a}(y) \;=\; W_{N}\bigl(a\,y + 1\bigr)
  \qquad (\lean{addChar}),
\]
the face at the representative of the class $a y$, plus one; and a homomorphism
$f : \Z/(N) \to \Z/(M)$
\emph{realises} the rung $M$ at $x$ when
\[
  f\bigl(\Res{x}{m}\bigr) \ne 0
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \text{$m$ is a $C_{M}$-witness}
  \qquad (\lean{LinearFaceRealises}),
\]
for every base $1 \le m \le N$ of the window. Both are readings of the residue by
a group homomorphism, and they are the only two the section considers.
\end{definition}

An additive character is a face, and a filter read by one is a filter read
linearly. The projection onto characters does not diagonalise the Fermat map: if
$\psi_{a}(y^{N-1}) = c\,\psi_{a}(y)$ for all $y$ and some constant $c$, then $a = 0$
($\lv$ \lean{no\_additive\_eigencharacter}). The proof is two evaluations. At
$y = 0$ the eigenvalue is $1$, because $\psi_a(0) = 1$ whatever $a$ is. At
$y = -1$ the power $y^{N-1}$ is $1$ --- the exponent is even, so the sign is
irrelevant --- and the eigenvector equation forces $\psi_a(1) = \psi_a(-1)$, that
is, it forces the classes $a$ and $-a$ to coincide, which is $2a = 0$; and $2$ is a
unit modulo the odd $N$, since $2x = N+1$ is $1$ mod $N$. Hence $a = 0$. There is
no Fourier-type shortcut to the residues of the power map, and the negative is a
theorem rather than a remark.

That the branch and the fold are bookkeeping, and not discontinuities, is also a
theorem. Every real function on a finite set of complex points extends to a
continuous function of the plane ($\lv$ \lean{continuous\_interpolation}), and
composing that extension with the point $(-\zeta)^{A(x,m)}$ gives a continuous $F$
with $\operatorname{monsterC}(x,m) = F\bigl((-\zeta)^{A(x,m)}\bigr)$ for every base
($\lv$ \lean{monster\_branchless}). The expression of \S\ref{sec:origin} is
therefore a continuous function of its own base point, on which the two-valued
output is imposed by the fold. It is a projection of the circle onto a sign.

Unlike \S\ref{sec:counts}, \S\ref{sec:pw} and \S\ref{sec:pw-conditional}, every
result of this section is machine-checked
with no hypothesis and with the axiom footprint of the rest of the book: there
is no conditional marker in it, and no open statement. The section ends on a
refuted formulation (\S\ref{sec:face-tower}).

\subsection{The invariant on the circle}
\label{sec:face-invariant}

The face is not a second invariant. It is the invariant of Part~V read
analytically: the same number $G(N)$, in the coordinate in which ``$M$ sees $N$''
becomes ``the face is not $1$''. The identity that says so composes the bridge
between the arithmetic filter and the complex reading with
Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction} of \S\ref{sec:invariant}.

The bridge first. For $M \ne 0$, a base is a $C_M$-witness if and only if it lies
in the window, its residue is nonzero, and its face is not $1$ ($\lv$
\lean{isWitnessMod\_iff\_Wface}); in symbols,
\begin{align*}
  \text{$m$ is a $C_{M}$-witness}
  \;&\Longleftrightarrow\;
  1 \le m \;\wedge\; m \le N \;\wedge\; \Res{x}{m} \ne 0\\
  &\;\wedge\; W_{M}\bigl(\Res{x}{m}\bigr) \ne 1 .
\end{align*}
That is Definition~\ref{def:rung-filter} with the congruence $r \not\equiv 1
\pmod M$ rewritten through the first item of \S\ref{sec:face-rung}. Negating it
turns ``no base is a witness'' into ``every base with nonzero residue has trivial
face'' ($\lv$ \lean{forall\_Wface\_one\_iff\_noWitnessMod}), and the second
statement of \S\ref{sec:invariant} is the same statement about $M$ and $G(N)$.

\begin{theorem}[The face of the invariant]
\label{thm:face-invariant}
\lv{} \lean{dvd\_residueGcd\_iff\_forall\_Wface}
Let $x \ge 2$, let $M \ge 1$, and write $G(N) = \lean{residueGcd}\,x$ for the
invariant at the canonical exponent (Definition~\ref{def:exponent-invariant} of
\S\ref{sec:exponent}). Then
\[
  M \mid G(N)
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  W_{M}\bigl(\Res{x}{m}\bigr) = 1
  \ \text{ for every base } 1 \le m \le N \text{ with } \Res{x}{m} \ne 0 .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The proof composes two equivalences, and the only hypothesis is $M \ne 0$.

First, the bridge: ``no base of the window is a $C_M$-witness'' and ``every base
with nonzero residue has face $1$'' are the same statement, by $\lv$
\lean{forall\_Wface\_one\_iff\_noWitnessMod}, whose proof is the negation of
$\lv$ \lean{isWitnessMod\_iff\_Wface} read at the two sides of the definition of
the witness.

Second, the invariant: ``no base of the window is a $C_M$-witness'' is
$M \mid G(N)$, which is Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction} of \S\ref{sec:invariant}: the
invariant is a gcd of the terms of the window, so a number divides it exactly when
it divides every term, and a term vanishes exactly at a non-witness.

Composing the two, in the direction that starts from the divisibility, gives the
display. The composition is where the face enters and the gcd leaves; nothing else
is used.
\end{proof}

The statement is an identity between a divisibility and a family of values of one
function, and it has three readings that the section uses.

The first is that $G(N)$ is the largest modulus with trivial face, and that it has
one for every composite: at $M = G$ every nonzero residue has face $1$ ($\lv$
\lean{forall\_Wface\_residueGcd\_one}, which needs $G \ne 0$, that is, $N$
composite). So the divisors of the invariant are exactly the moduli the filter
cannot see, which is Part~V's theorem with the modulus named as a value rather than
as a divisor.

The second is a change of coordinates, not an algorithm, and it is worth the
sentence because the complex presentation of 2009 invites the opposite reading.
The equivalence quantifies over the bases of the window, one modular
exponentiation each, exactly as the arithmetic filter does; the modulus became
smaller in the theorems of \S\ref{sec:schedule}, and the work did not, which is
that section's cost paragraph. Reading the filter on the circle changes what is
computed at each base --- a root of unity instead of a remainder --- and changes
nothing about how many bases there are. No sentence of this section bears on the
cost of a filter, and none claims a usable test.

The third reading is the one the examples make concrete. At the counterexample of
\S\ref{sec:refutation} the invariant is $4$ ($\lv$
\lean{residueGcd\_xc\_eq\_four}, \S\ref{sec:korselt}), so every nonzero residue of
$2508013$ has face $1$ at $M = 1$, $M = 2$ and $M = 4$: the absence of a witness
there is the triviality of a character, and nothing is detected because the face is
nothing but $1$. At $1729 = 7\cdot 13\cdot 19$ the invariant is
$1$, so the face at $M = 4$ need not be $1$ at every nonzero residue, and it is not:
at the base $m = 20$ the residue is $456$, which is $0$ modulo $4$, so the face is
$W_{4}(456) = -i$ and the criterion of 2009 fires there. The two numbers are
both Korselt numbers and the face behaves in opposite ways at them: what the face
records is $G$, and $G$ is not Carmichaelness.

Read at $M = N$, the face needs no invariant: the criterion becomes the
classical one.

\begin{theorem}[The face at $M = N$ is the classical criterion]
\label{thm:face-lossless}
\lv{} \lean{prime\_iff\_forall\_Wface\_one}
For $x \ge 2$, the candidate $N$ is prime if and only if
\[
  W_{N}\bigl(\Res{x}{m}\bigr) = 1
  \quad\text{for every } 2 \le m \le N .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The classical criterion of \S\ref{sec:prime-direction} says that $N$ is prime if
and only if $\Res{x}{m} = 1$ for every base $2 \le m \le N$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:prime-iff-resid-one}). It remains to replace the equality of
residues by the equality of faces, under the quantifier, in both directions.

At $M = N$ the replacement is exact. The residue is a representative in $[0,N)$,
and $1 < N$, so the congruence $r \equiv 1 \pmod N$ has exactly one solution in
range, namely $r = 1$: $W_{N}\bigl(\Res{x}{m}\bigr) = 1$ if and only if
$\Res{x}{m} = 1$ ($\lv$ \lean{Wface\_N\_eq\_one\_iff\_resid\_one}). The two
inequalities on the residue are the range facts of
Definition~\ref{def:residue}; the value $1$ of the face is the first item of
\S\ref{sec:face-rung} at $M = N$.

Transporting the equivalence through the quantifier gives the display. The range
$2 \le m \le N$ is not changed, and it need not be: at $m = 1$ the residue is $0$,
which is not $1$, and no face argument is needed to exclude that base.
\end{proof}

The theorem says that the complex presentation of the criterion, at the modulus $N$
itself, is the criterion without loss --- and this is the contrast the section
exists to make. The expression of \S\ref{sec:origin} reads the same residues
through a two-valued face and loses everything but a parity; the face at $M = N$ is
injective on residues and loses nothing. One modulus, two readings of very
different strength.

The universal quantifier of the display can be compressed to a single equation,
and the compression is the equality case of \S\ref{sec:face-rung}. The bases
$2 \le m \le N$ give $N-1$ faces, each of unit modulus with real part at most
$1$; their real parts sum to $N-1$ exactly when each face is $1$, and a face with
real part $1$ is $1$. Hence $N$ is prime if and only if the sum of the faces over the window
equals $N-1$,
\[
  \sum_{m=2}^{N} W_{N}\bigl(\Res{x}{m}\bigr) \;=\; N-1
  \qquad (\lv\ \lean{prime\_iff\_Wface\_sum}).
\]
The scalar form is not used again, and it is stated because it is the shape in
which the criterion can be compared with a character sum; the sum is over the
window, not over the group, and no cancellation is claimed.

Each arithmetic criterion of Parts V and VI has its face, and the section collects
them in one paragraph rather than in five displays, because each is the previous
theorem with a different modulus and the same bridge. For a modulus that does not
divide $N(N-1)$ the prime direction holds with the face at $M$ ($\lv$
\lean{prime\_iff\_forall\_Wface\_of\_not\_dvd}), the complex form of the criterion
of \S\ref{sec:korselt}; at the closed modulus $N+1$ it holds verbatim ($\lv$
\lean{prime\_iff\_forall\_Wface\_succ}); at the least non-divisor of $N(N-1)$ it
holds with $M$ replaced by that value ($\lv$
\lean{prime\_iff\_forall\_Wface\_minNonDivisor}), which is the face of the least
safe modulus of \S\ref{sec:least-safe}; and at the schedule
$M(N) = \lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor + 1$ of Definition~\ref{def:sqrt-schedule} of
\S\ref{sec:schedule} it holds with $M$ replaced by the schedule ($\lv$
\lean{prime\_iff\_forall\_Wface\_sqrtSched}), with the scalar companion of that
schedule summing the faces at $M(N)$ instead of the faces at $N$ ($\lv$
\lean{prime\_iff\_Wface\_sqrtSched\_sum}). What the face adds to those criteria is
not a new test: it is the statement that all of them are the same statement, read
at different moduli.

\subsection{The four fibres}
\label{sec:face-fibres}

The modulus $4$ is the one the notebook chose, and on the circle its face is a
function taking exactly the four values of $\mu_4$, indexed by the classes of the
residue modulo $4$. The values are attained:
\[
  W_{4}(r) = 1 \iff r \equiv 1 \pmod 4, \qquad
  W_{4}(2) = i, \qquad
  W_{4}(3) = -1,
\]
and every face at $M = 4$ is one of $1$, $i$, $-1$, $-i$ ($\lv$
\lean{Wface\_four\_eq\_one\_iff}, $\lv$ \lean{Wface\_four\_two\_eq\_I}, $\lv$
\lean{Wface\_four\_three}, $\lv$ \lean{Wface\_four\_eq\_mu4}). The expression of
2009 reports a two-valued shadow of this four-valued function: the value
$\sigma(x,m)$ of \eqref{eq:value} is $\pm 1$ at every base ($\lv$
\lean{monsterSign\_eq\_pm\_one}), and its sign is the parity of a single integer
($\lv$ \lean{monsterSign\_eq\_bit}).

The 2009 witness condition, read on the residue, is a disjunction of two classes:
$r \ne 0$ and $r$ congruent to $3$ or to $N-1$ modulo $4$ ($\lv$ \lean{W2009},
Theorem~\ref{thm:witness-mod-four} of \S\ref{sec:residue}). That is two of the four
fibres of the face, and the display names them.

\begin{theorem}[The 2009 predicate is two of the four fibres]
\label{thm:face-fibres}
\lv{} \lean{isWitness\_iff\_Wface\_four}
Let $x \ge 2$ and let $m$ be a base with $1 \le m \le N$. Then $m$ is a witness of
the criterion of 2009 if and only if
\[
  \Res{x}{m} \ne 0
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  W_{4}\bigl(\Res{x}{m}\bigr) \in \bigl\{W_{4}(3),\, W_{4}(N-1)\bigr\}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Start from the residue form of the predicate: $m$ is a witness exactly when
$\Res{x}{m} \ne 0$ and the residue is $3$ or $N-1$ modulo $4$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:witness-mod-four}). It remains to read the two congruences as
equalities of faces.

At $M = 4$ the second item of \S\ref{sec:face-rung} says that two faces agree
exactly when the residues are congruent modulo $4$ ($\lv$
\lean{Wface\_eq\_Wface\_iff}). So $r \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ is $W_{4}(r) = W_{4}(3)$,
and $r \equiv N-1 \pmod 4$ is $W_{4}(r) = W_{4}(N-1)$.

Substituting the two equivalences into the disjunction, in both directions, gives
the display. Nothing else is used: the range hypotheses are those of the residue
form, and the arithmetic of the substitution is the translation of a congruence
into an equality of classes.
\end{proof}

The two right-hand values are the fibres, and which classes they are depends on
$N$. The first is fixed: $W_{4}(3) = -1$, the class $3$. The second is
$W_{4}(N-1)$, which is $-i$ when $N \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ and $i$ when $N \equiv 3
\pmod 4$. So the pair of fibres the notebook names is $\{3, 0\}$ in the first class
and $\{3, 2\}$ in the second, while the face's own non-triviality does not depend on
the class of $N$ at all. That discrepancy is a theorem and not an observation: the
condition $W_{4}(r) \ne 1$ is strictly coarser than the 2009 predicate, since there
is always a residue whose face fires and which is not a 2009 witness ($\lv$
\lean{Wface\_four\_ne\_one\_not\_iff\_witness\_mod\_four}), and when $N \equiv 1
\pmod 4$ the class $2$ is such a residue ($\lv$
\lean{Wface\_four\_fires\_on\_class\_two\_not\_witness}).

Two numbers show the difference, and the first of them is the book's running
example at this modulus of the face. At $1729$, which is $1$ modulo $4$, the
nonzero residues are
\[
  1,\ 456,\ 533,\ 742,\ 988,\ 1197,\ 1274,
\]
of classes $1, 0, 1, 2, 0, 1, 2$ modulo $4$: the face takes the values $1$, $-i$
and $i$ there, the fibre $-1$ is empty, and the two 2009 fibres are the classes
$3$ and $0$. The criterion fires at the base $m = 20$, whose residue is $456$ and
whose class is $0$. At $1105 = 5\cdot 13\cdot 17$, also $1$ modulo $4$, the
nonzero residues are
\[
  1,\ 170,\ 221,\ 391,\ 715,\ 885,\ 936,
\]
of classes $1, 2, 1, 3, 3, 1, 0$: all four values of the face occur, the 2009
fibres are $391$, $715$ and $936$, and the class-$2$ residue $170$ --- the
idempotent that is $1$ modulo $13$ and $0$ modulo $5$ and $17$, attained at the
base $171$ --- fires
under the face and is not a witness of 2009. So on the same class of $N$ the
criterion names two fibres out of four, and the two it omits are not empty.

The pairing of Part~II reads on the four fibres as a table, and the table is the
complete one. For odd $n$ and nonzero residues $r + r' = n+1$, the 2009 predicate
holds at $r$ exactly when it holds at $r'$ precisely if $r \equiv r' \pmod 4$, and
both hold exactly in the two cases $r \equiv r' \equiv 3$ with $n \equiv 1$ and
$r \equiv r' \equiv 2$ with $n \equiv 3$ modulo $4$ ($\lv$
\lean{w2009\_complementary\_pair}). On attainable residues summing to $N+1$ the
same table says that the criterion fires on both, on neither, or on exactly one,
according to the two classes modulo $4$ ($\lv$
\lean{witness\_complementary\_pair}). This is where the two fibres and the pairing
law of \S\ref{sec:pairing} meet, and it is the arithmetic behind the observation
that the absence of a witness for the criterion is a condition modulo four.

\subsection{One member, one bit}
\label{sec:face-member}

The family of 2009 is a one-parameter family of readings, and the section now asks
what one of its members can settle. The answer is one bit, and the road to it is
the pairing law of Part~II in the coordinate of the half-angle.

\begin{definition}[The members of the family]
\label{def:member}
For $x \ge 2$, a coefficient $c \in \Z$ and a base $m$, the \emph{inner exponent}
of the member $c$ is
\[
  A_{c}(x,m) \;=\; \bigl(1 + c\,\mathrm{term}(x,m)\bigr) \bmod 2N
  \qquad (\lean{coeffExp}),
\]
its \emph{argument} is the reduction of $2x\,A_c$ modulo the working modulus $2N$,
\[
  s_{c}(x,m) \;=\; 2x\,A_{c}(x,m) \bmod 2N
  \qquad (\lean{coeffArg}),
\]
and its \emph{value} is the branch map of \eqref{eq:value} applied to that argument,
\[
  \sigma_{c}(x,m) \;=\;
  \begin{cases} \phantom{-}1, & s_{c}(x,m) \equiv 0 \pmod 4,\\ -1, & \text{otherwise},\end{cases}
  \;\cdot\;
  \begin{cases} \phantom{-}1, & s_{c}(x,m) \le N,\\ -1, & s_{c}(x,m) > N,\end{cases}
  \qquad (\lean{coeffSign}).
\]
The member \emph{fires} at the base $m$ when its value differs from its value at
$m = 1$, where the residue is $0$ --- the base every filter of the book treats as a
non-witness:
\[
  \text{the member $c$ fires at $m$}
  \quad:\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \sigma_{c}(x,m) \ne \sigma_{c}(x,1)
  \qquad (\lean{CoeffFires}).
\]
\end{definition}

At $c = \operatorname{sgn}(x)$ the member is the expression of \S\ref{sec:origin},
with the exponent and the value of \eqref{eq:inner-exponent} and \eqref{eq:value};
the family is the expression with the coefficient set free. Two reductions make it
readable. The value depends on the base only through the residue, and the argument
has a closed form in the residue:
\[
  s_{c}(x,m) \;=\; 2\Bigl(\bigl(x\,(1 + c\,\Res{x}{m})\bigr) \bmod N\Bigr)
  \qquad (\lv\ \lean{coeffArg\_eq}),
\]
because the term agrees with the residue modulo $N$
(Corollary~\ref{cor:term-residue} of \S\ref{sec:definitions}) and doubling turns a
congruence modulo $N$ into one modulo $2N$. The inner factor of that display is the
\emph{half-angle}
\[
  V_{c}(r) \;=\; \bigl(x\,(1 + c\,r)\bigr) \bmod N ,
\]
a representative in $[0,N)$, and $x$ is the inverse of $2$ modulo $N$, since
$2x = N+1 \equiv 1$. So a member reads the reduced integer $V_c(r)$ and reports the
sign of the doubled argument $2V_c(r)$.

\begin{definition}[The member on a residue]
\label{def:fires}
For $x \ge 2$, $c \in \Z$ and $r \in \Z$, the \emph{sign} of the member $c$ at the
residue $r$ is the branch map of \eqref{eq:value} applied to the argument
$2V_{c}(r)$,
\[
  \operatorname{sgnV}_{N}\bigl(2V_{c}(r)\bigr) \;=\;
  \begin{cases} \phantom{-}1, & 2V_{c}(r) \equiv 0 \pmod 4,\\ -1, & \text{otherwise},\end{cases}
  \;\cdot\;
  \begin{cases} \phantom{-}1, & 2V_{c}(r) \le N,\\ -1, & 2V_{c}(r) > N,\end{cases}
  \qquad (\lean{resSign}),
\]
written $\lean{sgnV}$ as a function of the pair $(N,V)$; and the member
\emph{fires} at $r$ when that sign differs from its value at $r = 0$,
\[
  \operatorname{fire}_{c}(r) \;=\; \bigl[\,\operatorname{sgnV}_{N}(2V_{c}(r))
  \ne \operatorname{sgnV}_{N}(2V_{c}(0))\,\bigr]
  \qquad (\lean{resFire}).
\]
\end{definition}

The two descriptions agree: the value at a base is the sign at its residue ($\lv$
\lean{coeffSign\_eq\_resSign}) and the member fires at a base exactly when it fires
at the base's residue ($\lv$ \lean{coeffFires\_iff\_resFire}). The sign is
$\pm1$ ($\lv$ \lean{coeffSign\_eq\_one\_or}, $\lv$ \lean{resSign\_eq\_one\_or}),
and it depends on the products $c\,r$ and not on the two factors separately: if
$c\,r \equiv c'\,r' \pmod N$ the two signs agree ($\lv$
\lean{resSign\_congr}). That last fact is what lets a product of members be read
through the classes of its coefficients, and \S\ref{sec:face-products} reads it that
way.

The pairing law for one member is the section's fourth display.

\begin{theorem}[A member fires on one half of a pair]
\label{thm:face-member-pair}
\lv{} \lean{resFire\_pair}
Let $x \ge 2$, let $c \in \Z$ and let $r \in \Z$. Then the member $c$ fires at
exactly one of $r$ and $N-r$ if and only if $N \nmid c\,r$; in the notation of
Definition~\ref{def:fires},
\[
  \operatorname{fire}_{c}(r) \oplus \operatorname{fire}_{c}(N-r)
  \;=\; \bigl[\,N \nmid c\,r\,\bigr].
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $V_{1} = V_{c}(r)$ and $V_{2} = V_{c}(N-r)$, both representatives in
$[0,N)$.

The two half-angles sum to the inverse of two. Expanding the definition,
\[
  V_{1} + V_{2} \;\equiv\; x\bigl(2 + cN\bigr) \;\equiv\; 2x \;\equiv\; 1
  \pmod N ,
\]
because $cN$ vanishes modulo $N$; and $0 \le V_{1} + V_{2} < 2N$, so the sum is
$1$ or $N+1$ and nothing else.

On those two possibilities the branch map behaves in one way. A direct check on the
two cases and on the two comparisons with $N$ gives: exactly one of the two signs
differs from the reference value at $V = x$ if and only if $V_{1} \ne x$. The
reference is the sign at $r = 0$, whose half-angle is $V_{c}(0) = x$; at the
exceptional value $V_{1} = x$ the sum forces $V_{2} = x$ as well, since
$1 - x \equiv x \pmod N$ when $2x \equiv 1$, so both signs equal the reference and
neither member fires.

The exception is exactly the vanishing of the product. Since $x$ is a unit modulo
$N$ --- twice it is $1$ --- the divisibility $N \mid x\,(1 + c\,r) - x = x\,c\,r$
is the divisibility $N \mid c\,r$; so $V_{1} = x$ if and only if $N \mid c\,r$. The
dichotomy of the previous paragraph therefore reads: the two fires differ exactly
when $N \nmid c\,r$, which is the display.
\end{proof}

Two corollaries are drawn at once and used below without further comment. The sign
of the member either flips between the two halves of a pair or is the reference on
both ($\lv$ \lean{coeffSign\_pair}), which is the same dichotomy in the language of
\S\ref{sec:face-base}; and no member fires on both halves ($\lv$
\lean{not\_coeffFires\_pair}), the case $N \mid c\,r$ of the display. Together they
give the obstruction that the rest of the section runs on ($\lv$
\lean{not\_coeff\_realises\_of\_pair}): if two attainable residues summing to $N$
are both witnesses of a filter, then no coefficient realises that filter on the
window, because a realisation would have to fire on both. In symbols,
\[
  \forall c \in \Z,\quad
  \lnot\,\bigl(\forall k,\ 1 \le k \le N:\
  \text{$c$ fires at $k$} \iff k \in W\bigr).
\]
The hypothesis is that $W$ holds at two bases whose residues sum to $N$; the
conclusion is that the family has no member for $W$, whatever $c$ is.

The obstruction is tight in both directions, and this is what fixes the size of
what a member can settle. At one end, a member does realise the one-bit filter:
with $c = -2$ the half-angle is $V(r) = x - r \bmod N$, the sign differs from the
reference exactly when $r$ is a nonzero even residue, and therefore the member
 $-2$ realises the
filter $C_{2}$ at every $x$ ($\lv$ \lean{coeff\_realises\_witnessMod\_two}). At the
other end, $c = 0$ gives a constant value and realises $C_{1}$, which has no
witnesses ($\lv$ \lean{coeff\_realises\_witnessMod\_one}). Between the two there is
nothing, and the reason is that the obstruction is met at infinitely many
candidates. For every $M \ge 3$ and every bound $n$ there is $x > n$ with $N(x)$
composite such that no coefficient $c$ realises the rung $M$ at $x$ ($\lv$
\lean{infinite\_not\_coeff\_realises\_witnessMod\_of\_three\_le}): the family of
 2009 realises, pointwise and at arbitrarily large candidates, no filter beyond one
 bit.
The proof splits on the arithmetic of $M$, and each branch supplies a pair of
witnesses summing to $N$ at candidates that are arbitrarily large.

If $M$ has an odd prime factor $p$, take $N = p\,q$ with $q \equiv 1 \pmod{2^{p}}$
prime, which Dirichlet's theorem for the progression $1$ modulo $2^{p}$ supplies
above any bound. The local image at $q$ contains $-1$: the exponent $N-1 = 2x-2$
shares a large enough power of two with $q-1$ --- $2^{p} \mid q-1$ while
$2^{p} \nmid p-1$ --- for the local power map at $q$ to reach $-1$ ($\lv$
\lean{two\_mul\_gcd\_dvd\_of\_two\_pow}, $\lv$
\lean{exists\_pow\_eq\_neg\_one\_mod\_prime}, \S\ref{sec:local}). So there is an
attainable pair with residues congruent to $0$ modulo $p$ and to $-1$ and $1$
modulo $q$ ($\lv$ \lean{exists\_attainable\_pair\_of\_local\_neg\_one}). Both
members of the pair are $0$ modulo the prime $p \mid M$, so neither is $1$ modulo
$M$, and both
are $C_{M}$-witnesses ($\lv$ \lean{not\_coeff\_realises\_witnessMod\_of\_two\_pow}).

If $M$ has no odd prime factor, then $4 \mid M$, and $N = 3q$ with $q \equiv 1
\pmod{12}$ prime gives the pair $\{2q+1,\, q-1\}$ of attainable residues, which are
$3$ and $0$ modulo $4$: two $C_{4}$-witnesses, hence two $C_{M}$-witnesses,
summing to $N$ ($\lv$ \lean{not\_coeff\_realises\_witnessMod\_of\_four\_dvd}).

Either way the obstruction applies, and Dirichlet supplies the candidates above
every bound. The two forms that keep the hypothesis on $M$ are the same argument
stated for a fixed $M$ with an odd factor ($\lv$
\lean{infinite\_not\_coeff\_realises\_witnessMod}) and for $4 \mid M$ ($\lv$
\lean{infinite\_not\_coeff\_realises\_witnessMod\_of\_four\_dvd}). The statement is
about one member at a time, and \S\ref{sec:face-products} asks whether a product of
members does better.

The same machinery gives the concrete instance at the book's running example, and
it is worth one clause because it shows the obstruction at a number rather than at
a family. At $N = 35$ the schedule of \S\ref{sec:schedule} is
$\lfloor\sqrt{35}\rfloor + 1 = 6$ ($\lv$ \lean{sqrtSched\_thirtyfive}), and the bases
$m = 8$ and $m = 15$ are $C_{6}$-witnesses whose residues sum to $35$; so no
coefficient realises that schedule at $35$ ($\lv$
\lean{not\_coeff\_realises\_sqrtSched\_thirtyfive}), which is the pair obstruction
read on a schedule rather than on a fixed modulus ($\lv$
\lean{not\_coeff\_realises\_schedule\_of\_pair}).

The next rung up needs the arithmetic of the classes modulo three, and it is stated
as three readings of one reduced value. The member $c = 2$ fires exactly on the odd
residues ($\lv$ \lean{resFire\_two}), at the bases of the window as at the
residues ($\lv$ \lean{coeffFires\_two\_iff\_resid\_odd}); at a candidate $N = 6h+5$ the member
$c = 2h+1 = (N-2)/3$ fires exactly when the residue is nonzero and
$\lfloor r/3\rfloor$ is even ($\lv$ \lean{resFire\_third}, $\lv$
\lean{coeffFires\_third\_iff}); and at a candidate $N = 6h+3$ the member $c = 2N/3$
fires exactly on the class $r \equiv 1 \pmod 3$ ($\lv$ \lean{resFire\_twoThirds}).
The three reductions are linear on each class of $r$ modulo $3$, which is why they
can be read off a single remainder ($\lv$ \lean{emod\_eq\_of\_eq\_mul\_add}); and
the negation law $\operatorname{fire}_{-c}(r) = \operatorname{fire}_{c}(N-r)$
($\lv$ \lean{resFire\_neg\_coeff}) is the pair law of
Theorem~\ref{thm:face-member-pair} in the coefficient. The rung $C_{3}$ itself is
the condition $r \ne 0$ and $r \not\equiv 1 \pmod 3$ ($\lv$
\lean{isWitnessMod\_three\_iff}).

At $341 = 11\cdot 31$, which is $2$ modulo $3$, the two members $c = 2$ and
$c = (341-2)/3 = 113$ read that rung exactly: their product fires at a base
precisely when the base is a $C_{3}$-witness, so the pair reads the rung at every
residue ($\lv$ \lean{listRealises\_three\_of\_two\_mod\_three}). This is the book's running
example used once, and it is the positive side of the dichotomy of
\S\ref{sec:face-products}: at a candidate that is $2$ modulo $3$ a product of two
members is enough.

\subsection{What Products of Members Settle}
\label{sec:face-products}

A product of members is a product of signs, and the section asks what such a
product can read on the residues. The definitions are the natural ones, and the
answer is a congruence class of $N$.

\begin{definition}[Products of members]
\label{def:product}
For $x \ge 2$, a finite list $cs$ of coefficients and a residue $r \in \Z$, the
\emph{sign} of the product is the product of the signs of its members,
\[
  \Sigma_{cs}(r) \;=\; \prod_{c \in cs} \operatorname{sgnV}_{N}\bigl(2V_{c}(r)\bigr)
  \qquad (\lean{listSign}),
\]
and the product \emph{fires} at $r$ when that sign differs from its value at
$r = 0$,
\[
  \operatorname{Fire}_{cs}(r) \;=\;
  \Bigl[\,\Sigma_{cs}(r) \ne \Sigma_{cs}(0)\,\Bigr]
  \qquad (\lean{listFire});
\]
so a product fires exactly when an odd number of its members do ($\lv$
\lean{listFire\_iff\_listSign}), and its sign is $\pm1$ ($\lv$
\lean{listSign\_eq\_one\_or}). The \emph{target} of a rung $M$ at a residue $r$ is
the $C_{M}$ witness condition on the residue,
\[
  T_{M}(r) \;=\; \bigl[\,r \ne 0 \ \wedge\ r \not\equiv 1 \pmod M\,\bigr]
  \qquad (\lean{target}),
\]
and the product \emph{realises} the rung on the residues when its fires agree with
the target on the whole range,
\[
  \forall r \in \Z,\ 0 \le r < N:\quad \operatorname{Fire}_{cs}(r) = T_{M}(r)
  \qquad (\lean{ListRealises}),
\]
or on the attainable residues only when they agree at the bases of the window,
\[
  \forall m,\ 1 \le m \le N:\quad
  \operatorname{Fire}_{cs}\bigl(\Res{x}{m}\bigr) = T_{M}\bigl(\Res{x}{m}\bigr)
  \qquad (\lean{ListRealisesAtt}).
\]
\end{definition}

The pair law survives the passage to products, and it is the tool the section uses.
The parity of the two fires of a product at $r$ and $N-r$ counts the members whose
product with $r$ is not annihilated:
\[
  \operatorname{Fire}_{cs}(r) \oplus \operatorname{Fire}_{cs}(N-r)
  \;=\; \bigoplus_{c \in cs} \bigl[\,N \nmid c\,r\,\bigr]
  \qquad (\lv\ \lean{listFire\_pair}),
\]
which is Theorem~\ref{thm:face-member-pair} summed over the list. On a unit
residue that count does not depend on the residue at all ($\lv$
\lean{foldr\_dvd\_unit}), because divisibility by $N$ of $c\,r$ is divisibility of
$c$ when $r$ is a unit. Hence two units with different pair patterns rule out every
product of members, of any length and with any coefficients ($\lv$
\lean{not\_listRealises\_of\_pair\_mismatch}): a realisation would equate the two
patterns, since both are functions of the coefficients alone.

The pair obstruction now gives a dichotomy, and the section states it as one
display.

\begin{theorem}[No product reads a rung at four and above, off one class]
\label{thm:face-products-four}
\lv{} \lean{not\_listRealises\_of\_four\_le}
Let $x \ge 3$, let $M \ge 4$, and suppose $M \nmid (N-2)$, that is $N \not\equiv 2
\pmod M$. Then no list of coefficients realises the rung $M$ at $x$ on the
residues:
\[
  \lnot\,\lean{ListRealises}\ M\ x\ cs
  \quad\text{for every list } cs .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The proof splits on the class of $N$ modulo $M$ and produces, in each case, two
units whose pair patterns differ.

If $M \nmid (N-3)$, the units $1$ and $2$ already differ. The residue $1$ is a
non-target --- it is $1$ modulo $M$ --- while $N-1$ is a target, because
$M \nmid (N-2)$ is the hypothesis read at $N-1$; and the residue $2$ is a target,
since $2 \not\equiv 1 \pmod M$ for $M \ge 4$, while $N-2$ is a target because
$M \nmid (N-3)$. So the pattern of the pair $(1, N-1)$ is $0 \oplus 1 = 1$ and that
of $(2, N-2)$ is $1 \oplus 1 = 0$, and the mismatch rules out every list ($\lv$
\lean{not\_listRealises\_of\_not\_dvd}).

If $M \mid (N-3)$, the units $1$ and $4$ do the same work. The unit $4$ is coprime
to the odd $N$, and $1 < 4 < N$ because $x \ge 3$. The residue $1$ is again a
non-target and $N-1$ a target, so the pair $(1, N-1)$ has pattern $1$; and $4$ is a
target because $4 \not\equiv 1 \pmod M$, while $N-4$ is a target because
$M \mid (N-3)$ but $M \nmid (N-5)$ --- otherwise $M$ would divide $2$, which $M \ge
4$ excludes. So the pair $(4, N-4)$ has pattern $0$, and the mismatch again rules
out every list.

In both cases the patterns are computed at units, so by $\lv$
\lean{foldr\_dvd\_unit} they do not depend on which unit represents the pair, and
the obstruction of the previous paragraph applies to an arbitrary list.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis is not an extra assumption: it is the negation of
$N \equiv 2 \pmod M$, which is the one class the theorem leaves open, and the
theorem says nothing about what happens there. What it does say is that for every
modulus from four up the congruence class of $N$ modulo $M$ decides whether a
product can read $M$ at all, and that only one class survives; whether a product
realises a rung at $M \ge 4$ inside that class is not a question this section
answers.

The class where products do read a rung is $M = 3$, and there the dichotomy is
complete --- the section's positive result. At a
candidate $N \equiv 1 \pmod 3$ no product realises the rung ($\lv$
\lean{not\_listRealises\_three\_of\_one\_mod\_three}); at $N \equiv 2 \pmod 3$ the
two members $2$ and $(N-2)/3$ realise it ($\lv$
\lean{listRealises\_three\_of\_two\_mod\_three}); at $N \equiv 0 \pmod 3$ the three
members $1$, $N-1$ and $2N/3$ realise it ($\lv$
\lean{listRealises\_three\_of\_zero\_mod\_three}), the first two giving $[r \ne 0]$
by the pair law and the third reading $r \equiv 1 \pmod 3$. So a product of members
realises $C_{3}$ on every residue exactly when $N \not\equiv 1 \pmod 3$ ($\lv$
\lean{exists\_listRealises\_three\_iff}), and a realisation on the residues is a
realisation at the bases of the window ($\lv$ \lean{listRealises\_witnessMod}).

This is the crux of the section's second half, and it refutes the blanket claim in
one line. A product does realise a rung above two: at $N = 15$, which is $0$ modulo
$3$, the product of the members $1$ and $4$ fires exactly at the $C_{3}$-witnesses
($\lv$ \lean{product\_realises\_witnessMod\_three\_fifteen}, $\lv$
 \lean{not\_coeff\_realises\_of\_pair}), while no single member does. So ``no product of members reads $M$ for $M \ge 3$'' is false as stated, and
what survives is the class condition of Theorem~\ref{thm:face-products-four}. The
two-member statements are worth recording because they show
that the product structure is not a formal trick: a product of two members fires
exactly when exactly one factor does ($\lv$ \lean{productFires\_iff\_xor}), where
the product sign and the product fire are the two-member cases of
Definition~\ref{def:product} ($\lean{productSign}$, $\lean{ProductFires}$); a
product can fire on both halves of a pair when both factors do ($\lv$
\lean{productFires\_pair\_of\_both}), so the pair obstruction does not extend to
products by itself; and a product whose two factors both flip their sign is
invariant on the pair ($\lv$ \lean{productSign\_invariant\_of\_both\_flip}). The
positive instances are the family of those: the pair $(2, (N-2)/3)$ realises
$C_{3}$ at every $N \equiv 2 \pmod 3$ ($\lv$
\lean{product\_realises\_witnessMod\_three\_of\_two\_mod\_three}), with the
instances $35$ and $65$ ($\lv$
\lean{product\_realises\_witnessMod\_three\_two\_mod\_thirtyfive}, $\lv$
\lean{product\_realises\_witnessMod\_three\_two\_mod\_sixtyfive}).

On the attainable residues alone the obstruction is sharper, and the section uses
it to close the last class. Two attainable complementary pairs that are annihilated
by the same coefficients but have different pair patterns rule out every product on
the attainable residues, without any hypothesis on the rest of the range ($\lv$
\lean{not\_listRealisesAtt\_of\_pairs}); and equal annihilators give equal pair
counts ($\lv$ \lean{foldr\_dvd\_congr}), which is what makes the obstruction a
comparison of integers in $\Z/(N)$. The smallest instance is $85 = 5\cdot 17$, a
candidate that is $1$ modulo $3$: the side pairs $(30, 55)$ and $(35, 50)$ ---
residues divisible by $5$, and attainable because $-1$ is in the local image at
$17$ --- have patterns $1$ and $0$, so no product realises $C_{3}$ there, even on
the attainable residues ($\lv$ \lean{not\_listRealisesAtt\_three\_85}). So the
dichotomy of $C_{3}$ holds on the residues in the strong form, on the attainable
set in the weak form, and the two forms agree at $85$.

The product span is a module over the units, and that is the last structural remark
of the subsection. Multiplying the coefficient by a unit of the residue multiplies
the residue instead, $\operatorname{fire}_{c\,h}(r) = \operatorname{fire}_{c}(h\,r)$
($\lv$ \lean{resFire\_mul\_coeff}), and the same identity holds for a whole list
($\lv$ \lean{listFire\_mul\_coeff}). So the products of members are closed under
multiplication of the coefficients by the attainable units, and the question of what
they realise is a question about a submodule of the functions on the attainable set
and not about a set of coefficients.

A linear face is not a way out either, and the section closes the subsection
with that negative because it is what Definition~\ref{def:linear} was for. For
every $M \ge 2$ and every bound $B$ there is a composite $N > B$ at which $C_M$
\emph{has a witness} --- $M \nmid G(N)$
--- and yet no additive homomorphism realises it ($\lv$
\lean{not\_linear\_face\_realises}). The construction takes $N = q^{2}$ with $q >
M+B$ prime: then $N$ and $M$ are coprime, so every additive homomorphism
$\Z/(N) \to \Z/(M)$ vanishes and cannot be a target at all, while
$G(q^{2}) = q$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:prime-power-value} of \S\ref{sec:local}) is not
divisible by $M$. The failure of linearity is therefore not tied to the absence of a
witness:
it happens at composites where the invariant is as small as it can be, and it is a
property of the class of readings and not of the number.

\subsection{The tower, and the bit it does not determine}
\label{sec:face-tower}

The $2$-adic tower of \S\ref{sec:ladder} stacks the moduli $2^{j}$, and the section
ends on it because it is the one place where the comparison of many bits can be
asked exactly and answered negatively. A rung of the tower has a coefficient, an
exponent, an argument and a sign, all of them the constructions of
\S\ref{sec:ladder} at the rung's own modulus
($\lean{towerCoeff}$, $\lean{towerExp}$, $\lean{towerArg}$, $\lean{towerSign}$), and
the sign at $j = 2$ is the expression of \S\ref{sec:origin}
($\lv$ \lean{towerSign\_two\_eq\_monsterSign}). What the tower compares is the sign
with its own reference value, the value at the residue $1$.

\begin{definition}[The tower's fire vector]
\label{def:tower}
For $x \ge 2$ and $j \ge 2$ the \emph{rung-$j$ reference} is the sign of the rung at
the residue $1$,
\[
  \operatorname{ref}_{j} \;=\; \operatorname{towerSign}(x,j,m)
  \quad\text{at any base } m \text{ with } \Res{x}{m} = 1
  \qquad (\lean{towerRef}),
\]
and the rung \emph{fires} at the base $m$ when the sign there differs from it,
\[
  \operatorname{TowerFires}(x,j,m)
  \quad:\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \operatorname{towerSign}(x,j,m) \ne \operatorname{ref}_{j}
  \qquad (\lean{TowerFires}).
\]
The \emph{fire vector} at $m$ over the rungs $2, \dots, J$ is the tuple of these
comparisons; it is a function of the base, and the section asks what it determines.
\end{definition}

The tower is a rewriting of the $2$-power criteria, and the rewriting is exact in
the direction that matters for a filter. The rung $2^{j}$ holds exactly when the
rung $2^{j-1}$ holds or the rung $j$ fires at a nonzero residue ($\lv$
\lean{isWitnessMod\_pow\_or\_towerFires}), which stacks into the flat statement that
$C_{2^{J}}$ holds exactly when $C_{2}$ holds or some rung of the vector fires
($\lv$ \lean{isWitnessMod\_two\_pow\_iff\_towerFires}), and into the collapse that
reads the same fact on the residue: a nonzero residue is $1$ modulo $2^{J}$ exactly
when it is odd and no rung up to $J$ fires ($\lv$
\lean{resid\_one\_mod\_two\_pow\_iff\_towerRefs}). So the tower's fires describe the
witness classes of every $2$-power rung at once, and the vector is the natural
object of the subsection.

It does not determine the residue.

\begin{theorem}[The fire vector does not determine the residue]
\label{thm:face-tower-vector}
\lv{} \lean{exists\_same\_towerFires\_ne\_mod\_two\_pow}
There are $x \ge 2$, $J \ge 2$ and bases $m, m'$ of the window with
\[
  \operatorname{TowerFires}(x,j,m) \iff \operatorname{TowerFires}(x,j,m')
  \quad\text{for every } 2 \le j \le J ,
\]
and yet $\Res{x}{m} \not\equiv \Res{x}{m'} \pmod{2^{J}}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The witness is explicit: $x = 18$, that is $N = 35$; $J = 4$; $m = 2$ and $m' = 4$.

The residues are $1$ and $4$. They differ modulo $2^{4} = 16$, so the two bases are
separated at the last rung of the vector.

Neither base fires at any rung of the vector. For $j \in \{2,3,4\}$ the sign of the
rung equals the reference value at both bases, which is a finite check over the
three rungs and the two bases; hence the two fire vectors are equal, and both are
the all-zero vector.

Equal vectors and distinct residues give the display. The two bases are separated
by parity, and that is the correction of the next paragraph.
\end{proof}

The negative is not the end of the subsection, because the pair the theorem produces
is separated by one bit the vector does not contain. At $N = 35$ the residues are
$1$ and $4$: one is odd and one is even ($\lv$
\lean{q8\_counterexample\_separated\_by\_parity}), and parity is a rung the tower
does not have. Adding it repairs the statement, and the repair is a theorem.

The mechanism is the doubling identity of the half-angle. For a representative
$0 \le r < N$ the doubled half-angle is
\[
  2\bigl((x\,r) \bmod N\bigr) \;=\; r + N\,(r \bmod 2)
  \qquad (\lv\ \lean{two\_mul\_half\_emod}),
\]
whose parity is the parity of $r$; iterating it along the powers of $x$ gives the
congruence $x^{i}\bigl((x\,r) \bmod N\bigr) \equiv x^{i+1} r \pmod N$ ($\lv$
\lean{pow\_mul\_half\_emod}). From the identity one reads a lift: two residues whose
iterated half-angles have equal parities at every step agree modulo $2^{J}$ ($\lv$
\lean{emod\_two\_pow\_eq\_of\_half\_parities}). The rung-$j$ fire reads the parity of
the previous half-angle up to a flip that depends on $x$ and $j$ alone
($\lv$ \lean{towerFires\_iff\_bit}, whose flip is the sign of the negated half
coefficient, $\lv$ \lean{neg\_halfCoeff\_emod}), so equal fire vectors on the rungs
$2, \dots, J$ together with equal parity force the residues to agree modulo $2^{J}$
($\lv$ \lean{resid\_emod\_two\_pow\_eq\_of\_towerFires}).

So the fire vector plus one parity bit is lossless on the low $J$ bits of the
residue, and the vector alone is not. That is the last statement of the section,
and it is a statement about what a comparison of members can settle rather than
about what a modulus can see: the tower reads the residue's bits through signs, and
the signs are one bit short.

\medskip

What the section has shown, in one sentence, is that the invariant is a trivial
character --- the moduli that see $N$ are exactly the $M$ at which the face is not
$1$ --- that the criterion of 2009 is two of the four fibres of the face at
$M = 4$, and that the family of 2009 decides by divisibility and by a class: one
member reads one bit, a product of members reads a rung only when $N$ lies in the
one class the rung allows, and the tower's signs are one bit short of the low bits
of the residue.

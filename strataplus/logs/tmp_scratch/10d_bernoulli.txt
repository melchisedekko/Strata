
\section{The Bernoulli--Demjanenko Identification}
\label{sec:bernoulli}

The previous section left the members as bits: one coefficient, one bit at each
residue, a product firing where an odd number of its members fire, and the pair
law as the only law those bits obey in general. This section does one thing, and
it is not a theorem about the criterion. It names the same bits twice --- once by
the parity of a dilated residue, once by a character sum --- and the naming is
the content: the bits are the rows of a matrix the literature already has, and
their character sums are the Bernoulli numbers that carry the relative class
number. Nothing here is discovered.

The same expression is studied for its own sake in
\cite{Companion-complex-formula}, where the formula is the subject and the
question is what it computes; here the formula is already in hand, in the form
the previous section gave it, and the question is where it sits. What the
section establishes is a claim of location and not of discovery: that each
member of the family of \S\ref{sec:face-member} is a row of the matrix
associated with Demjanenko and studied since
\cite{Tsumura1996Demjanenko,FolzZimmer1987Demjanenko,Schwarz1993Demjanenko}, and
that the character sum of the member with coefficient $1$ is the first
generalised Bernoulli number $B_{1,\xi}$ \cite{Washington1997Cyclotomic}, with an
explicit constant in front of it. The two identities are \S\ref{sec:identity}
and \S\ref{sec:rows}; both are theorems in the strict sense, with no hypothesis
beyond their statements and with the marker $\lv$ alone.

The identities determine the objects rather than resembling them, and they are
used: from the two
 identities, one transfer lemma, the pair law and finite certificates come three
 statements about what products of members can settle: that the pair law is not the only law
(\S\ref{sec:twenty-nine}), the half-units law on a composite with a unitary split
(\S\ref{sec:half-units}), and the law that needs no unit coefficient at all
(\S\ref{sec:semiprime}); between them sits the finite criterion that decides a
single case (\S\ref{sec:certificates}). Two kinds of sentence appear below, and
the section keeps them apart. A theorem carries a marker and the Lean name of the
declaration that proves it. A \emph{reading} is the paper's interpretation of a
theorem --- the division by $N$, the name $B_{1,f}$ for the quotient, the
identification of $(-1)^{h}$ with the odd character modulo $4$, the span sentence
of \S\ref{sec:rows} --- and each is marked as a reading in the sentence that
makes it, because nothing in the module introduces a Bernoulli number or a
matrix. Every measured figure in \S\ref{sec:certificates} carries its script and
its range, and none of them carries a marker.

\subsection{The character sum of a member}
\label{sec:identity}

Throughout this subsection $h \ge 0$, $N = 2h+1$, $K$ is a field with $2 \ne 0$,
and $f : \N \to K$ satisfies
\[
  f(0) = 0, \qquad
  f(N-a) = -f(a) \ \ (0 < a < N), \qquad
  f(2a) = f(2)\,f(a) \ \ (2a < N).
\]
The member with coefficient $1$ has a sign at every residue
(Definition~\ref{def:fires} of \S\ref{sec:face-member}); write $s(r)$ for it, the
value of $\lean{resSign}$ at $x = h+1$ and coefficient $1$. So $s(r) \in \{\pm
1\}$, the member fires at $r$ exactly when $s(r) \ne s(0)$ ($\lv$
\lean{resFire}), and its character sum is $\sum_{r<N} s(r)\,f(r)$ when $f$ is a
character.

The sign is the product of two factors, one reading the parity of the half-angle
$V_{1}(r)$ of \S\ref{sec:face-member} and one comparing it with $N/2$, and at
coefficient $1$ both are explicit, because $h+1$ is the representative of
$2^{-1}$ modulo $N$: twice it is $N+1$. On an odd residue $r = 2k-1$ with
$1 \le k \le h$ the half-angle is $k$, and the sign is $(-1)^{k}$ ($\lv$
\lean{resSign\_one\_odd}). On an even residue $r = 2k$ the half-angle is
$h+1+k$, except at $k = h$, where $r = N-1$ and the half-angle is $0$. At the
exceptional residue the two factors are $+1$ and $+1$, so the sign is
$1 = (-1)^{2h}$; at every other even residue the factors are $(-1)^{h+1+k}$ and
$-1$, so the sign is $(-1)^{h+k}$. In every case, then, the sign is
$(-1)^{h+k}$ ($\lv$ \lean{resSign\_one\_even}); at $k = 0$ this is
$s(0) = (-1)^{h}$.

Two consequences of the hypotheses are used at every step below. From
$f(2a) = f(2)f(a)$, the even values are $f(2k) = f(2)f(k)$ for $k \le h$. From
$f(N-a) = -f(a)$ read at $a = 2(j+1)$, together with $f(2(j+1)) = f(2)f(j+1)$,
the odd value at $2h+1-2(j+1)$ is $-f(2)f(j+1)$ for $j < h$; read at $a = j+1$,
the value at $2h-j$ is $-f(j+1)$.
The three forms say one thing: $f$ is odd about $N/2$ and multiplicative at $2$.

One combinatorial fact remains, and it is the only manipulation of ranges in the
proof. A sum over $[0,2h]$ splits into its even and its odd indices ($\lv$
\lean{sum\_range\_even\_odd}); it also splits at $h$, with the upper half read
backwards as $2h-j$ ($\lv$ \lean{sum\_range\_split\_half}); and the odd indices,
read backwards, are $2h+1-2(j+1)$ ($\lv$ \lean{sum\_range\_odd\_reflect}). Each
is a regrouping of a finite sum, and together they let every sum below be
computed twice and the two values compared.

Put $S_{1} = \sum_{k \le h} f(k)$, $P = \sum_{k \le h} k\,f(k)$, $R =
\sum_{k \le h} (-1)^{k} f(k)$, $A = \sum_{a<N} a\,f(a)$ and $\mathrm{Mon} =
\sum_{r<N} s(r)\,f(r)$. Four relations hold ($\lv$ \lean{monster\_core}, in the
order written):
\begin{gather*}
  \mathrm{Mon} = 2(-1)^{h} f(2)\,R, \qquad 2R = 2\,f(2)\,S_{1}, \\
  A = 2P - N S_{1}, \qquad A = 4\,f(2)\,P - N f(2)\,S_{1}.
\end{gather*}
The first is the two sign formulas applied to a split of $\mathrm{Mon}$ by
parity, with the odd part read backwards. The even part contributes
$(-1)^{h} f(2)\,(-1)^{k} f(k)$ at the index $k$; the odd part, at the residue
$2h+1-2(j+1)$, contributes $(-1)^{h} f(2)\,(-1)^{j+1} f(j+1)$, and summing it
against $j < h$ reproduces $R$ once the term $k = 0$ is dropped by $f(0) = 0$.
The total is twice $(-1)^{h}f(2)R$, as claimed.

The second relation computes the alternating sum $T = \sum_{i<N} (-1)^{i}
f(i)$ twice. Split at $h$ and reflect the upper half: at $j < h$ its term is
$(-1)^{j+1} f(j+1)$, so $T = R + R$. Split by parity and reflect the odd part:
the even part contributes $f(2)f(k)$ at $k \le h$ and the odd part contributes
$f(2)f(j+1)$ at $j < h$, so $T = f(2)S_{1} + f(2)S_{1}$. The two computations of
one sum agree, and the factor $2$ is then divided out in $K$.

The third and the fourth relation compute the weighted sum $A$ in the same two
ways. Split at $h$: the upper half contributes $(2h-j)(-f(j+1)) = -
\bigl(N-(j+1)\bigr)f(j+1)$ at $j$, so $A = P - N S_{1} + P$. Split by parity: the
even part is $2f(2)P$ and the odd part is $2f(2)P - Nf(2)S_{1}$, so
$A = 4f(2)P - Nf(2)S_{1}$. These are the last two relations.

The identity is now one linear combination of the four.

\begin{theorem}[The character sum of the member $1$]
\label{thm:monster-bernoulli}
\lv{} \lean{monster\_bernoulli}
Let $h \ge 0$, let $K$ be a field with $2 \ne 0$, and let $f : \N \to K$ satisfy
$f(0) = 0$, $f(N-a) = -f(a)$ for $0 < a < N$ and $f(2a) = f(2)f(a)$ for $2a < N$,
where $N = 2h+1$. Then
\[
  N \sum_{r<N} s(r)\,f(r)
  \;=\;
  2(-1)^{h}\, f(2)\,\bigl(1 - 2f(2)\bigr) \sum_{a<N} a\,f(a).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
From the second relation, $R = f(2)S_{1}$, since $2 \ne 0$ in $K$. Substituting
into the first gives
\[
  \mathrm{Mon} = 2(-1)^{h} f(2)^{2} S_{1}.
\]
Multiply the third relation by $2f(2)$ and subtract the fourth:
\[
  2f(2)\,A - A \;=\; \bigl(4f(2)P - 2Nf(2)S_{1}\bigr)
  - \bigl(4f(2)P - Nf(2)S_{1}\bigr) \;=\; -N f(2) S_{1},
\]
that is $\bigl(1 - 2f(2)\bigr)A = N f(2) S_{1}$. Multiplying this by
$2(-1)^{h}f(2)$ and substituting the value of $\mathrm{Mon}$ gives
\[
  N\,\mathrm{Mon} \;=\; 2(-1)^{h} f(2)^{2} \cdot N S_{1}
  \;=\; 2(-1)^{h} f(2)\,\bigl(1 - 2f(2)\bigr) A,
\]
which is the display.

In the Lean the same elimination is written as one linear combination of the
four relations, with coefficients $2N$, $2N(-1)^{h}f(2)$, $8(-1)^{h}f(2)^{2}$
and $-4(-1)^{h}f(2)$, whose value is twice the difference of the two sides of
the display; the factor $2$ is cancelled because $2 \ne 0$ ($\lv$
\lean{monster\_bernoulli}).
\end{proof}

The theorem has the following reading. Divide by
$N$. The quotient $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{a<N} a\,f(a)$ is the first generalised
Bernoulli number attached to $f$, written $B_{1,f}$; for a Dirichlet character
$\xi$ modulo $N$ it is $\sum_{a=1}^{N-1} a\,\xi(a)/N$, the value whose $2$-adic
valuation the analytic class number formula reads
\cite{Washington1997Cyclotomic}. And $(-1)^{h}$ is the odd character modulo $4$
at $N$, since $N = 2h+1 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ exactly when $h$ is even. So the
display reads
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bernoulli}
  \sum_{r<N} s(r)\,f(r)
  \;=\;
  2\,\chi_{4}(N)\, f(2)\,\bigl(1 - 2f(2)\bigr)\, B_{1,f}.
\end{equation}
Both moves are readings and not theorems: Lean keeps $N$ on the left of the
identity, and the module introduces neither a Bernoulli number nor a character
modulo $4$. What the theorem says is exact; the reading adds nothing to it but
names, and the names are what make the character sum of a member of 2009
recognisable as a classical object.

The case that matters is the one in which $f$ is an odd Dirichlet character, and
it is a corollary of the display rather than a second theorem. Let $\psi$ be an
odd character modulo $N = 2h+1$ with values in $K$ and $h \ge 1$. Then $f(a) =
\psi(a \bmod N)$ has $f(0) = 0$; it is odd, $f(N-a) = \psi(-a) = -\psi(a)$,
because $\psi(-1) = -1$ is what oddness says; and it is multiplicative at $2$,
$f(2a) = \psi(2)\psi(a)$. The three hypotheses hold, so the display gives
\[
  N \sum_{r<N} s(r)\,\psi(r)
  \;=\;
  2(-1)^{h}\,\psi(2)\,\bigl(1 - 2\psi(2)\bigr) \sum_{a<N} a\,\psi(a)
\]
($\lv$ \lean{monster\_bernoulli\_dirichlet}), which is the same identity with
$\psi$ in place of $f$.

Two readings close the subsection, and both are the paper's. First, in
$\mathbb{C}$ the prefactor does not vanish: $\lvert\psi(2)\rvert = 1$ because $2$
is a unit modulo the odd $N$, so $\lvert 2\psi(2)\rvert = 2$ and
$\lvert 1 - 2\psi(2)\rvert \ge 1$. Hence the identity \emph{determines}
$B_{1,\psi}$ from the character sum of the member, and that sentence is the
section's title in one line. Second, and in the same breath: the identity holds
for every $f$ with the three properties, and every odd character is such an $f$,
but it does not say that every such $f$ is a character. The general statement is
the theorem, and the character is its most important instance.

\subsection{The rows of the Demjanenko matrix}
\label{sec:rows}

A member is a parity before it is a comparison, and the parity is the one the
half-angle already carries. For $x \ge 2$, $c \in \Z$ and $r \in \Z$, with $w =
xcr \bmod N$ taken in $[0,N)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:member-parity}
  \operatorname{fire}_{c}(r)
  \;=\;
  \bigl[\, xcr \bmod N \ \text{is odd} \,\bigr]
\end{equation}
($\lv$ \lean{resFire\_iff\_parity}).

The proof is a case analysis on the two branches of the half-angle. Write $V =
V_{c}(r) = x(1+cr) \bmod N$, so that $V = (x+w) \bmod N$ and $V_{c}(0) = x$. The
sign is the product of the parity factor $(-1)^{[V \text{ odd}]}$ and the
half-comparison $(-1)^{[V > N/2]}$; at $r = 0$ the second factor is $-1$, because
$x = (N+1)/2 > N/2$. If $x+w < N$ then $V = x+w$ also lies above $N/2$, so the
second factor is $-1$ at both residues and the two signs differ exactly when the
parities of $x+w$ and $x$ differ, that is exactly when $w$ is odd. If $x+w \ge N$
then $V = x+w-N \le (N-1)/2$, so the second factor changes between the two
residues while the parity of $V$ is the parity of $x+w$ shifted by the odd $N$;
the two signs differ exactly when the parities of $V$ and $x$ agree, which is
again exactly when $w$ is odd. In both branches the member fires at the residues
where its half-angle parity changes.

Demjanenko's function is the indicator of the upper half, $w \mapsto [w > N/2]$,
and on least residues it is a parity as well: for odd $n$ and $0 \le w < n$,
\[
  n < 2w \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (2w \bmod n) \ \text{is odd}
\]
($\lv$ \lean{demjanenko\_iff\_parity}). If $2w < n$ the residue is the even
number $2w$ and both sides fail; if $2w \ge n$ the residue is $2w-n$, which is
odd because $2w \ne n$, and both sides hold.

Composing the two parities gives the row identity.

\begin{theorem}[Every member is a Demjanenko dilate]
\label{thm:demjanenko-rows}
\lv{} \lean{resFire\_eq\_demjanenko}
Let $x \ge 2$, $N = 2x-1$, and $c, r \in \Z$. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:row}
  \operatorname{fire}_{c}(r)
  \;=\;
  \bigl[\, N < 2\,(x^{2} c\, r \bmod N) \,\bigr].
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $w' = x^{2}cr \bmod N$, a representative in $[0,N)$, and $w = xcr \bmod N$.
Since $2x^{2} = x + xN$, reduction modulo $N$ gives $2x^{2}cr \equiv xcr \pmod
N$, hence $2w' \equiv w \pmod N$. Both $2w' \bmod N$ and $w$ are least residues
modulo $N$, so they are equal. By \eqref{eq:member-parity} the member fires
exactly when $2w' \bmod N$ is odd, and by Demjanenko's parity that is exactly
when $N < 2w'$.
\end{proof}

The theorem has a reading, and the reading is the identification. The member $c$
is row $x^{2}c$ of the matrix $\Delta$ with entries $[N < 2(au \bmod N)]$, read
modulo $2$. Since $2x \equiv 1 \pmod N$ we have $4x^{2} \equiv 1$, so $x^{2}
\equiv 4^{-1}$ and row $a$ is the member $4a$, the map $c \mapsto 4c$ being a
bijection of the coefficient classes modulo $N$. A product of members is the
exclusive or of its members ($\lv$ \lean{parityOn\_listFire},
\lean{card\_listFire\_zmod}), and the rows of $\Delta$ are exactly the members;
hence the $\mathbb{F}_{2}$-span of the products of members is the row space of
$\Delta$ modulo $2$. That sentence is a reading and not a theorem: Lean proves
\eqref{eq:row} pointwise at $c$, and the span follows from it in one line, but
no declaration in the module states the span.

The matrix is not ours, and the section says so where the matrix is first named.
In \cite{Tsumura1996Demjanenko}, Definition~1.1 at $l = 2$ is the parity matrix
of least residues of products, which is $\Delta$ read modulo $2$; the
$2$-divisibility of the relative class number is read off that matrix in
\cite{FolzZimmer1987Demjanenko} and \cite{Schwarz1993Demjanenko}. What is proved
here is the bridge and not the matrix: that the member of 2009 with coefficient
$c$ is the row $x^{2}c$, with the exact row index and the exact dilation, and
that its character sum is the Bernoulli number of \eqref{eq:bernoulli}.

The same member reads a second classical object, and the second reading is a
theorem about the same four sums. The sum of $f$ against the upper-half
indicator is
\[
  \sum_{r<N} s(r)\,f(r)
  \;=\;
  -2(-1)^{h} f(2)^{2} \sum_{u<N} \bigl[\, u > N/2 \,\bigr] f(u)
\]
($\lv$ \lean{monster\_demjanenko}). The proof uses the first two relations of
\lean{monster\_core}. The upper-half sum is $-S_{1}$: on the range $k \le h$ its
terms are zero, and on the upper half, read backwards as $2h-j$, the term at
$j < h$ is $-f(j+1)$, so the sum is $-\sum_{j<h} f(j+1) = -S_{1}$ once the term
$k = 0$ is dropped. Substituting $R = f(2)S_{1}$ into the first relation gives
the display. So on odd characters the member and Demjanenko's function have
character sums that differ by the unit $-2(-1)^{h}f(2)^{2}$, which is the form
in which the identification is used in the literature.

\subsection{The pair law is not the only law}
\label{sec:twenty-nine}

The pair law is the one law that products obey in general: at a pair $r$, $N-r$
the fires of a product differ exactly by the members not annihilated at $r$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:face-member-pair}, $\lean{resFire\_pair}$, and its sum over a
list, $\lv$ \lean{listFire\_pair}; \S\ref{sec:face-products}). Read on a list
that realises a rung, it says that at every residue $r$ with $0 < r < N$ the
 number of coefficients not killed by $r$ has the parity of the exclusive or of the
 two target values,
 \[
   \sum_{c \in cs} \bigl[\, N \nmid cr \,\bigr] \;=\; T_{M}(r) \oplus T_{M}(N-r)
   \qquad \text{in } \Z/2\Z,
 \]
 which is even exactly when the target is symmetric at $r$, and it is what makes a
 rung $M \ge 4$ unreadable off the class $N \equiv 2 \pmod
M$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:face-products-four}, $\lv$
\lean{not\_listRealises\_of\_four\_le}).

On the surviving class the pair law obstructs nothing, and the reason is a
symmetry of the target. If $N \equiv 2 \pmod M$ then for $0 < r < N$ the two
residues $r$ and $N-r$ are both nonzero and $N-r \equiv 2-r \pmod M$, so $N-r
\equiv 1 \pmod M$ exactly when $r \equiv 1 \pmod M$; hence
\[
  T_{M}(N-r) = T_{M}(r) \qquad (0 < r < N)
\]
($\lv$ \lean{target\_pair\_of\_two\_mod}). A realisation equates the two sides of
the pair law with two equal targets, so the displayed sum vanishes for every
 list that realises a rung on that class, and the pair law can never be the reason
 a $C_{M}$ fails there.

At the prime $N = 29$ with $M = 9$ the pair law allows a realisation and none
exists; in the census of \S\ref{sec:certificates} this is the first such pair of
a prime and a modulus.

\begin{example}[$N = 29$, and $C_{9}$]
\label{ex:twenty-nine}
At $x = 15$ the candidate is $N(15) = 29$ ($\lv$ \lean{N\_fifteen}), and $29 =
3\cdot 9 + 2$, so $N \equiv 2 \pmod 9$ ($\lv$
\lean{N\_fifteen\_mod\_nine}). The certificate is the list of eight residues
\[
  \lean{lam29} = (2,\ 14,\ 19,\ 20,\ 21,\ 23,\ 24,\ 25),
\]
and its two properties are finite: every member fires at an even number of them
($\lv$ \lean{parityOn\_lam29\_resFire}), and the target fires at an odd number
($\lv$ \lean{parityOn\_lam29\_target}).
\end{example}

The certificate is read by one function. For a finite list $L$ of residues and a
Boolean $g$ on $\Z$, let $\lean{parityOn}(L,g)$ be the parity of the number of
$r \in L$ with $g(r)$ true, the fold of the exclusive or over $L$ from false.
Four properties make it a tool. It depends only on the values of $g$ on $L$
($\lv$ \lean{parityOn\_congr}); the constant false has parity false ($\lv$
\lean{parityOn\_false}); it is additive over the exclusive or ($\lv$
\lean{parityOn\_xor}); and the parity of a product on $L$ is the exclusive or of
the parities of its members ($\lv$ \lean{parityOn\_listFire}). A member depends
on its coefficient only modulo $N$ ($\lv$ \lean{resFire\_emod}), so the first
property reduces a statement about all $c \in \Z$ to the $N$ coefficient
classes.

No product realises $C_{9}$ at $N = 29$: the predicate
$\lean{ListRealises}\ 9\ 15\ cs$ fails for every list of coefficients $cs$
($\lv$ \lean{not\_listRealises\_nine\_29}). The proof is the certificate. If a list
realised the target on $[0,N)$, then on the eight residues its fire parity would
equal the parity of $T_{9}$, which is true; but the parity of a product is the
exclusive or of the parities of its members, each of which is false, so the
parity of the product is false, a contradiction.

The instance shows two things. The pair law, which holds at every $N$, is not
the only law products obey: at $N = 29$ there is a second. And the second law is
not local to $29$: by \S\ref{sec:identity} the character sums of the
members are Bernoulli numbers, so in the reading of \eqref{eq:bernoulli} a law
that constrains the parity of a product on a set of units is a statement about
the $2$-adic valuation of $B_{1,\xi}$. That statement is the half-units law of
\S\ref{sec:half-units}; the measured form of the link at $29$ is quoted in
\S\ref{sec:certificates} with its script.

\subsection{The half-units law}
\label{sec:half-units}

The second law lives on the units of the lower half. For odd $n$ let
\[
  U(n) \;=\; \{\, u : 1 \le u \le n/2,\ \gcd(u,n) = 1 \,\}
  \qquad (\lean{halfUnits}),
\]
the \emph{half units}, and write $\#U(n)$ for its cardinal. For $a$ coprime to
$n$, Gauss's count is
\[
  \operatorname{flip}(n,a)
  \;=\;
  \#\{\, u \in U(n) : n/2 < au \bmod n \,\}
  \qquad (\lean{flipCount}),
\]
and the fold back into the lower half is
\[
  \operatorname{fold}(n,a,u)
  \;=\;
  \min\bigl(au \bmod n,\ n - au \bmod n\bigr)
  \qquad (\lean{gaussFold}).
\]

Gauss's lemma holds for the units of $\Z/n\Z$ at any odd $n \ge 3$, not only at
a prime:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gauss}
  a^{\#U(n)} \;=\; (-1)^{\operatorname{flip}(n,a)}
  \qquad \text{in } \Z/n\Z
\end{equation}
for every $a$ coprime to $n$ ($\lv$ \lean{gauss\_transfer}). The proof is the
classical one, and it is given because the identification is only as good as the
lemma that makes it non-trivial.

The fold is again a unit: the residue of a product of two numbers coprime to $n$
is coprime to $n$ ($\lv$ \lean{coprime\_mul\_mod}), so the fold of a half unit
lies in $U(n)$ ($\lv$ \lean{gaussFold\_mem}). The fold is injective on $U(n)$: if
two half units fold to the same number, their images in $\Z/n\Z$ are congruent up
to sign, so $u \equiv \pm v$ modulo $n$, and two half units congruent up to sign
are equal ($\lv$ \lean{gaussFold\_injOn}, $\lv$
\lean{eq\_of\_cast\_eq\_or\_neg}). Hence $u \mapsto \operatorname{fold}(n,a,u)$
is a permutation of $U(n)$.

Compare the products of the two sides. On one hand $\prod_{u \in U}(au) =
a^{\#U}\prod_{u \in U} u$. On the other, in $\Z/n\Z$ each $au$ is
$\varepsilon_{u}\operatorname{fold}(n,a,u)$ with $\varepsilon_{u} = \pm 1$ ($\lv$
\lean{gaussFold\_cast}), so $\prod_{u \in U}(au) =
(-1)^{\operatorname{flip}}\prod_{u \in U}\operatorname{fold}(n,a,u) =
(-1)^{\operatorname{flip}}\prod_{u \in U} u$, the last equality because the fold
permutes $U(n)$. The product $\prod_{u \in U} u$ is a unit of $\Z/n\Z$ and
cancels, which is \eqref{eq:gauss}.

Half the totient counts the half units. The units of $\Z/n\Z$ in $[1,n]$ split
into $U(n)$ and its image under $u \mapsto n-u$, an injection onto the units
above $n/2$; both halves are non-empty because $n$ is odd and $n \ge 3$, and the
number of units is $\varphi(n)$. So
\[
  \#U(n) \;=\; \varphi(n)/2
\]
($\lv$ \lean{card\_halfUnits\_mul\_two}); the section writes half the totient
for $\#U(n)$ from here on.

On a unitary split the exponent can be halved. If $n = de$ with $d, e \ge 3$
coprime and $a$ coprime to $n$, then
\[
  a^{\varphi(n)/2} \equiv 1 \pmod n
\]
($\lv$ \lean{pow\_half\_totient}). The totient is multiplicative on coprime
factors, $\varphi(n) = \varphi(d)\varphi(e)$, and both factors are even, so
$\varphi(n)/2 = \varphi(d)\bigl(\varphi(e)/2\bigr) =
\varphi(e)\bigl(\varphi(d)/2\bigr)$. Euler's theorem at $d$, raised to the power
$\varphi(e)/2$, gives $a^{\varphi(n)/2} \equiv 1 \pmod d$; at $e$, raised to
$\varphi(d)/2$, it gives the same congruence modulo $e$. The two moduli are
coprime, so the congruence holds modulo $n$.

On the same split the Gauss count is even. With $n = de$ as above, $n$ odd and
$a$ coprime to $n$,
\[
  \operatorname{flip}(n,a) \ \text{is even}
\]
($\lv$ \lean{flipCount\_even}). By \eqref{eq:gauss} and half the totient,
$a^{\varphi(n)/2} = (-1)^{\operatorname{flip}}$ in $\Z/n\Z$; by the halved
exponent the left side is $1$; so $(-1)^{\operatorname{flip}} = 1$ in $\Z/n\Z$.
Since $-1 \ne 1$ in $\Z/n\Z$ for $n \ge 3$, the count cannot be odd.

Now the law.

\begin{theorem}[The half-units law]
\label{thm:half-units}
\lv{} \lean{even\_card\_halfUnits\_resFire}
Let $x \ge 2$ and suppose $N = de$ with $d, e \ge 3$ coprime. Let $c \in \Z$ be
coprime to $N$. Then the member $c$ fires at an even number of the half units:
\[
  \#\{\, u \in U(N) : \operatorname{fire}_{c}(u) \,\}
  \;\equiv\; 0 \pmod 2 .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Reduce $c$ to its least residue $c' = c \bmod N \in [0,N)$, which is still
coprime to $N$ ($\lv$ \lean{resFire\_toNat}). On a natural residue the member is a
comparison in the upper half, $\operatorname{fire}_{c'}(u) = [N/2 < (x^{2}c'u
\bmod N)]$ ($\lv$ \lean{resFire\_nat}), which is \eqref{eq:row} read on $\N$. So
the filtered set is exactly the flip set of $a = x^{2}c'$, and the number of
fires is $\operatorname{flip}(N, x^{2}c')$.

The factor $x^{2}c'$ is coprime to $N$: the factor $x$ is ($\lv$
\lean{coprime\_x\_N}), the factor $c'$ is by hypothesis, and a product of two
numbers coprime to $N$ is coprime to $N$. Applying the evenness of the count with
the split $d, e$ gives the display.
\end{proof}

Two consequences follow, and both are stated inline because both are read off the
display. First, the parity of the number of fires of a product on a finite set of
natural numbers is the sum of the parities of its members: if every member fires
at an even number of $U(N)$, every product does ($\lv$
\lean{even\_card\_listFire}), which is the parity of a filter by an exclusive or
($\lv$ \lean{even\_card\_filter\_xor}). Second, on a split
with unit coefficients no product realises $C_{N-2}$ ($\lv$
\lean{not\_listRealises\_N\_sub\_two}). Suppose a list of coefficients coprime to
$N$ realised it. On $U(N)$ the fire set of the product equals the fire set of the
target; each member fires at an even number of $U(N)$ by the display, so the
product does; the target $[u \ne 0 \wedge u \not\equiv 1 \pmod{N-2}]$ on $U(N)$ is
$U(N)$ with $1$ removed, because $u \equiv 1 \pmod{N-2}$ with $0 < u < N$ has the
two solutions $1$ and $N-1$ and only $1$ lies in the lower half; and $\#U(N) =
\varphi(N)/2 = \varphi(d)\varphi(e)/2$ is even, since both factors of the totient
are even, so the target fires $\#U(N)-1$ times, an odd number. Even equals odd.

The hypothesis of that consequence is a split, and when $N$ has two distinct
prime factors the split exists: $d = p^{v_{p}(N)}$ and $e = N/d$ are coprime, and
each is at least $3$ because each contains an odd prime ($\lv$
\lean{exists\_split\_of\_two\_primes}). In that form the statement names a
hypothesis a reader can check ($\lv$
\lean{not\_listRealises\_N\_sub\_two\_of\_primes}). This is the form the
certificates of the next subsection do not have, and the reason they are needed.

The law is composite-only, and the reason is the transfer. On a prime $N$ the
identity \eqref{eq:gauss} is Euler's criterion: $a^{(N-1)/2} =
(-1)^{\operatorname{flip}(N,a)}$ is $-1$ exactly for a non-residue, so odd counts
occur and the count is the Legendre symbol, which is not an obstruction. On a
split the Carmichael exponent halves, the count is forced even, and every unit
coefficient obeys the law. The observation is the module's, stated in its header
rather than as a theorem, and it is why the law belongs here rather than in the
previous section: the previous section could state the pair law for every $N$,
and this one states a law that exists only because $N$ is composite.

\subsection{Certificates, and two prime instances}
\label{sec:certificates}

The half-units law and its corollary need a composite, and the interesting
instances of the section are primes; on a prime there is no split, so the laws say
nothing and single cases have to be decided by a finite check. The check is the certificate of
\S\ref{sec:twenty-nine} in general form. Let $L$ be a finite list of natural
numbers below $N$. If every coefficient $c < N$ fires at an even number of
residues of $L$ and the target $C_{M}$ fires at an odd number of them, then no
product of members, of any length and with any coefficients, realises $C_{M}$
($\lv$ \lean{not\_listRealises\_of\_natCert}). The parity of a product on $L$ is
the exclusive or of the parities of its members ($\lv$
\lean{parityN\_listFire}), so it is false; a realisation would make it the
target's, which is true.

Three reductions turn both conditions into finite arithmetic in $\N$. The member
on natural numbers is a parity ($\lv$ \lean{resFire\_natParity}), a coefficient
acts as its least residue ($\lv$ \lean{resFire\_toNat}), and the target on a
natural residue is the condition $r \ne 0 \wedge r \bmod M \ne 1$ ($\lv$
\lean{target\_natCast}). The parity of the member $c$ on $L$ is the function
$\lean{natParity}(x,c,L)$, and the parity of a Boolean on a list of naturals is
$\lean{parityN}(L,g)$, with the same three properties as $\lean{parityOn}$: it
depends only on the values on $L$ ($\lv$ \lean{parityN\_congr}), it is additive
over the exclusive or ($\lv$ \lean{parityN\_xor}), and the parity of a product is
the exclusive or of the member parities ($\lv$ \lean{parityN\_listFire}).

The converse is a check as well, and it is used once: a list of natural
coefficients whose fires exclusive-or to the target at every residue below $N$
realises $C_{M}$ ($\lv$ \lean{listRealises\_of\_nat}). So on a prime the
realisability of a rung is a finite question in two directions, and both
directions are decided by the same arithmetic.

\begin{example}[$N = 47$ and $N = 163$]
\label{ex:one-sixty-three}
At $x = 24$ the candidate is $N = 47$, and the rung $C_{5}$ \emph{is} realised:
the sixteen coefficients
\[
  1,\ 4,\ 6,\ 8,\ 10,\ 11,\ 12,\ 14,\ 15,\ 16,\ 17,\ 18,\ 19,\ 20,\ 22,\ 24
\]
have fires that exclusive-or to $T_{5}$ at every residue below $47$ ($\lv$
\lean{listRealises\_5\_47}); here $h^{-}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{47})) = 695$, which is
odd. At $x = 82$ the candidate is
$N = 163$, and the rung $C_{23}$ is \emph{not} realised: $163 \equiv 2 \pmod{23}$,
so the pair law allows it, and the certificate is the list of $54$ residues
$\lean{lam163}$, on which every coefficient below $163$ fires an even number of
times and the target $C_{23}$ an odd number ($\lv$
\lean{not\_listRealises\_23\_163}).

The two instances differ in arithmetic and not in the pair law, and neither is
covered by Theorem~\ref{thm:half-units}: both $47$ and $163$ are prime, so neither
has a unitary split, and the certificate is the only route.
\end{example}

A third instance is used once and is not an example of the book. At $x = 8$ the
candidate is $N = 15$, and the rung $C_{13}$ is not realised by any product, with
any coefficient ($\lv$ \lean{not\_listRealises\_13\_15}). It is the smallest
instance of the semiprime law of \S\ref{sec:semiprime}, and the place where the
certificate method and the class computation meet.

The measured link between the second law and the relative class number is
quoted, not proved, and each figure carries its script and its range. The script
\texttt{experiments/\allowbreak q6\_\allowbreak bernoulli.py}, whose output is
\texttt{experiments/\allowbreak results/\allowbreak q6\_\allowbreak
bernoulli.txt}, records five checks.

(A) The identity of \eqref{eq:bernoulli} was checked on $4105$ odd characters
modulo the primes below $300$, with largest error $2.3\cdot10^{-11}$.

(B) The ideal criterion against the brute-force span, for the primes below $300$
and $M \in \{3,\dots,11\}$, gave $0$ mismatches.

(C) For the primes $N \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ below $1000$, the existence of a full
$2$-divisible cyclotomic component agreed with $h^{-}\bigl(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{N})\bigr)$
being even in every case, $0$ mismatches; the column records
$v_{2}\bigl(h^{-}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{163}))\bigr) = 2$.

(D) For $N < 1500$ and $4 \le M < 60$ with $N \equiv 2 \pmod M$, the pairs on
which $C_{M}$ is not realised although the pair law allows it involve $21$
primes, and every one of them has $h^{-}$ even; the first is $N = 29$ with $M \in
\{9, 27\}$, where the same column records
$v_{2}\bigl(h^{-}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{29}))\bigr) = 3$.

(E) On the side $N \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ of the same range, the criterion that a
target is realised exactly when its polynomial vanishes on every component on
which the member span vanishes completely agreed with the brute-force answer in
$213$ cases, with $0$ mismatches.

These figures are the script's output, not theorems, and the exact criterion behind the certificates is a measured pattern
of the script and not a statement of this paper. The same holds for the
realisation scan \texttt{experiments/\allowbreak q6\_\allowbreak
product\_span.py}: to $N < 200$ it records $C_{9}$ realised at $N = 11$ and at
$N = 47$, and not realised at $N = 29$, which is the certificate's answer at the
last of the three.

\subsection{Every coefficient on a semiprime}
\label{sec:semiprime}

The two statements of \S\ref{sec:half-units} ask something of the list: the
half-units law asks the coefficients to be units, and its corollary asks for a
split. On a semiprime neither restriction is needed, and the reason is that the
coefficient classes are exhausted by four kinds and the pair law counts them.

Throughout, $x \ge 2$, $p \ne q$ are primes, $N = pq$ and $S = U(N)$; both
primes are odd. A coefficient falls into one of four classes modulo $N$: it is a
unit, or $p \mid c$ with $q \nmid c$, or $q \mid c$ with $p \nmid c$, or $N \mid
c$. The middle two read the residue modulo one factor only, the first class is
covered by Theorem~\ref{thm:half-units}, and the last fires nowhere.

Class constancy says that on the class $p \mid c$ with $q \nmid c$ the parity does
not depend on the cofactor. For $c_{1}$ with $q \nmid c_{1}$ the member
$p c_{1}$ reads $u$ modulo $q$,
\[
  \operatorname{fire}_{p c_{1}}(u)
  \;=\;
  \bigl[\, x c_{1} u \bmod q \ \text{is odd} \,\bigr]
\]
($\lv$ \lean{resFire\_class}), and the parity of its fires on $S$ depends on
$c_{1}$ in no way at all:
\[
  \#\{\, u \in S : \operatorname{fire}_{p c_{1}}(u) \,\}
  \;\equiv\;
  \#\{\, u \in S : q/2 < u \bmod q \,\} \pmod 2
\]
($\lv$ \lean{even\_card\_class\_iff}). The proof is an involution of $S$ that
flips both sides at once.

Because $p$ and $q$ are coprime there is a unit $e$ with $e \equiv -1 \pmod p$,
$e \equiv 1 \pmod q$ and $0 \le e < N$ ($\lv$ \lean{exists\_crt\_unit}); it
satisfies $e^{2} \equiv 1$ ($\lv$ \lean{crt\_unit\_sq}) and $\gcd(e,N) = 1$
($\lv$ \lean{crt\_unit\_coprime}). The fold $u \mapsto \operatorname{fold}(N,e,u)$
is an involution of $S$ ($\lv$ \lean{crt\_fold\_invol}) without fixed points
($\lv$ \lean{crt\_fold\_ne}): a fixed point would give $u \equiv eu$, so
$(e-1)u \equiv 0$ with $u$ a unit, hence $e \equiv 1 \pmod N$, which contradicts
$e \equiv -1 \pmod p$ at $p \ge 3$. Modulo $q$ the fold either fixes the residue
or sends it to $q - (u \bmod q)$ ($\lv$ \lean{crt\_fold\_mod\_q}); the two
alternatives are decided by whether a sum of two residues below $q$ is a multiple
of $q$, in which case it is exactly $q$ ($\lv$ \lean{add\_eq\_of\_dvd\_add}), and
by the complement formula $m(q-b) \equiv -mb \pmod q$ ($\lv$
\lean{mul\_sub\_mod}).

Now form the Boolean $G(u) = \operatorname{fire}_{p c_{1}}(u) \oplus [q/2 < u
\bmod q]$. It is invariant under the fold: the first factor reads $u \bmod q$
through an odd multiple, so it flips exactly when $u \bmod q$ is sent to its
complement, and the second factor flips at the same time. A fixed-point-free
involution of a finite set has an even cardinality ($\lv$
\lean{even\_card\_of\_invol}), so $\#\{u \in S : G(u)\}$ is even; the parity of a
filter by an exclusive or ($\lv$ \lean{even\_card\_filter\_xor}) turns that
evenness into the equivalence of the two parities, which is the display.

For an arbitrary coefficient the member's parity on $S$ is a linear functional of
two counts. Put $\beta_{c}(r) = [N \nmid cr]$ and
\[
  U_{q} = \#\{\, u \in S : u \bmod q > q/2 \,\}, \qquad
  U_{p} = \#\{\, u \in S : u \bmod p > p/2 \,\}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:class-parity}
  \#\{\, u \in S : \operatorname{fire}_{c}(u) \,\}
  \;\equiv\;
  \bigl(\beta_{c}(1) + \beta_{c}(q)\bigr) U_{q}
  + \bigl(\beta_{c}(1) + \beta_{c}(p)\bigr) U_{p}
  \pmod 2
\end{equation}
($\lv$ \lean{card\_member\_zmod}).

The proof is the case analysis on the class of the least residue $c'$ of $c$. If
both primes divide $c'$ then $c' = 0$ and nothing fires, while $\beta_{c}(1)$,
$\beta_{c}(p)$ and $\beta_{c}(q)$ all vanish and the right side is $0$. If $p
\mid c'$ exactly, write $c' = p c_{1}$ with $q \nmid c_{1}$; then $\beta_{c}(1) =
1$, because $N \mid p c_{1}$ would give $q \mid c_{1}$; $\beta_{c}(q) = 0$,
because $c'q = Nc_{1}$; and $\beta_{c}(p) = 1$, because $N \mid p^{2}c_{1}$ would
again give $q \mid c_{1}$. The right side is $U_{q}$, and the left side is $U_{q}$
by class constancy. The class $q \mid c'$ exactly is symmetric, with $U_{p}$ in
place of $U_{q}$. If $c'$ is a unit then all three invariants are $1$, the right
side is $2U_{q} + 2U_{p} = 0$ in $\Z/2\Z$, and the left side is $0$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:half-units}.

The class formula is used only through sums, and the sums live in $\Z/2\Z$,
where $1+1 = 0$. Three identities make the passage. The parity of a filter by an
exclusive or is the sum of the two parities in $\Z/2\Z$ ($\lv$
\lean{card\_filter\_xor\_zmod}); an exclusive-or fold read in $\Z/2\Z$ is the sum
of its values ($\lv$ \lean{foldr\_xor\_zmod}); and two natural numbers with the
same parity are equal in $\Z/2\Z$ ($\lv$ \lean{natCast\_zmod\_two\_eq}). From the
first two, the parity of a product of members on a finite set is the sum of the
parities of its members ($\lv$ \lean{card\_listFire\_zmod}); and the resulting sum
is linear, so the coefficient of $U_{q}$ is the sum of the $\beta(1)$ plus the sum
of the $\beta(q)$ over the list, and likewise for $U_{p}$ ($\lv$
\lean{sum\_map\_lin}).

Now the semiprime law.

\begin{theorem}[No product realises $C_{N-2}$ on a semiprime]
\label{thm:semiprime-nsubtwo}
\lv{} \lean{not\_listRealises\_N\_sub\_two\_semiprime}
Let $x \ge 2$, let $p \ne q$ be primes, put $N = pq$, and let $cs$ be any finite
list of coefficients. Then $cs$ does not realise $C_{N-2}$ at $x$:
\[
  \lnot\, \lean{ListRealises}\ (N-2)\ x\ cs .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Assume a list $cs$ realises the target.

The pair law at $r$, summed over the list, gives for every $0 < r < N$
\[
  \sum_{c \in cs} \bigl[\, N \nmid cr \,\bigr] \;=\; 0
  \qquad \text{in } \Z/2\Z .
\]
Indeed \lean{listFire\_pair} of \S\ref{sec:face-products} writes the exclusive or
of the product's fires at $r$ and at $N-r$ as the exclusive or over the list of
$[N \nmid cr]$; a realisation makes the two fires equal to $T_{N-2}(r)$ and
$T_{N-2}(N-r)$, which are equal because $N \equiv 2 \pmod{N-2}$ with $N-2 \ge 3$
($\lv$ \lean{target\_pair\_of\_two\_mod}); an exclusive or of equal Booleans is
false, and $\lv$ \lean{foldr\_xor\_zmod} reads the fold as a sum.

Read the relation at $r = 1$, $r = q$ and $r = p$. Since $N = pq$, a coefficient
 is a unit exactly when $p \nmid c$ and $q \nmid c$; the condition $N \nmid c$ holds
 for the units and for the $p$-only and $q$-only coefficients alike, $N \mid cq$ is
 $p \mid c$, and $N \mid cp$ is $q \mid c$. So with $n_{u}$, $n_{p}$ and $n_{q}$
 the numbers of unit, $p$-only and $q$-only coefficients in the list, the three
 relations read
 \[
   n_{u} + n_{p} + n_{q} = 0, \qquad n_{u} + n_{q} = 0, \qquad n_{u} + n_{p} = 0
   \qquad \text{in } \Z/2\Z ,
 \]
 and hence $n_{u} = n_{p} = n_{q} = 0$: a realising list has an even number of
 coefficients in each class.

The product's parity on $S$ is the sum of the member parities ($\lv$
\lean{card\_listFire\_zmod}); substituting \eqref{eq:class-parity} for each member
and summing gives, by $\lv$ \lean{sum\_map\_lin},
\[
  \#\{\, u \in S : \operatorname{Fire}_{cs}(u) \,\}
  \;\equiv\;
  \Bigl(\sum_{c} \beta_{c}(1) + \sum_{c} \beta_{c}(q)\Bigr) U_{q}
  + \Bigl(\sum_{c} \beta_{c}(1) + \sum_{c} \beta_{c}(p)\Bigr) U_{p} .
\]
The sum of the $\beta_{c}(1)$ is $n_{u} + n_{p} + n_{q}$, the sum of the $\beta_{c}(q)$ is $n_{u}
+ n_{q}$ and the sum of the $\beta_{c}(p)$ is $n_{u} + n_{p}$, so all three
vanish and the product fires at an even number of half units.

The target fires at an odd number. On $S$ the target $[u \ne 0 \wedge u
\not\equiv 1 \pmod{N-2}]$ is $S$ without $1$, because $u \equiv 1 \pmod{N-2}$ with
$0 < u < N$ has the two solutions $1$ and $N-1$ and only $1$ lies in the lower
half. The cardinality $\#S = \varphi(N)/2 = \varphi(p)\varphi(q)/2$ is even by
half the totient and the multiplicativity of $\varphi$ on the coprime factors, so
the target fires $\#S - 1$ times, which is odd in $\Z/2\Z$.

The product and the target have the same fire set on $S$ by the realisation, so
an even number equals an odd number: $1 = 0$ in $\Z/2\Z$, which is absurd.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:semiprime-nsubtwo} is the strongest of the three laws, and it is
the only place in the book where the pair law is used as a counting device rather
than stated as a symmetry. In \S\ref{sec:face-products} the law rules products out
by a mismatch of the pair patterns; here it counts how many coefficients of each
class a realising list would have to carry, and the count is zero of each. What it
rests on is the hypothesis: the argument uses the two factors of $N$, and it says
nothing about $C_{M}$ for $M \ne N-2$ or about a composite with more than two
prime factors.

\medskip

What the section has shown, in one sentence, is that the member of the family of
2009 with coefficient $c$ is row $x^{2}c$ of the Demjanenko matrix read modulo
$2$, and that the character sum of the member $1$ is
$2\chi_{4}(N)f(2)\bigl(1-2f(2)\bigr)B_{1,f}$; and that from those two readings
Gauss's transfer, the pair law and finite certificates come three laws about what
 products of members can settle:
the pair law is not the only law, the half-units law holds on a split for unit
coefficients, and on a semiprime every coefficient obeys the law for $C_{N-2}$.

The matrix, the
$2$-divisibility of the relative class number, the Bernoulli numbers and the
half-sum method are all in the literature and are cited where they are first
used; the claim is the bridge, and the bridge is elementary, since \eqref{eq:row}
is three lines of arithmetic on $2x = N+1$. No figure of
\S\ref{sec:certificates} is a theorem, and the exact criterion behind the
certificates is a measured pattern of the script rather than a statement of this
paper. Nothing here bears on the cost of the criterion or on the problem of the
two sections that follow. The face of 2009 is, by these identities, classical
mathematics.

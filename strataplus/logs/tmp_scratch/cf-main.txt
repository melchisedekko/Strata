                                                1. Introduction
   Fermat’s little theorem gives the oldest test of primality: if N is prime, then aN −1 ≡ 1
(mod N ) for every a coprime to N . In 2009 the author wrote down, in a pocket diary, an
expression in a root of unity meant to return a sign ±1 at each base and so to tell the
primes from the composites, together with an elementary criterion for primality that is
studied in [10]. This article is about the expression: a root of unity doing the work of
a congruence. For an odd candidate N = 2x − 1 ≥ 3, a base m with 1 ≤ m ≤ N , and
ζ = exp(iπ/N ), the expression is
                      (︂            )︂N/2                    (︂                                )︂
C ∗ (x, m) = (−1)x (−ζ)A(x,m)               ,      A(x, m) = 1 + (−1)x (m + N − 1)N −1 mod 2N,
with principal branches throughout. Its base −ζ = exp(2πix/N ) is a primitive N -th root
of unity, and N is odd, so no power of the base is −1: the sign that the expression is asked
to produce is manufactured by the outer exponent N/2, and by nothing else (Section 2).
What that sign is worth is a question of arithmetic, and this article answers it with classical
objects: a face valued in the M -th roots of unity, the rows of Demjanenko’s matrix [8],
and a character sum that is a generalised Bernoulli number.
    The arithmetic is that of the criterion of [10], recalled once. For a base m let r(x, m) =
(m − 1)N −1 mod N be the Fermat residue. The criterion computes an intermediate integer
E(x, m) from that residue (Section 2), reports f (x, m) = E(x, m) mod 4, which is 0 or 2,
and asserts that N is prime exactly when f (x, m) = 0 at every base; a base is a witness,
f (x, m) = 2, exactly when its Fermat residue is nonzero and congruent to 3 or to N − 1
modulo 4. The first finding of Section 2 is that the complex number is that value shifted
by one,
                    C ∗ (x, m) = f (x, m) − 1       (x ≥ 2, 1 ≤ m ≤ N ),
so the expression is an integer sign and a function of the Fermat residue and of nothing
else, and its prime direction is Fermat’s little theorem.

                        2. The base, the fold, and the sign
    The expression that Section 1 records is a filter built from a root of unity, so the first
step in reading it is cyclotomic rather than arithmetic. Fix the candidate N = 2x − 1
with x ≥ 2, so that N ≥ 3 is odd, and take the base ζ = (−1)1/N on the principal branch.
Everything below is computed in the group generated by ζ and in the integers modulo
2N = 4x − 2. Two questions are asked: where the ±1 of the expression comes from, and
what the complex number is a function of. The answers are that the base never takes the
value −1 — the outer fold takes care of that — and that the expression is a function of
the Fermat residue the arithmetic criterion reads, and of nothing else.

2.6. The prime direction. At a prime candidate the criterion returns f (x, m) = 0 at
every base, which is its prime direction in [10]. By Theorem 2.4 the expression is then
−1 at every base [L] (monsterFull_const_of_prime), and in particular it takes the same
value at any two bases [L] (monsterFull_prime_const) — the constancy that the source
reads off as primality.
    More is true: the expression cannot see anything but the Fermat residue. If two bases
have the same residue modulo N , their inner terms are congruent modulo N , and the
criterion absorbs the difference, the criterion’s value depending on the inner term only
modulo N — the absorption lemma E_eq_of_term_eq of [10]. The two values of f (x, m)
therefore agree, and so do the two values of C ∗ [L] (monsterFull_depends_on_fermat).
This is the precise sense in which the expression is a Fermat test, and it holds at every
candidate, prime or composite.
    At a prime candidate the residue is 1 at every base of the range: for 2 ≤ m ≤ N the
number m − 1 lies in [1, N − 1], so it is a unit modulo the prime N , and its (N − 1)-st
power is 1 by Fermat’s little theorem. Two bases with the same residue give the same
value, so
               C ∗ (x, m) = C ∗ (x, N )  (monsterFull_prime_eq_fermat)
for 2 ≤ m ≤ N [L] . Unwound, the prime direction of the source reduces to Fermat’s little
theorem.

2.7. The source’s sign. One ingredient of the source pages is a sign, and this subsection
proves what dropping it does. The pages write the inner exponent as 1 ± (m + N − 1)N −1 ,
the sign alternating with x as the factor (−1)x of A; dropping that sign — fixing it to
+1 — gives the variant with A+ = (1 + t) mod 2N , with s+ = 2x A+ mod 2N , and with
σ + defined from s+ exactly as σ is defined from s (monsterExpPlus, monsterArgPlus,
monsterSignPlus).
    When x is even the sign being dropped is already +1, and the two variants are the
same function: the exponents agree [L] (monsterExpPlus_eq_of_even) and therefore
so do the values [L] (monsterSignPlus_eq_of_even). When x is odd the sign is −1,
and dropping it is an involution of the reduced argument. The two arguments sum to
2 modulo 2N : each is congruent to 2x(1 ± t), and their sum is 4x ≡ 2 (mod 4x − 2)
[L]
    (monsterArgPlus_add_monsterArg). Since s+ is the remainder in [0, 2N ), it is (2 −
s) mod 2N [L] (monsterArgPlus_eq_sub).
    On the region s ∈ {0, 2} ∪ [N + 2, 2N ) the involution negates the value. For s = 0 the
image is 2, and for s = 2 it is 0; in both cases the branch factor is +1 on both sides, while
the parity factor changes, since exactly one of 0 and 2 is divisible by 4. For s ≥ N + 2
the image is 2N + 2 − s, which is positive and at most N : the branch factor changes
from −1 to +1, while the first factor is unchanged because 2N + 2 − s ≡ s (mod 4) —
indeed 2N + 2 = 4x ≡ 0 (mod 4), and s is even. Hence σ + = −σ on that region [L]
(monsterSignPlus_eq_neg_of_arg). Since a global negation cannot change whether a
function is constant, on any set of bases whose arguments stay in the region the variant
has the same constant/non-constant verdict as the original.
    The case the negation law leaves open is the middle band 4 ≤ s ≤ N + 1, and the

is a C2J+1 -witness exactly when it is a C2J -witness or has a nonzero residue and fires at
rung J + 1. In the first case the induction hypothesis replaces C2J by C2 or a fire at a
rung j ≤ J; in the second there is a fire at rung J + 1 with a nonzero residue. Together
these are C2 or a fire at a rung j with 2 ≤ j ≤ J + 1, and each of those cases conversely
gives C2J+1 , through the induction hypothesis or through the stacking law. This closes the
step and the induction.                                                                   □
22                                 ALESSANDRO SGARBI

    The ladder collapses in the same way at the level of congruences. If the residue is
nonzero, then it is 1 modulo 2J exactly when it is odd and no rung j with 2 ≤ j ≤ J fires
at m [L] (resid_one_mod_two_pow_iff_towerRefs). For the proof, negate the ladder
display: with a nonzero residue the base fails to be a C2J -witness exactly when it is not a
C2 -witness and no rung in the range fires, and not being a C2 -witness is being 1 modulo 2,
that is oddness.
    So the ladder C2J is the disjunction of the bottom filter C2 with J −1 single comparisons,
one per rung, and r ≡ 1 (mod 2J ) is the statement that the bottom bit is 1 and no rung
above it fires. Stacking rungs adds one bit at a time, and no rung reads more than one:
the family carries one bit per member however many rungs are taken.

5.8. Products of members. Two members can be multiplied, and the product is a new
function of the residue rather than another member of the family. For coefficients c1 , c2
and a base m the product sign is σc1 (m) σc2 (m) (productSign), and the product fires at
m when that sign differs from its value at m = 1 (ProductFires). The pair obstruction
of Section 5.6 says nothing about it: it obstructs one coefficient, not a product of two.
     Two proved instances measure the difference. At N = 35 the product of the members
of coefficients 2 and ⌊35/3⌋ = 11 fires exactly at the C3 -witnesses [L] (product_realises_
witnessMod_three_two_mod_thirtyfive), and at N = 65 the product of the members of
coefficients 2 and ⌊65/3⌋ = 21 does the same [L] (product_realises_witnessMod_three_
two_mod_sixtyfive). Each statement is a conjunction of N decidable comparisons of
residues, one for each base k in [1, N ], and each is verified base by base. So a product of
two members can realise a filter with M = 3, which no single coefficient realises at every
N , and the reach of products is a question about products. Its laws are the subject of
Section 7.
     Each member of the family is a parity dilate of the residue, hence a row of Demjanenko’s
matrix modulo 2; each carries one bit; the two filters of at most one bit are realised, by
c = 0 and c = −2; nothing with M ≥ 3 is realised by a single coefficient on a tail; and the
2-adic ladder stacks the bits one rung at a time. The family is indexed, and the object it
computes can now be summed. What the sum of a member against a Dirichlet character
is, is the subject of Section 6.

    Section 6 evaluated one member: summed against an odd character, the member of
coefficient 1 returns a generalised Bernoulli number, and read against a row of Demjanenko’s

computes. That does not answer what a product of members can determine, and this is
the question the second half of the article asks. At each residue a member reports one bit

{0, 1}-vectors indexed by the residues, the fire functions of products are the F2 -span of

whether the target vector of CM , the indicator of its witness condition (targetM , displayed

CM on every residue when it agrees with the target at every residue of [0, N ): this is the
uniform form, a statement about the span and the target alone, and it is the form of most
results below. A product realises CM on the attainable residues when it agrees with the
target at the residues that bases actually attain (Section 7.7). The second form is the one

are 0 and 1, where no target of modulus M ≥ 2 fires, so the empty product realises every
such CM there, and the question has content only at composite N .

every residue of N carries. Two pairings are involved, and they are not the same pairing.

idempotents, and on it the product of the two faces is one fixed value. On a member — a
{±1}-valued function of the residue — the natural pair is r + r′ = N , and on it the two
fires are complementary up to a single bit. From the second pairing three consequences
follow: for x ≥ 3 no product of members realises CM for M ≥ 4 unless N ≡ 2 (mod M );
for C3 the law is a complete dichotomy, realised by two members when N ≡ 2 (mod 3)
and by three when N ≡ 0; and on the residues a base actually attains, four bases at
N = 85 are enough to settle the question.

                               9. What the face cannot be
    Section 8 fenced the products of members at two invariants and, where both were silent,
at a finite certificate. Every one of those statements is about combinations of members.
This section asks instead what kind of object the face itself is, and it proves four negative
results: no additive map realises a filter CM even where G(N ) says that CM has a witness;
the Fermat map is not diagonalised by the additive characters; the ladder of fires does
not determine the residue, though it does once one parity bit is added; and the branch of
Section 2 is removable pointwise but cannot be an additive homomorphism. The section
closes with the questions none of the four settles.
    Throughout, x ≥ 2, N = 2x − 1, and r = r(x, m) is the Fermat residue of the base
m; CM is the rung-M filter and a CM -witness the base of Section 3; a member, its sign
σc and its firing are those of Section 5; and G(N ) is the largest trivialising modulus of
Theorem 3.3, whose divisors are exactly the moduli whose face is trivial.
9.1. No additive face. The first question is whether the face can be carried by a
homomorphism. An additive map f : Z/N → Z/M can be read as a filter: it fires at the

N, M ≥ 1 with gcd(N, M ) = 1, every additive f : Z/N → Z/M is zero [L] (no_add_

9.4. The branch. The fourth question concerns the branch itself. In Section 2 the sign
of the expression is computed by cases on whether the doubled argument lies below or
above N . The case split cannot come from a homomorphism: for odd N and every j, each
additive f : Z/N → Z/2j is zero [L] (no_add_hom_odd). That is the coprime vanishing
above, with gcd(N, 2j ) = 1; the branch compares a canonical representative with N , and
such a comparison is not an operation of the additive group. So no homomorphism can
supply the case split.
    It is nevertheless removable pointwise. For a finite set T ⊂ C and any function
g : C → R there is a continuous F : C → R agreeing with g on T [L] (continuous_
interpolation). The extension is Lagrange’s formula written in norms,

the value as a function of the base point exists at every N , and its construction uses the
finite set of base points at that N : the function F depends on x, and no uniformity in N
is asserted. A homomorphic reading is impossible, the only additive map into Z/2j being
zero.

9.5. What is open. Three questions remain open, and a fourth is answered elsewhere.
    First, the global face: a closed form for G(N ) read on the circle, through the face of
Section 3, rather than as a gcd. Theorem 3.3 says that the moduli whose face is trivial are
exactly the divisors of G(N ), which is an interface and not a formula. With it stands the
products question on the class N ≡ 2 (mod M ) for odd M ≥ 5: the pair law of Section 7
is silent there by construction, the half-units law of Section 8 fences only M = N − 2,
and M = 3 is the one modulus at which a product’s reach is settled at every N , by the
dichotomy of Theorem 7.3.
    Second, the schedule remnant of the products question. Theorem 5.4 closes the
single-coefficient
            √      question at every M ≥ 3 on a tail of N ; at the schedule modulus
M (N ) = ⌊ N ⌋ + 1 of Section 3, whether any product of members realises CM (N ) on a tail
is open, and in the class where the pair law is silent there, that is where M (N ) | N − 2,
no invariant of this article settles it.
    Third, the 2-adic ladder as a morphism. The lift lemma says that the fires on the rungs
together with the parity of the residue determine the residue modulo 2J ; whether the map
carrying a residue to that data is a homomorphism for multiplication modulo N is open.
    The fourth asks, for squarefree N , whether the idempotents of Z/N — the Chinese-
remainder projectors that split a squarefree candidate into its prime-power parts —
determine the divisors of G(N ), and with them the moduli M for which N is a CM -
pseudoprime. It is answered in [11]: on the squarefree Korselt numbers (squarefree N
with p − 1 | N − 1 at every prime p | N ) they do, and on every squarefree composite N
with at least two prime factors the invariant is the gcd of the numerator cosets when the
numerators carry nothing and each tops its coset, and 1 otherwise.
    Together, the four results say what the face is not. It is not a linear object, even
where G says that it has a witness; the Fermat map it reads is not diagonalised by its own
additive characters; its ladder of rungs does not encode the residue, though the ladder with
one parity bit does; and its branch can be smoothed by a continuous function of the base
point but cannot be a homomorphism. The object itself was identified in Section 6, and
the fences around what its products can determine were drawn in Section 7 and Section 8.

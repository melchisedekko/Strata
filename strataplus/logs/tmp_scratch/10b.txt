
\section{How Large the Exceptional Set Is}
\label{sec:counts}

The previous section produced one exceptional semiprime, and one is enough to
answer whether the absence of a witness survives off the Korselt locus; it is not
enough to answer how much of it there is. What Part~V settled is \emph{which}
moduli have no witness: exactly the divisors of $G(N)$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:reduction} of \S\ref{sec:invariant}),
and on a composite the invariant is positive ($\lv$ \lean{residueGcd\_pos}) and
divides $N(N-1)$ ($\lv$ \lean{residueGcd\_dvd\_mul\_sub\_one}), both in
\S\ref{sec:invariant}. Write $G(N) = \GE{N-1}{x}$ for its value at the canonical
exponent $e = N-1$ (Definition~\ref{def:exponent-invariant},
\S\ref{sec:exponent}). None of that bounds the \emph{size} of $G(N)$: Parts~II--V fix the coefficient,
the exponent, the shift, the window and the modulus, and none of them constrains
the invariant. So $G(N) > 1$ is a condition that no parameter predicts, and this
section counts the numbers at which it holds.

Two sets carry the count. For $X \ge 1$ let
\[
  \mathcal{B}(X) \;=\; \bigl\{(p,q) \;:\; p < q \text{ odd primes},\;
  pq \le X,\; G(pq) > 1 \bigr\}
  \qquad (\lean{blindSemiprimes}) ,
\]
the exceptional semiprimes below $X$ --- the semiprimes at which some $C_M$ has no
witness; and let $\mathcal{M}(X)$ be the same set with
$G(pq) > 1$ replaced by $\lean{AllZero}\bigl((pq+1)/2\bigr)$
(\lean{missedSemiprimes}), the semiprimes on which the criterion of 2009 vanishes
at every base (\S\ref{sec:definitions}). One predicate separates the two, and the
section keeps them apart, because they answer different questions:
$\mathcal{B}$ is about the absence of a witness at a modulus, $\mathcal{M}$ about the
vanishing of a formula. Every figure below is either a cardinality of one of these
two sets at a stated $X$, or a measured density quoted with its range. None of
them counts operations: the cost of the criterion is
\S\ref{sec:schedule}'s and \S\ref{sec:least-safe}'s, and no sentence of this
section bears on it.

One member is proved, and it is \S\ref{sec:off-carmichael}'s.
$N = 300\,979\,369 = 7759 \cdot 38791$ has $G(N) = 2$ and is not a Carmichael
number ($\lv$ \lean{blind\_modulus\_off\_carmichael}, $\lv$
\lean{residueGcd\_counterexample\_eq\_two}, \S\ref{sec:off-carmichael}). So
$\mathcal{B}(X)$ is nonempty from $X = 300\,979\,369$ on, which is all the
previous section was asked to supply. The number returns once, in
\S\ref{sec:counts-mass}, as one member of a measured census; it is not this
section's headline, and no figure of this section is built on it.

What the section proves is two bounds, and they are not the same kind of
statement. The upper one is a counting theorem about one local image at a time,
and it becomes $X^{1/2+2\gamma}$ for every $\gamma > 0$ once the published
theorem on exponential sums over subgroups of $\mathbb{F}_q^{\times}$ is fed into
it. The lower one is a counting theorem about a single family, and it rests on two
hypotheses that are open: Bateman--Horn for the pair $\bigl(n, 1+j(n-1)\bigr)$,
and one fixed-coset density. Each bound carries its hypothesis in the statement,
the section says at each display which hypothesis that is, and the two together
pin the exponent at $1/2$ --- which is the answer to the question of quantity.
That answer is conditional twice over, and saying so is part of the answer.

\subsection{One image, and the count it bounds}
\label{sec:counts-index}

The route is an index, and the index is \S\ref{sec:families}'s. On $N = pc$ with
$p$ prime, $1 < c$ and $\gcd(p,c) = 1$, the invariant is bounded by the index of
 the local image: $G(N) \le \gcd(N-1,\, p-1)$ ($\lv$
\lean{residueGcd\_le\_gcd\_sub\_one}, \S\ref{sec:families-pin}), because the
integers of one coset that are congruent to $p$ modulo a divisor $M$ of $G(N)$
number at most $(p-1)/M$, so $M \cdot |S_p| \le p-1$ ($\lv$
\lean{mul\_card\_powRange\_le}). Two consequences are drawn from it in this
subsection. Every divisor $M$ of $G(N)$ lies below every prime factor $p$ to which the bound applies,
$M < p$ ($\lv$ \lean{lt\_prime\_of\_dvd\_residueGcd}); and on a semiprime the
bound is the one between the two predecessors, $G(pq) \le \gcd(p-1, q-1)$
($\lv$ \lean{residueGcd\_semiprime\_le\_gcd}), since
$\gcd(pq-1, q-1) = \gcd(p-1, q-1)$ ($\lv$ \lean{gcd\_sub\_one\_semiprime},
\S\ref{sec:wrap}). \S\ref{sec:families} proved both as the boundary of its own
method --- a bound on the size of the invariant is not a bound on the number of
numbers that attain it --- and left the counting to this Part.

The hypothesis the count needs is about how a coset of a local image sits in
$(0,q)$. For a function $L : \N \to \N$, say that $L$ \emph{meets the lower half}
if, for every prime $q$, every $u$ coprime to $q$ and every exponent $e$: whenever
the image $S_q = \{\, b^{e} \bmod q : b \text{ prime to } q \,\}$
(\lean{powImage}) has order $|S_q| \ge L(q)$, every coset $u S_q$ has an integer
representative in $[1, q/2)$. That is the proposition \lean{CosetMeetsLowerHalf},
and it is not empty: if $|S_q|$ is even, then $-1 \in S_q$ ($\lv$
\lean{exists\_pow\_eq\_neg\_one\_mod\_prime}, \S\ref{sec:local}), so a coset
contains both $x$ and $q-x$ and one of the two lies below $q/2$ ($\lv$
\lean{coset\_meets\_lower\_half\_of\_even}). Failing orders are therefore odd;
a sweep of the primes below $2 \cdot 10^{4}$ finds the largest failing order
$s = 15$ at $q = 17761$, with $\log s / \log q$ falling from $0.43$ to $0.28$
across the range (\texttt{experiments/\allowbreak coset\_lower\_half\_test.py}).
That is a measurement about small primes and it is not used as anything else.

The engine is one image, and it is worth stating before the count, because the
count is its contrapositive. Suppose $L$ meets the lower half, and let $N = pc$
with $p$ prime, $1 < c$, $\gcd(p,c) = 1$, and the image at $p$ of order at least
$L(p)$. Then $G(N) = 1$ ($\lvCond$ \lean{residueGcd\_eq\_one\_of\_large\_image}).
The proof reads the side coset as integers. If a prime $\ell$ divided $G(N)$,
then $\ell \le G(N) \le \gcd(N-1,p-1) \le p-1$, and every element $X$ of the
coset $u S_p$ satisfies $X \equiv p \pmod{\ell}$ ($\lv$
\lean{dvd\_prime\_sub\_side}, \S\ref{sec:families-pin}); as an integer in
$(0,p)$ it is $X = p - \ell k$ with $0 < k < p/\ell$, and reading $k$ modulo $p$
gives $k \equiv -X/\ell \pmod p$. So the whole coset $-(\ell c)^{-1} S_p$ would
lie in $(0, p/2)$. The hypothesis, applied to the opposite coset, puts an element
of $(\ell c)^{-1} S_p$ below $p/2$, that is an element of
$-(\ell c)^{-1} S_p$ above it --- and an integer of $(0,p)$ above $p/2$ is not in
$(0, p/2)$. Read contrapositively: if $G(N) > 1$, then at every prime
$p \parallel N$ the local image is small,
\[
  \frac{p-1}{\gcd(N-1,\, p-1)} \;<\; L(p)
  \qquad (\lvCond\ \lean{local\_image\_lt\_of\_one\_lt\_residueGcd}) .
\]
That inequality is the sharpest form the Part's sentence takes. An exceptional $N$ has
no large local image anywhere, so it is close to Korselt at each of its primes;
what the five parameters cannot supply is missing at every single local image at
once.

\begin{theorem}[The count, at the index of one local image]
\label{thm:counts-upper}
\lvCond{} \lean{card\_blind\_semiprimes\_le}
Let $L : \N \to \N$ meet the lower half, in the sense that for every prime $q$,
every $u$ coprime to $q$ and every $e$ with $|S_q| \ge L(q)$, the coset $u S_q$
has an integer representative in $[1, q/2)$. Let $X \ge 1$ and $K \ge 1$ be such
that $L(q) \le K$ for every $q \le X$. Then
\[
  \#\,\mathcal{B}(X) \;\le\; \bigl(\lfloor \sqrt X \rfloor + 1\bigr)\, K^{2} .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{The three numbers of an exceptional pair.} Let $(p,q) \in \mathcal{B}(X)$, and put
$x = (pq+1)/2$, so that $N(x) = pq$ and $x \ge 2$, the two factors being odd
primes with $p \ge 3$. Write
\[
  g = \gcd(p-1,\, q-1) , \qquad a = \frac{p-1}{g} , \qquad
  b = \frac{q-1}{g} .
\]
The exponent at $x$ is $N(x) - 1 = pq - 1$, and
$\gcd(pq-1, q-1) = \gcd(p-1,q-1) = g$ ($\lv$ \lean{gcd\_sub\_one\_semiprime}).

\emph{The image at the larger prime is small.} By the engine stated above,
$G(pq) > 1$ forces
$(q-1)/\gcd(pq-1, q-1) < L(q)$, that is $b < L(q)$. Since $q \le pq \le X$, the
bound on the level gives $L(q) \le K$, so $1 \le b < K$.

\emph{The other two are small as well.} The quotient $a$ satisfies $a \le b$,
because $p < q$. And $g \le p-1 < p$, while $p^{2} < pq \le X$ gives
$p \le \lfloor \sqrt X \rfloor$; hence $g \le \lfloor \sqrt X \rfloor$.

\emph{Recovery.} The pair is determined by $(g,a,b)$: from $p - 1 = ga$ and
$q - 1 = gb$ both primes are read off. So $(p,q) \mapsto (g,a,b)$ is an
injection of $\mathcal{B}(X)$ into the product of the three ranges.

\emph{Counting the target.} There are $\lfloor \sqrt X \rfloor + 1$ values of
$g$ and $K$ values of each of $a$ and $b$, so at most
$(\lfloor \sqrt X \rfloor + 1) K^{2}$ triples. The bound follows.
\end{proof}

The statement is worth restating in the form in which it will be used, because
that form is its whole content: the bound is a statement about the \emph{index},
not about the absence of a witness. It says that an exceptional semiprime is
determined by the common
divisor of its two predecessors and by the two quotients, and that all three are
small. What makes it a count of the exceptional set is the hypothesis, which
converts
$G > 1$ into the smallness of one quotient; what keeps it honest is that the
hypothesis is written into the statement and is not proved here.

\subsection{The published theorem, read twice}
\label{sec:counts-bgk}

The hypothesis of the count is a consequence of a theorem that is not in
Mathlib, and the transcription matters. \lean{BGKBound} states the
Bourgain--Glibichuk--Konyagin theorem as published, in the form of Theorem~1.1 of
\cite{Kowalski2024SmallSubgroups}: for every $\gamma > 0$ there are $\nu > 0$ and
$C$, depending on $\gamma$ alone, such that
\[
  \Bigl| \sum_{h \in H} e(ah/q) \Bigr| \;\le\; C\,|H|\, q^{-\nu}
\]
for every prime $q$, every subgroup $H \subseteq \mathbb{F}_q^{\times}$ of order
at least $q^{\gamma}$, and every $a \not\equiv 0$. The transcription is the one
sentence that makes it usable: every subgroup of the cyclic group
$\mathbb{F}_q^{\times}$ is the image of a power map $b \mapsto b^{e}$, of order
$(q-1)/\gcd(e, q-1)$, and its elements are the integers of
$\lean{powImage}\, q\, e$, a set that is nonempty because it contains $1$ ($\lv$
\lean{one\_le\_card\_powImage}). The
statement is a proposition in the development, never an axiom, and the bound is
kept on the real and imaginary parts, which is the form the trigonometric
argument below consumes (\lean{ExpSumSmall}).

One variant is recorded beside it. \lean{ShkredovBound} is the same inequality
with Shkredov's exponent made explicit, $\nu = \delta/(27 + 2/\delta)$, and
Shkredov's form implies the one used here ($\lvCond$
\lean{bgkBound\_of\_shkredovBound}). The section states the theorem once, in the
ineffective form, and notes that a reader who wants numbers has the exponent; the
threshold below is not computed either way, because the constant is not.

\emph{The threshold.} Given $\nu$ and $C$, choose $Q$ with $(3C)^{1/\nu} < q$ for
every $q \ge Q$. Then $C q^{-\nu} < 1/3$, so every nontrivial exponential sum
over an image of order at least $q^{\gamma}$ is below one third of the image's
size ($\lvCond$ \lean{expSumSmall\_of\_bgkBound}). This is the hypothesis
\lean{ExpSumSmall}, and it is named as a hypothesis at every use.

\emph{From sums to cosets, without Erd\H{o}s--Tur\'an.} The polynomial
\[
  P(t) \;=\; 1 + 2 \sin 2\pi t - \cos 4\pi t
\]
has mean $1$ and is $\le 0$ on $[1/2,1)$, because
$1 + 2\sin\theta - \cos 2\theta = 2 \sin\theta\,(\sin\theta + 1) \le 0$ for
$\theta \in [\pi, 2\pi)$ ($\lv$ \lean{trig\_upper\_half}). Suppose a whole coset
$u S_q$ lay in $(q/2, q)$. Summing $P$ over it gives
$|S_q| + 2\,\mathrm{Im}\,S(u) - \mathrm{Re}\,S(2u) \le 0$, which is impossible
when both sums are below $|S_q|/3$. Hence every coset of a large image meets
$[1, q/2)$ ($\lvCond$ \lean{cosetMeetsLowerHalf\_of\_expSumSmall}), which is the
hypothesis of the count, discharged from the published theorem.

\emph{The idempotent, and the coset below it.} The two-image form of the engine
needs one idempotent coefficient in the upper half. For $N = pq$ with
$qu \equiv 1 \pmod p$ and $pv \equiv 1 \pmod q$, one has $p \le 2u$ or
$q \le 2v$ ($\lv$ \lean{idem\_coeff\_upper\_half}): otherwise $0 < qu + pv < pq$
would be $1$ modulo $pq$. On the side given by that inequality, a coset element
below the coefficient produces a side residue below the idempotent, and the
side-residue criterion of \S\ref{sec:korselt} gives $G = 1$ ($\lv$
\lean{residueGcd\_eq\_one\_of\_coset\_below}). The two-image statement is then:
if both local images of a semiprime have order at least $L$, then $G = 1$
($\lvCond$ \lean{residueGcd\_eq\_one\_of\_large\_images}), and with the
exponential-sum hypothesis in place of the coset hypothesis,
$\lvCond$ \lean{residueGcd\_eq\_one\_of\_large\_images\_expSum}. It is the
statement that an exceptional $N$ is close to Korselt at \emph{both} primes; the count
needs the one-image form, applied at the larger prime alone, which is the
statement of this subsection.

\begin{theorem}[A large local image forces $G = 1$]
\label{thm:large-image-BGK}
\lvCond{} \lean{residueGcd\_eq\_one\_of\_large\_image\_BGK}
Assume $\lean{BGKBound}$. Then for every $\gamma > 0$ there is $Q$ such that for
every $x \ge 2$ and all $p, c$ with $p$ prime, $1 < c$, $N(x) = pc$ and
$\gcd(p,c) = 1$: if $Q \le p$ and
\[
  p^{\gamma} \;\le\; \frac{p-1}{\gcd\bigl(N(x)-1,\; p-1\bigr)} ,
\]
then $G(N(x)) = 1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{The level.} Put
\[
  \lean{bgkLevel}\,\gamma\, Q\, q \;=\;
  \begin{cases} \lceil q^{\gamma} \rceil & Q \le q ,\\ q & q < Q , \end{cases}
\]
a level that is the threshold above $Q$ and vacuous below it, since no image has
order at least $q$. At that level the exponential-sum bound holds
($\lvCond$ \lean{bgkLevel\_hyp}): above the threshold it is
$\lean{ExpSumSmall}$ from \lean{expSumSmall\_of\_bgkBound}, and below it no image
qualifies, the hypothesis being empty.

\emph{The coset hypothesis.} Apply the derivation of the previous paragraph at
the level \lean{bgkLevel}, with the value $L(2) = 2$ at $q = 2$, which the
threshold supplies by $3 \le Q$. Then \lean{CosetMeetsLowerHalf} holds at that
level ($\lvCond$ \lean{cosetMeetsLowerHalf\_of\_expSumSmall}).

\emph{The one-image engine.} With the coset hypothesis in hand, the theorem of
\S\ref{sec:counts-index} applies at the single prime $p$: above the threshold the
level at $p$ is $\lceil p^{\gamma} \rceil$, and
$\lceil p^{\gamma} \rceil \le (p-1)/\gcd(N-1,p-1)$ because the quotient is an
integer of size at least $p^{\gamma}$. The hypothesis of this statement,
$p^{\gamma} \le (p-1)/\gcd(N-1,p-1)$, is exactly what that comparison needs.
Hence $G(N(x)) = 1$.
\end{proof}

The content of the display is its contrapositive, and the prose states it that
way rather than in a footnote: an exceptional $N$ has \emph{every} local image small,
and equivalently
\[
  \gcd\bigl(N-1,\, p-1\bigr) \;>\; \tfrac14\, p^{1-\gamma}
  \qquad \text{at every } p \parallel N ,
\]
for $p$ above a threshold that depends on $\gamma$ alone. (The factor $1/4$ is
the one the ceiling costs: $\lceil p^{\gamma} \rceil \le 2p^{\gamma}$ and
$p - 1 \ge p/2$.) That is the quantitative form of ``no parameter explains'': an
exceptional $N$ is one whose primes each see almost all of the predecessor $p-1$ inside
the exponent $N-1$ --- the numbers as close to Korselt as a non-Korselt number
can be. The exponential-sum version of the same statement, with
\lean{ExpSumSmall} in place of the coset hypothesis, is $\lvCond$
\lean{residueGcd\_eq\_one\_of\_large\_image\_expSum}; the two differ only in which
hypothesis is written into the signature.

\subsection{The count at the exponent}
\label{sec:counts-count}

\begin{theorem}[The exceptional semiprimes are $X^{1/2+2\gamma}$]
\label{thm:counts-BGK}
\lvCond{} \lean{card\_blind\_semiprimes\_BGK}
Assume $\lean{BGKBound}$. Then for every $\gamma > 0$ there is $Q$ such that for
every $X \ge 1$,
\[
  \#\,\mathcal{B}(X) \;\le\;
  \bigl(\lfloor \sqrt X \rfloor + 1\bigr)\,
  \bigl(Q + \lceil X^{\gamma} \rceil\bigr)^{2} .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{The level and its maximum.} Take the level \lean{bgkLevel} of the previous
subsection, and $K = Q + \lceil X^{\gamma} \rceil$. Above the threshold the level
at $q$ is $\lceil q^{\gamma} \rceil$, which is at most $\lceil X^{\gamma} \rceil$
for $q \le X$; below it the level is $q$, which is at most $X$ and hence at most
$K$. So $L(q) \le K$ on $[0,X]$.

\emph{The hypothesis of the count.} The level's exponential-sum bound gives
\lean{CosetMeets\allowbreak{}LowerHalf} and, with it, the hypothesis of
Theorem~\ref{thm:counts-upper}, including its case $q = 2$.

\emph{The count.} Theorem~\ref{thm:counts-upper} at that $K$ gives
$(\lfloor \sqrt X \rfloor + 1)K^{2}$, which is the displayed bound.
\end{proof}

Read as an order of magnitude the theorem says
$\#\,\mathcal{B}(X) \ll_{\gamma} X^{1/2+2\gamma}$ for every $\gamma > 0$, and
the constant is ineffective: $\gamma$ is given, $Q$ exists, and no value of it is
produced. Two things about the statement deserve to be said where it stands
rather than in a note. The exponent $1/2$ is not an accident of the injection ---
the injection is the pair of predecessors, and the exponent of a semiprime is
what it is --- but the $2\gamma$ is what the hypothesis costs, and it can be
made arbitrarily small only because BGK is available for every $\gamma$. And the
theorem counts \emph{all} of the exceptional semiprimes, with no condition on their number of
prime factors beyond the two the injection needs.

\subsection{The same input on the criterion's side}
\label{sec:counts-fejer}

\S\ref{sec:wrap-image} left one hypothesis undischarged and said that the
derivation was this section's. The hypothesis is \lean{CosetMeetsClassesMod4}:
for a level $L$, every coset of a local image of order at least $L(q)$ has an
integer representative in every class modulo four. The derivation is the same
mechanism as the last subsection, with one change that costs a factor: the
substitution of ``every class modulo four'' for ``the lower half'' forces the
exponential sums to be small at ratio $1/26$ instead of $1/3$, and the
trigonometric polynomial that carries the ratio is a Fej\'er kernel of degree
eleven. The numbers $11$ and $26$ are not chosen; they are computed, and the
paragraph says where each comes from.

\emph{The kernel.} For $n \ge 0$ and real $t$,
\[
  F_n(t) \;=\; \frac{1}{n+1}\,
  \Bigl| \sum_{k=0}^{n} e(kt) \Bigr|^{2}
  \;=\; 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{2\,(n+1-k)}{n+1}\, \cos 2\pi k t ,
\]
the first form by definition (\lean{fejerKernel}) and the equality by expanding
the square (\lean{fejerKernel\_eq\_normSq}, \lean{normSq\_sum}) and regrouping
the double sum by the difference of its indices (\lean{card\_pair\_add},
\lean{sum\_cos\_of\_pair\_diff}, \lean{fejerKernel\_cosine}).

\emph{The coefficients at eleven.} At $n = 11$ the coefficients are
$c_k = 2(12-k)/12$ (\lean{fejer11CosineCoeff}), and their absolute sum is
\[
  \sum_{k=1}^{11} |c_k| \;=\; 11
  \qquad (\lv\ \lean{fejer11\_cosine\_coeff\_abs\_sum}) ,
\]
because $\sum_{k=1}^{11}(12-k) = 66$ and $66/6 = 11$. This is the first of the
four numbers: eleven modes, whose coefficients fall from $11/6$ to $1/6$ and
whose total weight is exactly the number of modes.

\emph{The sidelobe level.} The chord identity $|1 - e^{i\theta}| = 2|\sin(\theta/2)|$
($\lv$ \lean{normSq\_one\_sub\_exp\_mul\_I}, $\lv$
\lean{norm\_one\_sub\_exp\_mul\_I}) turns the geometric-series bound
($\lv$ \lean{geom\_exp\_sum\_norm\_le}) into the reciprocal-sine bound
$F_{11}(x) \le 1/(12 \sin^{2}\pi x)$ ($\lv$
\lean{fejerKernel\_le\_inv\_sin\_sq}). On the sidelobes $|x| \in [1/8, 1/2]$ this
is at most
$\lambda = 1/(12 \sin^{2}(\pi/8))$ (\lean{fejer11Lambda}, $\lv$
\lean{fejerKernel\_le\_lambda\_of\_eighth}), and
\[
  \lambda \;<\; \frac{15}{26}
  \qquad (\lv\ \lean{fejer11Lambda\_lt\_fifteen\_div\_twenty\_six}) ,
\]
from $\sin^{2}(\pi/8) = (2 - \sqrt 2)/4$ and $\sqrt 2 < 64/45$. So the mean of
the minorant $m(t) = F_{11}(t - 1/8) - \lambda$ (\lean{fejerMinorant11}) is
$1 - \lambda > 11/26$ ($\lv$
\lean{fejer11\_mean\_gt\_eleven\_div\_twenty\_six}). That is where $26$ and the
mean $11/26$ come from: the eleven modes are charged at $1/26$ of the mass each,
against a mean strictly above $11/26$, and the inequality is strict by exactly
the margin the next paragraph needs.

\emph{The minorant vanishes off the arc.} Call $t$ \emph{outside the quarter arc}
if it lies outside the open interval of length $1/4$ centred at $1/8$, modulo
one (\lean{OutsideQuarterArc}); every real $t$ outside the quarter arc has an
 integer translate with $|t - 1/8 - k| \in [1/8, 1/2]$ ($\lv$
\lean{exists\_repr\_outsideQuarterArc}), the kernel is one-periodic ($\lv$
\lean{fejerKernel\_add\_int}), and a point at distance at least $1/4$ from $0$
modulo one is outside the arc ($\lv$
\lean{outsideQuarterArc\_of\_ge\_one\_fourth}, $\lv$
\lean{outsideQuarterArc\_add\_int}). Hence
$m(t) \le 0$ off the arc ($\lv$ \lean{fejerMinorant11\_nonpos\_of\_outside}),
which is the pointwise half of the package, stated as
\lean{FejerPointwiseMinorantWorks} and proved as
\lean{fejerPointwiseMinorantWorks}.

\emph{The minorant as a series.} Off the arc the minorant is nonpositive, and on
the arc it is a trigonometric polynomial:
\[
  m(t) \;=\; (1 - \lambda) + \sum_{k=1}^{11} c_k \cos 2\pi k\,(t - 1/8)
  \qquad (\lv\ \lean{fejerMinorant11\_eq\_trig}) ,
\]
by the addition formula for the cosine ($\lv$ \lean{cos\_sub\_eighth}). At a point
$t = (u b^{e} \bmod q)/q - \delta$ of the shifted lattice each term splits into a
cosine sum and a sine sum over the image, with a phase that does not depend on the
element ($\lv$ \lean{cos\_affine\_shift}). The sums are bounded by the hypothesis
\lean{ExpSumSmallR}, the ratio $\rho$ form of the exponential-sum bound,
identified with the earlier form at $\rho = 1/3$ ($\lv$
\lean{expSumSmall\_iff\_expSumSmallR}): a cosine sum and a sine sum are each below
$\rho |S_q|$ ($\lvCond$ \lean{abs\_sum\_cos\_lt\_of\_expSumSmallR}, $\lvCond$
\lean{abs\_sum\_sin\_lt\_of\_expSumSmallR}), and any fixed linear combination is
below $\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}}\,\rho\,|S_q|$ ($\lvCond$
\lean{abs\_linear\_comb\_expSum\_le}).

\emph{The contradiction.} Suppose a whole coset lay outside the arc. Summing $m$
over it gives $\le 0$ by the pointwise half, while the series and the bound at
$\rho = 1/26$ give
\[
  \sum_{x \in uS_q} m(x/q) \;\ge\; |S_q|\,(1-\lambda)
  - \sum_{k=1}^{11} |c_k| \cdot \frac{|S_q|}{26}
  \;>\; |S_q|\,\Bigl(\frac{11}{26} - \frac{11}{26}\Bigr) \;=\; 0 ,
\]
the strict inequality being $1 - \lambda > 11/26$ and the second term being
$11\,|S_q|/26$ by the absolute sum of the coefficients. So some element of the
coset lies inside the arc. Writing $v$ with $4v \equiv 1 \pmod q$ ($\lv$
\lean{exists\_inv\_four\_of\_odd\_prime}, $\lv$ \lean{exists\_inv\_four}) the arc
condition becomes $4X < q$ for $X = (u b^{e} \bmod q)$, and for each $a < 4$ one
shifts into the exact class $X = a + 4Y$ with $a + 4Y < q$ ($\lv$
\lean{mod4\_iff\_scaled\_lt}). The four forms of that conclusion --- open quarter,
quarter, affine quarter, exact class --- are one statement with four signatures
($\lvCond$ \lean{coset\_meets\_open\_quarter\_of\_expSumSmall26}, $\lvCond$
\lean{coset\_meets\_quarter\_of\_expSumSmall26}, $\lvCond$
\lean{coset\_meets\_quarter\_affine\_of\_expSumSmall26}, $\lvCond$
\lean{coset\_meets\_class\_of\_expSumSmall26}), and the interval form is the
proposition \lean{CosetMeetsQuarterInterval}.

\emph{The package, and the gate at thirteen.} The two halves are packaged as
\lean{FejerElevenModeFourierWorks} and \lean{FejerQuarterMinorantWorks}, proved as
$\lv$ \lean{fejerElevenModeFourierWorks} and $\lv$ \lean{fejerQuarterMinorantWorks};
the Fourier conclusion is $\lvCond$ \lean{fejer\_eleven\_mode\_fourier}, and its
conversion from an arc to a residue class is $\lvCond$
\lean{fejer\_affine\_class\_mod4}. The level is \lean{bgkLevelClasses}, the
$1/26$ analogue of the previous level, and the published theorem supplies it
($\lvCond$ \lean{expSumSmallR\_of\_bgkBound}, $\lvCond$
\lean{expSumSmall26\_of\_bgkBound}, $\lvCond$ \lean{bgkLevelClasses\_hyp}). One
gate remains: the argument needs $13 \le q$, and the level supplies it, because
below the threshold the level is $q$ and no image can have order at least $q$
($\lv$ \lean{bgkLevelClasses\_gate}). With the gate,
\lean{CosetMeetsClassesMod4} follows at that level ($\lvCond$
\lean{cosetMeetsClassesMod4\_of\_expSumSmall26}), and the hypothesis
\S\ref{sec:wrap-image} left open is discharged.

Two theorems of \S\ref{sec:wrap-image} then run on the discharged hypothesis, and
they are stated here only to say that they do: at one large local image the
criterion does not vanish ($\lvCond$
\lean{not\_allZero\_of\_large\_image\_BGK}), and the semiprimes on which the
criterion vanishes at every base are counted by the same injection
($\lvCond$ \lean{card\_missed\_semiprimes\_BGK}), with
$(\lfloor \sqrt X \rfloor + 1)(Q + \lceil X^{\gamma} \rceil)^{2}$ as the bound.
Neither is displayed: the composites at which the criterion of 2009 vanishes at
every base are Part~III's subject
(\S\ref{sec:census}), and what this section supplies is the hypothesis under
which the count of \S\ref{sec:wrap-image} becomes a theorem. The criterion's side
therefore has a bound of the same shape as the invariant's, and the same
ineffective constant, because it is the same exponential sum read through a
different minorant.

\subsection{The lower shape}
\label{sec:counts-lower}

The upper bound says how many there can be. The lower bound says how many there
must be, on one family, and it is where the counting sets of the family belong:
they are what the lower bound counts, and they arrive after the upper bound
rather than before it, because a set defined to be counted is only worth defining
once the counting has a shape.

Write $q_j(n) = 1 + j(n-1)$ (\lean{familyQ}, definitionally equal to that
expression by $\lv$ \lean{familyQ\_eq}), and for $X \ge 1$ let
\[
  \mathcal{P}_j(X) = \bigl\{\, p \;:\; p \text{ and } q_j(p) \text{ prime},\;
  2 < p,\; p\,q_j(p) \le X \,\bigr\}
  \qquad (\lean{familyPairs}) ,
\]
with $\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{b}}_j(X) \subseteq \mathcal{P}_j(X)$ the members on
which $G(p\,q_j(p)) > 1$ (\lean{familyBlind}) and
$\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{m}}_j(X) \subseteq \mathcal{P}_j(X)$ the members on which
the criterion vanishes, $\lean{AllZero}\bigl((p\,q_j(p)+1)/2\bigr)$
(\lean{familyMiss}). The mate is the larger of the two primes whenever $j \ge 2$
and $p > 2$ ($\lv$ \lean{lt\_familyQ}), so a member of $\mathcal{P}_j(X)$ is a
genuine pair $p < q$ and $\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{b}}_j(X)$ sits inside
$\mathcal{B}(X)$ ($\lv$ \lean{familyBlind\_mem\_blindSemiprimes}) and
$\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{m}}_j(X)$ inside $\mathcal{M}(X)$ ($\lv$
\lean{familyMiss\_mem\_missedSemiprimes}); counting a subset counts at most the
whole, in the two cases
$\lv$ \lean{card\_familyBlind\_le\_blindSemiprimes} and
$\lv$ \lean{card\_familyMiss\_le\_missedSemiprimes}.

Two hypotheses, and then the theorem. The \emph{Bateman--Horn} hypothesis for the
family is $\lean{BatemanHornFamily}\, j\, C$: there is $C > 0$ with
$|\mathcal{P}_j(X)| \ge C \sqrt X/(\log X)^{2}$ for all large $X$. Its linear
form is the classical count of the pairs of linear forms
$\bigl(n,\, 1 + j(n-1)\bigr)$, $\lean{BatemanHornLinear}\, j\, C$, and it implies
the product form ($\lvCond$ \lean{batemanHornFamily\_of\_linear}). The
passage is the plumbing of the subsection: if $j Y^{2} \le X$ then the pairs
$n \le Y$ inject into $\mathcal{P}_j(X)$ ($\lv$
\lean{linear\_le\_familyPairs}), using $q_j(n) \le jn$ ($\lv$
\lean{familyQ\_le\_mul}); the choice $Y = \lfloor \sqrt{X/j} \rfloor$ satisfies
$j Y^{2} \le X$ ($\lv$ \lean{j\_mul\_sqrt\_sq\_le}); and
$\sqrt X \le \sqrt j\,(Y+1) \le 2\sqrt j\, Y$ ($\lv$
\lean{sqrt\_le\_mul\_sqrt\_div}) halves the logarithm at the cost of the
constant, $\log X \ge 2 \log Y$. The constant the passage produces is
$C/(4\sqrt j)$, which the last line weakens to $C$; the prose says only that it
is a constant, because the factor is $4\sqrt j$ and nothing depends on its value.

The \emph{density} hypothesis is $\lean{FixedCosetBlindDensity}\, j\, \delta$:
there is $\delta > 0$ with
$|\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{b}}_j(X)| \ge \delta\,|\mathcal{P}_j(X)|$ for all large
$X$; the same shape with $\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{m}}_j$ in place of
$\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{b}}_j$ is $\lean{FixedCosetMissDensity}\, j\, \delta$. The
shape of the conclusion is the predicate $\lean{GrowsLikeSqrtOverLogSq}\, F\, c$:
$c > 0$ and $F(X) \ge c\sqrt X/(\log X)^{2}$ from some point on. With these, the
lower half of the count is four lines.

\begin{theorem}[The lower shape]
\label{thm:counts-lower}
\lvCond{} \lean{card\_blind\_semiprimes\_ge}
Assume $\lean{BatemanHornFamily}\, j\, C$ and
$\lean{FixedCosetBlindDensity}\, j\, \delta$, with $j \ge 2$, $C > 0$ and
$\delta > 0$. Then there is $X_0$ such that for every $X \ge X_0$ with $X \ge 2$,
\[
  \#\,\mathcal{B}(X) \;\ge\; \delta\,C\,
  \frac{\sqrt X}{(\log X)^{2}} ,
\]
that is, $\lean{GrowsLikeSqrtOverLogSq}$ holds for $X \mapsto \#\,\mathcal{B}(X)$
with constant $\delta C$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{The two thresholds.} Unpack the two hypotheses: $C > 0$ and $X_1$ with
$|\mathcal{P}_j(X)| \ge C\sqrt X/(\log X)^{2}$ for $X \ge X_1$, and
$\delta > 0$ with $|\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{b}}_j(X)| \ge
\delta\,|\mathcal{P}_j(X)|$ for $X \ge X_2$. Take
$X \ge \max(X_1, X_2)$.

\emph{The chain.} Multiplying the first inequality by $\delta > 0$ gives
$\delta C \sqrt X/(\log X)^{2} \le \delta\,|\mathcal{P}_j(X)|$, which is at most
$|\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{b}}_j(X)|$ by the second, which is at most
$\#\,\mathcal{B}(X)$ because every member of $\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{b}}_j(X)$ is a
semiprime at which some $C_M$ has no witness and the
correspondence $p \mapsto (p, q_j(p))$ is injective ($\lv$
\lean{card\_familyBlind\_le\_blindSemiprimes}). The chain is the statement, with
$X_0 = \max(X_1,X_2)$ and $c = \delta C > 0$.
\end{proof}

The theorem is a packaging, and it is proved as one: the density hypothesis is
close to the conclusion, and the substance of the section is the upper bound and
the location of the mass. What the packaging does do is name the two inputs
exactly, which is the point --- the lower shape for $\mathcal{B}$ rests on
Bateman--Horn and on one equidistribution input, and on nothing else. The same
four lines give the count for $\mathcal{M}$,
$\lvCond$ \lean{card\_missed\_semiprimes\_ge}, with
$\lean{FixedCosetMissDensity}$ in place of
$\lean{FixedCosetBlindDensity}$; it is the statement that pairs with the count of
\S\ref{sec:wrap-image} and it is named once, here, because the composites at which
the criterion vanishes at every base are not this Part's subject.

\subsection{The exponent is one half}
\label{sec:counts-exponent}

The two bounds are now in hand, and the exponent follows from them without
further mathematics.

\begin{theorem}[The exponent of the count]
\label{thm:exponent-half}
\lvCond{} \lean{blind\_exponent\_half\_conditional}
Assume $\lean{BGKBound}$, $\lean{BatemanHornFamily}\, j\, C$ and
$\lean{FixedCosetBlindDensity}\, j\, \delta$, with $j \ge 2$, $C > 0$ and
$\delta > 0$. Then for every $\gamma > 0$ there are $c > 0$, $Q$ and $X_0$ such
that for every $X \ge X_0$ with $X \ge 2$,
\[
  c\,\frac{\sqrt X}{(\log X)^{2}} \;\le\; \#\,\mathcal{B}(X) \;\le\;
  \bigl(\lfloor \sqrt X \rfloor + 1\bigr)\,
  \bigl(Q + \lceil X^{\gamma} \rceil\bigr)^{2} .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Below.} Theorem~\ref{thm:counts-lower} at $\delta$ and $C$ gives
$c = \delta C > 0$ and $X_0$ with the lower inequality.

\emph{Above.} Theorem~\ref{thm:counts-BGK} at the given $\gamma$ gives $Q$ with
the upper inequality for every $X$.

Together they are the display, and the two quantifier orders are different: $Q$
depends on $\gamma$, and the lower constant does not.
\end{proof}

The exponent is $1/2$ in the sense that
\[
  \frac{X^{1/2}}{(\log X)^{2}} \;\ll\; \#\,\mathcal{B}(X) \;\ll_{\gamma}\;
  X^{1/2+2\gamma}
\]
for every $\gamma > 0$, and \emph{not} in the sense that the asymptotic constant
is known. The constant is a conjecture and is not assumed anywhere. With
Bateman--Horn's constant $C_{BH}(j)$ for the pair and the density $\delta_j$, the
prediction is
\[
  \#\,\bigl(\text{exceptional family members} \le X\bigr) \;\sim\;
  \delta_j\, C \,\frac{\sqrt{X/j}}{\log^{2} X} ,
\]
where the halving of the logarithm at $Y = \sqrt{X/j}$ contributes a factor $4$,
so that the constant $C$ in the display is $4\,C_{BH}(j)$; the statement is the proposition
\lean{BlindCountAsymptotic}, it is never used as a hypothesis, and the factor is
measured rather than derived
(\texttt{experiments/\allowbreak cursorG\_bh\_constant.py}). The same conjecture
for the criterion's own set --- the same shape with $\mathcal{M}(X)$ and
$\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{m}}_j(X)$ in place of the exceptional sets --- is the proposition
\lean{MissCountAsymptotic}, and neither is assumed anywhere in this section.

\subsection{Where the mass sits}
\label{sec:counts-mass}

An exponent does not say where the members are, and for this section the ``where''
is a stronger statement than the ``how many'' of \S\ref{sec:counts-exponent}.
Three steps locate it, and each is a statement about the local images rather than
about the count.

\emph{Even $j$: nothing.} If $j$ is even and $p > 2$, the two valuations
separate:
\[
  v_2\bigl(p\,q_j(p) - 1\bigr) \;<\; v_2\bigl(q_j(p) - 1\bigr)
  \qquad (\lv\ \lean{family\_even\_two\_adic\_gap}) ,
\]
because $p\,q_j(p) - 1 = (p-1)(1 + jp)$ with $1 + jp$ odd, while
$q_j(p) - 1 = j(p-1)$ with $j$ even. A gap in the $2$-adic valuation collapses
the invariant ($\lv$ \lean{residueGcd\_eq\_one\_of\_padicValNat\_lt},
\S\ref{sec:local}), so every member of the family has $G = 1$ ($\lv$
\lean{family\_even\_residueGcd\_eq\_one}) and the criterion does not vanish at any of
them
($\lv$ \lean{family\_even\_not\_allZero}, by $\lv$
\lean{not\_allZero\_of\_padicValNat\_lt}, \S\ref{sec:wrap}). The first live
value of $j$ on the family is therefore odd, and the smallest odd $j \ge 2$ is
$3$.

\emph{$j = 3$: nothing, and provably so.} Here $q_3(p) = 3p-2$ ($\lv$
\lean{familyQ\_three}), so $q_3(3) = 7$ ($\lv$
\lean{familyQ\_three\_seven}) and $(3,7)$ is a member for every $X \ge 21$
($\lv$ \lean{familyPairs\_three\_contains\_three}). But every member has
$G = 1$ ($\lv$ \lean{residueGcd\_eq\_one\_of\_three\_mul\_sub\_two},
\S\ref{sec:local}), so the family's exceptional set is empty
($\lv$ \lean{familyBlind\_three\_eq\_empty}). Hence the density hypothesis is
\emph{false} at $j = 3$ for every $\delta > 0$ ($\lv$
\lean{not\_fixedCosetBlindDensity\_three}): a family none of whose members is
exceptional cannot be exceptional for a positive proportion of its members, and
the family is nonempty
($\lv$ \lean{one\_le\_card\_familyPairs\_three}). So the density
hypothesis is not vacuous everywhere; it is refuted at the one
degree where the theory says it must be, which is what makes its failure
informative.

\emph{$j = 5$: one class.} If $G > 1$ on a member of the family at $j = 5$, then
\[
  p \;\equiv\; 7 \pmod 8
  \qquad (\lv\ \lean{family\_five\_seven\_mod\_eight\_of\_one\_lt}) ,
\]
and the proof separates the alternatives. On $q \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ --- which is
what $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ gives, since $q = 5p - 4$ --- the invariant collapses
outright ($\lv$ \lean{residueGcd\_eq\_one\_of\_family\_five\_one\_mod\_four},
\S\ref{sec:families-five}). On $p \equiv 3 \pmod 8$ the numerator at $p$ is
$v_p = (p+1)/4$, which is odd; the invariant divides it ($\lv$
\lean{residueGcd\_dvd\_numerator}, \S\ref{sec:numerators}) and divides $2$ ($\lv$
\lean{residueGcd\_dvd\_two\_of\_family\_five}, \S\ref{sec:families-five}), so
$G = 1$ ($\lv$
\lean{family\_five\_residueGcd\_eq\_one\_of\_not\_seven\_mod\_eight}). The exceptional set
therefore lives on a single residue class, of weight $1/4$ among the members, and
the density of the previous subsection is the density \emph{on that class}
multiplied by that weight.

\begin{conjecture}[The density at $j = 5$]
\label{conj:delta-five}
\sorry{}
For every $\delta < 31/4096$ the fixed-coset density for exceptional members holds at
$j = 5$: $\lean{FixedCosetBlindDensity}\, 5\, \delta$.
\end{conjecture}

The number is a model, and the model is the geometric face. On the class
$p \equiv 7 \pmod 8$ the coset of the fifth roots is equidistributed modulo the
primes of the family, its reduced elements fill the cube $[0,3/4]^{3}$, and the
condition $G > 1$ is $1/2 \le \sum x_i \le 5/4$ there; the volume of that region
is $31/128$, the parity factor $1/8$ brings the proportion on the class to $31/1024$, and
multiplying by the class weight $1/4$ gives
$31/4096 \approx 0.00757$. The theorem behind the model --- fixed-coset
equidistribution of the roots of $\Phi_5$ modulo primes --- is open, and the
development has no \texttt{sorry} for it: the density is a hypothesis in a
signature (\lean{FixedCosetBlindDensity}), and the conjecture above is the
statement that it holds at that constant, not a gap in a proof.
Remark~\ref{rem:mass} records what has been measured against the model, with its
ranges and its scripts.

\begin{remark}[The mass, measured]
\label{rem:mass}
The model is checked against the family, and the check is a measurement with a
range and a script, never a layer of proof. On the members with $p < 6 \cdot
10^{6}$ the observed density of $G = 2$ is $348/45126 = 0.00771$
(\texttt{experiments/\allowbreak delta5\_geometric.py}), against the model's
$0.00757$. The same count on a scan to $q < 3 \cdot 10^{7}$, split by whether
$p$ is prime, gives $0.00771$ on the prime side and $0.00741$ on the composite
side, with a two-proportion $z$-score of $+0.71$
(\texttt{experiments/\allowbreak family\_independence\_test.py}): primality of
$p$ is not what the coset condition sees, which is what makes the product
heuristic legitimate. And the count itself, over $j \in \{5,7,9,11,13\}$ with
$p < 3 \cdot 10^{6}$ and complete for $N \le 4.5 \cdot 10^{13}$, is in
Table~\ref{tab:count-blinds}. The last column is the ratio that the model would
make constant; it rises from $0.021$ to $0.037$ across three decades, which is
the honest reading of a range this short --- the shape is right, and the range
does not pin the constant.
\end{remark}

\emph{The reading.} The mass sits on one class of one family, the class
$p \equiv 7 \pmod 8$ of $q = 1 + 5(p-1)$, and $300\,979\,369$ is the member of
it that the book proves: $38791 - 1 = 5\,(7759 - 1)$, so the pair is on the
family at $j = 5$, its smaller prime is $7759 \equiv 7 \pmod 8$, and its
invariant is $2$ (\S\ref{sec:off-carmichael}). That closes the question the
section opened, in the only form in which the book can close it: the exceptional set off
Korselt is infinite if the density holds, sparse at the square-root scale, and
located on a single class of a single family.

\subsection{Scope}
\label{sec:counts-honesty}

Nothing here makes the criterion a test: the cost is in the bases, and it
is \S\ref{sec:schedule}'s and \S\ref{sec:least-safe}'s subject --- this section
counts numbers, not operations. Nothing here says that an exceptional $N$ is a
Carmichael number: that is false (\S\ref{sec:off-carmichael}), and
the sets counted here are semiprimes off the Korselt locus. And nothing numbered
in this section is unconditional. Its one unconditional input is the index bound
recalled at the head of \S\ref{sec:counts-index},
$G(N) \le \gcd(N-1,p-1)$, with the two corollaries drawn from it; each of the
five displays carries its hypothesis in its statement, $L$'s bound in
Theorem~\ref{thm:counts-upper}, the exponential-sum bound in
Theorems~\ref{thm:large-image-BGK} and~\ref{thm:counts-BGK}, Bateman--Horn and
the density in Theorem~\ref{thm:counts-lower}, and all three in
Theorem~\ref{thm:exponent-half}.

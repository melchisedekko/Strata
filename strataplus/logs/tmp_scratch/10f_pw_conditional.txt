
\section{What a Hypothesis Buys}
\label{sec:pw-conditional}

The problem P-W was stated in \S\ref{sec:pw} with a list of certified members
and a rule that carries a member along a family (\S\ref{sec:pw-propagate}); what it did not have is a way
of making a new member out of nothing. \S\ref{sec:pw-hypothesis} said so, and
deferred two questions to this section: what the hypothesis behind such a rule
is, and what the problem reduces to under it. The two are answered here, in that
order, and the order is the section's argument --- a reader who has just been
told that nobody can settle the problem is owed the hypothesis route first,
because it is what an outsider would try, and the reduction second, because a
reduction is worth having only once it is clear what it is a reduction of.

Two things can be done with a problem for which no way of making a new member is known, and this section does both.
The first is to assume the arithmetic --- Dickson's conjecture, or
Bateman--Horn, which is strictly stronger on a family --- and then the
infinitude is a theorem whose statement carries its hypothesis. The second is to
make the problem smaller, and exactly one move does that: at $\omega \le 5$ the
carry condition is redundant, so a miss there is the single congruence
$4 \mid v_p$ at every prime. The second move has a ceiling --- at $\omega = 6$
the two conditions separate, and a Carmichael number exists where they do ---
and that ceiling is where the book stops.

The plan of the section: at $\omega \le 5$
the two conjuncts of the miss condition --- $4 \mid v_p$ and \eqref{eq:pw-at}, in
Theorem~\ref{thm:pw-at} --- collapse into one, and at $\omega = 6$ they do not. The two halves of that sentence are
Theorems~\ref{thm:five-factors} and \ref{thm:six-factors}, and everything between
them is what makes the first half a reduction and the second half a ceiling.
\S\ref{sec:conditional-hypothesis} says
what a Bateman--Horn hypothesis is and extracts the two theorems it implies;
\S\ref{sec:conditional-families} runs the four families of P-W through it and
into the small problem; \S\ref{sec:conditional-carry} proves that the carry is
the Egyptian sum modulo every divisor of $N-1$, which is the one congruence the
rest of the section uses; \S\ref{sec:conditional-small} states the problem
without the no-carry conjunct and identifies its two forms, and
\S\ref{sec:conditional-worth} says what the hypothesis of that problem is worth;
\S\ref{sec:conditional-ceiling} shows why the reduction stops at $\omega = 6$,
on a number; \S\ref{sec:conditional-rate} proves that the last numerator is
forced and quotes what the census says about the rate;
\S\ref{sec:conditional-grid} bounds the error in the model behind that rate;
and \S\ref{sec:conditional-stop} says where the book stops.

The same problem is told in \cite{Companion-korselt-misses} in the
order of its three layers --- the certified instances, the conditional
infinitude, the open problem --- and that order is the article's frame. Here the
frame is the book's grades, so the conditional layer arrives first and the
reduction second, and the two documents state the same mathematics in different
orders on purpose. Nothing in this section is history. The provenance of the
formula was spent in \S\ref{sec:origin}, the parameter ledger in
\S\ref{sec:off-carmichael}, and what is left here is arithmetic.

\subsection{What a hypothesis buys}
\label{sec:conditional-hypothesis}

Throughout, $x \ge 2$, $N = N(x) = 2x-1$, and $\omega$ is the number of prime
factors of $N$. For $p \mid N$ the cofactor is $N/p$, which is
$\lean{cof}(S,p)$ of \S\ref{sec:wrap} at a prime of the support of a squarefree
number; a \emph{numerator} at $p$ is an integer $v_p$ with $0 < v_p < p$ and
$p \mid v_p\,(N/p)+1$; and $C$ is the carry of \eqref{eq:carry}, the integer with
\[
  \sum_{p \mid N} v_p\,\frac{N}{p} \;=\; (C+1)N - 1 .
\]
The notation is \S\ref{sec:korselt}'s and the two definitions are not repeated.
What is repeated, because the whole section turns on it, is that the carry is
bounded by the number of prime factors and not by their size:
$0 \le C \le \omega - 2$\ \ ($\lv$ \lean{carry\_nonneg}, $\lv$
\lean{carry\_le\_card\_sub\_two}).

Fix $k \ge 1$, a $k$-tuple $a = (a_1,\dots,a_k)$ of positive integers, a residue
$r$ and a modulus $M > 0$. The hypothesis of this subsection is the lower bound
of Bateman--Horn \cite{BatemanHorn1962} on the single progression
$m = Mt + r$, restricted to the $k$ forms of the family:
$\lean{BHFor}\,a\,r\,M$ asserts that there are $c > 0$ and $T_0$ such that for
every $T \ge T_0$
\[
  c\,\frac{T}{(\log T)^k}
  \;\le\;
  \#\{\, t \le T \;:\; a_i(Mt+r)+1 \text{ prime for all } i \,\} .
\]
The set counted is $\lean{goodSet}\,a\,r\,M\,T$. Two things about it are worth
saying before it is used. It is a proposition, a hypothesis written into the
statement of every result below that assumes it, and never an axiom: the
theorems below are theorems about a conditional, and each of them says so in its
header. And it is \emph{stronger} than the qualitative statement the families
need, which is the first theorem: a bound of order $T/(\log T)^k$ is unbounded,
so assuming it settles Dickson's question on the progression outright.

\begin{theorem}[Bateman--Horn implies Dickson on the progression]
\label{thm:bh-dickson}
\lvCond{} \lean{dicksonForK\_of\_BHFor}
Let $k \ge 1$, let $a$ be a $k$-tuple of positive integers, let $r, M \in \N$
with $M > 0$, and suppose $\lean{BHFor}\,a\,r\,M$. Then for every bound $B$
there is $t > B$ such that every number $a_i(Mt+r)+1$ is prime. Equivalently,
$\lean{DicksonForK}\,a\,r\,M$ of \S\ref{sec:pw-hypothesis} holds.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose the conclusion fails. Then some bound $B$ is never exceeded: every good
$t$ lies in $\{0,\dots,B\}$, so for every $T$ the set $\lean{goodSet}\,a\,r\,M\,T$
is contained in $\{0,\dots,B\}$ and has at most $B+1$ elements.

Put $\varepsilon = c/(2(B+1))$, a positive real. The only asymptotic input of
the theorem is $(\log T)^k = o(T)$; in the form needed here, there is $T_1$ with
$(\log T)^k \le \varepsilon T$ for $T \ge T_1$.

Let $T \ge \max(T_0, T_1, 3)$. The hypothesis gives
\[
  c\,\frac{T}{(\log T)^k}
  \;\ge\; c\,\frac{T}{\varepsilon T}
  \;=\; 2(B+1) \;>\; B+1 ,
\]
while the count of good $t \le T$ is at most $B+1$. That is a contradiction, so
for every $B$ there is a good $t > B$.

Finally put $m = Mt + r$. Since $M \ge 1$ and $r \ge 0$ one has $m \ge t > B$;
the congruence $m \equiv r \pmod M$ holds by construction; and every
$a_i m + 1$ is prime because $t$ is good. Those are the three clauses of
$\lean{DicksonForK}\,a\,r\,M$.
\end{proof}

The content of the theorem is one inequality read backwards: a lower bound of
order $T/(\log T)^k$ is unbounded, and an unbounded lower bound is what
infinitude means on a progression. Nothing number-theoretic entered --- the
passage from a count to a single good $t$ is the observation that a power of a
logarithm is little-oh of $T$ --- and that is the point of stating it. The
hypothesis has been moved from a conjecture about primes to a statement about
one family at a time, which is what makes the count below possible; nothing has
been added to the evidence for it.

\begin{theorem}[The count under Bateman--Horn]
\label{thm:bh-count}
\lvCond{} \lean{card\_pwData\_ge\_rpow\_of\_BHFor}
Let $k \ge 1$, let $a$ be a $k$-tuple of positive integers with $a_{i_0} > 0$
for some $i_0$, let $r, M \in \N$ with $M > 0$, and suppose that at every $t$
for which all $a_i(Mt+r)+1$ are prime the number $\lean{famNk}(a, Mt+r)$
carries P-W data in the sense of \S\ref{sec:pw-problem}. If
$\lean{BHFor}\,a\,r\,M$ holds, then there are $c' > 0$ and $X_0$ such that for
every $X \ge X_0$
\[
  c'\,\frac{X^{1/k}}{(\log X)^k}
  \;\le\;
  \#\{\, n \le X \;:\; \lean{PWData}(n) \,\} .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The count at the parameter of the family comes first
($\lvCond$ \lean{card\_pwData\_ge\_of\_BHFor}). On a good $t \le T$ the number
$\lean{famNk}(a, Mt+r)$ carries P-W data, by the hypothesis on the members, and
the map $t \mapsto \lean{famNk}(a, Mt+r)$ is strictly increasing: it is the
strictly monotone $\lean{famNk}$ ($\lv$ \lean{famNk\_strictMono}, which uses
$a_{i_0} > 0$) composed with the strictly increasing affine map $t \mapsto Mt+r$
($M > 0$). So distinct good $t$ give distinct members, all of them at most
$\lean{famNk}(a, MT+r)$, and
\[
  \#\{\, n \le \lean{famNk}(a, MT+r) \;:\; \lean{PWData}(n) \,\}
  \;\ge\; \#\lean{goodSet}\,a\,r\,M\,T
  \;\ge\; c\,\frac{T}{(\log T)^k}
\]
for $T \ge T_0$.

For the passage from $T$ to $X$, size the member. With $S = \sum_i a_i$ one has
$\lean{famNk}(a,m) \le (Sm+1)^k$ ($\lv$ \lean{famNk\_le\_pow}), and the member
is monotone in $m$, so it is enough to invert that inequality. Put
$D = S\,M$, $y = X^{1/k}$ and $T = \lfloor (y-1-Sr)/D \rfloor$. Then
$T \ge y/(2D)$ once $X$ is at least a bound depending on $S$, $r$, $M$, $T_0$
and $k$, and $T \ge T_0+3$ for that same bound.

At that $T$ the member is at most $X$: the inequality
$S(MT+r)+1 \le y$ is what the choice of $T$ gives, and raising it to the $k$-th
power and applying the size bound gives
$\lean{famNk}(a, MT+r) \le y^k = X$. Hence the count up to $X$ dominates the
count up to the member, and the count at the member is at least
$cT/(\log T)^k$ by the first step.

Two comparisons close the proof. The count is monotone in the bound, so it is
enough to compare at $X$. And $T \le X$, because $T \le MT+r < \lean{famNk}(a, MT+r) \le X$;
therefore $\log T \le \log X$ and $1/(\log X)^k \le 1/(\log T)^k$.

Combining, with $c' = c/(2D)$,
\[
  c'\,\frac{X^{1/k}}{(\log X)^k}
  \;\le\; c\,\frac{T}{(\log T)^k}
  \;\le\; \#\{\, n \le X \;:\; \lean{PWData}(n) \,\} ,
\]
which is the display of the theorem.
\end{proof}

The shape of the conclusion deserves a sentence of its own, because it is the
same shape in every instance below. The exponent on $X$ is $1/k$ and the power
of the logarithm is $k$, and both come from the single inequality
$\lean{famNk}(a,m) \le (Sm+1)^k$: a product of $k$ primes grows like $m^k$, so a
count of $T$ parameters is a count of $T$ numbers of size $T^k$. The theorem
promises infinitude, and it promises it in the only form a lower bound can take.

At accessible sizes the bound is no evidence. At
$k = 4$ the factor $X^{1/4}/(\log X)^4$ is $0.011$ at $X = 10^{18}$ and $1.39$
at $X = 10^{30}$. The first evaluation is a factor of ninety below one: below
$10^{18}$ the theorem says nothing at all, and the census of \S\ref{sec:conditional-rate}
is what the reader has there. The second is arithmetic at a scale no census
reaches, and a constant $c'$ smaller than one would move it. Those two numbers
are arithmetic, not Lean, and they carry no marker: they are the theorem's own
shape read twice.

\subsection{The four families, and where they land}
\label{sec:conditional-families}

The four families of \S\ref{sec:pw-propagate} are the ones that carry P-W data:
the three four-term tuples $(1,2,5,10)$ at $m \equiv 546 \pmod{720}$,
$(1,2,6,9)$ at $m \equiv 5\,670 \pmod{10\,080}$ and $(5,7,15,21)$ at
$m \equiv 4\,056 \pmod{26\,880}$, and the five-term tuple $(2,3,10,15,30)$ at
$m \equiv 12\,780 \pmod{21\,840}$. Each is certified by the data of
 \eqref{eq:pw-data} and the polynomial identity stated with it, which has, in each
 case, the coefficients of the family:
\[
  \sum_i u_i\,(\alpha_i X + \beta_i) \prod_{j \ne i} (a_j X + 1)
  \;=\; L\,\Bigl( \prod_i (a_i X + 1) - 1 \Bigr)
  \qquad (X \in \Z),
\]
with $L = 36$ for $(1,2,5,10)$, $L = 280$ for $(1,2,6,9)$ and $L = 64$ for
$(5,7,15,21)$. The three kernel-checked expansions are $\lv$
\lean{hpoly\_1\_2\_5\_10}, $\lv$ \lean{hpoly\_1\_2\_6\_9} and $\lv$
\lean{hpoly\_5\_7\_15\_21}; the five-term identity is \S\ref{sec:pw-propagate}'s
and is not restated.

With the identity in hand, each family yields a counting statement:
$\lvCond$ \lean{card\_pwData\_ge\_1\_2\_5\_10}, $\lvCond$
\lean{card\_pwData\_ge\_1\_2\_6\_9}, $\lvCond$
\lean{card\_pwData\_ge\_5\_7\_15\_21} and $\lvCond$
\lean{card\_pwData\_ge\_2\_3\_10\_15\_30} each assume
$\lean{BHFor}$ at their own modulus and conclude the $T$-form of
Theorem~\ref{thm:bh-count}, and the four $X$-forms
$\lvCond$ \lean{card\_pwData\_ge\_rpow\_1\_2\_5\_10}, $\lvCond$
\lean{card\_pwData\_ge\_rpow\_1\_2\_6\_9}, $\lvCond$
\lean{card\_pwData\_ge\_rpow\_5\_7\_15\_21} and $\lvCond$
\lean{card\_pwData\_ge\_rpow\_2\_3\_10\_15\_30} are the same instances of
Theorem~\ref{thm:bh-count}. Nothing distinguishes the four but the exponents
$1/4$, $1/4$, $1/4$ and $1/5$: the $k$ of the family enters the count as
$X^{1/k}$, and the logarithms as $(\log X)^k$.

The landing is a different theorem, and it is the one the section needs.
$\lvCond$ \lean{pwProblemSmall\_of\_dicksonFor} takes the numerical hypotheses
of a four-term family, its polynomial identity and $\lean{DicksonFor}$ for its
class, and returns $\lean{PWProblemSmall}$. Four steps. Dickson's statement,
applied to the bound $\max(n_0, m_0)$, gives a good $m > \max(n_0, m_0)$ in the
class; the propagation theorem of \S\ref{sec:pw-propagate} turns ``all four forms
prime at $m$'' into $\lean{PWData}(\lean{famN}(a,m))$; a product of four primes
has at most four prime factors ($\lv$ \lean{card\_primeFactors\_prod\_le}, the
prime factors of a product injecting into the index set), so the bound
$\omega \le 5$ holds; and $\lv$ \lean{pwSmall\_of\_pwData} deletes the Egyptian-sum
conjunct, which is exactly the passage from the data of \S\ref{sec:pw-problem}
to the predicate of \S\ref{sec:conditional-small}. The member exceeds its
parameter, $\lean{famN}(a,m) > m \ge n_0$ ($\lv$ \lean{lt\_famN} of
\S\ref{sec:pw-propagate}), which is the bound of the problem.

The four instances are that paragraph four times: $\lvCond$
\lean{pwProblemSmall\_of\_dickson\_1\_2\_5\_10}, $\lvCond$
\lean{pwProblemSmall\_of\_dickson\_1\_2\_6\_9} and $\lvCond$
\lean{pwProblemSmall\_of\_dickson\_5\_7\_15\_21} at the three four-term families, with the numerical
hypotheses discharged by finite evaluation and the polynomial identity supplied
by the matching expansion; and $\lvCond$
\lean{pwProblemSmall\_of\_dickson\_2\_3\_10\_15\_30} at the five-term family,
where the propagation step is the five-term one of \S\ref{sec:pw-propagate} and
the monotonicity step is $\lv$ \lean{lt\_famNk}. Each of the four carries its
hypothesis in the signature and none of them is $\lv$; the unconditional problem
is untouched by all four, exactly as in \S\ref{sec:pw-hypothesis}.

The point of the subsection is the last clause, not the count. The four families
do not merely give infinitely many P-W members conditionally --- that was
\S\ref{sec:pw-hypothesis} --- they give infinitely many with $\omega \le 5$, and
that is what makes them land in the small problem rather than beside it. The
reduction of \S\ref{sec:conditional-small} is therefore a reduction of a problem
that a hypothesis already populates: a family known to be infinite gives small
misses outright, and \S\ref{sec:conditional-small} composes exactly that. No
member is quoted here; the eight certified rows are \S\ref{sec:pw-members}'.

\subsection{The carry is the Egyptian sum modulo every divisor of \texorpdfstring{$N-1$}{N-1}}
\label{sec:conditional-carry}

The second half of the section lives inside the carry, and the one congruence it
uses is a rewriting of the carry's definition. It is worth stating as a numbered
result because everything after it is an application, and because what it does
\emph{not} use is the reason it can be applied where the Korselt locus is not
available.

\begin{theorem}[The carry is the Egyptian sum modulo every divisor of \texorpdfstring{$N-1$}{N-1}]
\label{thm:carry-congruence}
\lv{} \lean{egyptianSum\_modEq\_carry}
Let $S$ be a finite set of primes with $\prod_{p \in S} p = N$, let $v_p$ be
integers with $p \mid v_p\,(N/p)+1$ for $p \in S$ --- so that
$N \mid \sum_{p \in S} v_p\,(N/p) + 1$ ($\lv$ \lean{egyptianSum\_add\_one\_dvd},
\S\ref{sec:korselt}) --- and let $m$ be an integer with $m \mid N-1$. Then
\[
  \sum_{p \in S} v_p\,\frac{N}{p} \;\equiv\; C \pmod m .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By the definition of the carry, \eqref{eq:carry},
\[
  \sum_{p \in S} v_p\,\frac{N}{p} \;=\; (C+1)N - 1 .
\]
Write $N = mt + 1$, which is what $m \mid N-1$ says. Subtracting $C$ from the
identity gives
\[
  \sum_{p \in S} v_p\,\frac{N}{p} - C
  \;=\; (C+1)N - 1 - C
  \;=\; (C+1)(N-1)
  \;=\; (C+1)\,mt ,
\]
a multiple of $m$. Hence the sum and the carry are congruent modulo $m$.
\end{proof}

The display is four lines and it is numbered for what it does not use. No
primality, no Korselt condition, no self-duality of the cofactors, no property
of the numerators beyond the divisibility that makes $C$ exist. It is a
rewriting of the definition of $C$, and that is why it survives both uses the
section makes of it: on numbers that are not Korselt, where the measurement of
\S\ref{sec:conditional-rate} applies it, and with the numerator set replaced by any other
set of integers of the same shape.

Three refinements are used below, each one step from the display. In the
library's own letters, with $n = \prod_{p \in S} p$ and numerators satisfying
$p \mid v_p\,\lean{cof}(S,p)+1$, the divisibility $N \mid \sum + 1$ is automatic
and the congruence reads $C \equiv \sum_{p \in S} v_p\,\lean{cof}(S,p) \pmod m$
for every $m \mid n-1$ ($\lv$ \lean{carry\_modEq\_sum}); this is the form used
below, because the library's numerators are indexed by the prime support and the
cofactor is the quotient. If $m$ divides every numerator and $m \mid N-1$, then
$m$ divides the whole sum term by term and, by the identity of the proof above,
$m$ divides $C = \sum_{p \in S} v_p\,\lean{cof}(S,p) - (C+1)mt$ ($\lv$
\lean{dvd\_carry\_of\_dvd\_numerators}); the section uses that four times and
never reproves it.

The third refinement is the following congruence, in its inverse form. Suppose every cofactor is self-dual modulo $m$, that is
$\lean{cof}(S,p)^2 \equiv 1 \pmod m$, and let $\varepsilon_p$ invert
$\lean{cof}(S,p)$ modulo $m$. Then $C \equiv \sum_p \varepsilon_p\,v_p \pmod m$
($\lv$ \lean{carry\_modEq\_sum\_inv}). The proof is two congruences and a sum.
From $\varepsilon_p \lean{cof}(S,p) \equiv 1$ and
$\lean{cof}(S,p)^2 \equiv 1$ one gets
$(\varepsilon_p - \lean{cof}(S,p))\lean{cof}(S,p) \equiv 1 - 1 = 0$; the
cofactor is a unit modulo $m$, so $m \mid \varepsilon_p - \lean{cof}(S,p)$;
summing the differences with the weights $v_p$ is a term-by-term divisibility
that passes to the sums ($\lv$ \lean{dvd\_sum\_sub\_sum}), and adding it to the
display gives the inverse form. At $m = 4$ self-duality is a property of parity:
an odd $x$ satisfies $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, since $x = 2k+1$ gives
$x^2 - 1 = 4k(k+1)$ ($\lv$ \lean{odd\_mul\_self\_modEq\_one\_mod\_four}), and
every cofactor of an odd number is odd. So on $N \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ with odd
cofactors one has $C \equiv \sum_p \varepsilon_p\,v_p \pmod 4$ with
$\varepsilon_p$ the inverse of $N/p$ modulo four ($\lv$
\lean{carry\_modEq\_sum\_inv\_four}).

That is the congruence the run had measured, and the measurement is the one
figure this subsection quotes. On all $1\,394\,806$ Carmichael numbers
$N \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ below $10^{18}$ the two sides agree modulo four, with zero
mismatches (\texttt{experiments/results/pw\_carry\_congruence.txt}, field F1;
the range is the census's, the script is
\texttt{experiments/pw\_carry\_congruence.py}). The figure carries no marker: it
is a check on the statement, not a statement, and the theorem above is what the
check is a check of.

One sentence, and it is the only place the book says it. Nothing in this
subsection mentions $G$. The reduction of Part~V and the local theory of Part~VI
decided \emph{which moduli see $N$}; the carry decides \emph{what the numerators
do modulo $m$}, and the two questions meet only through the numerator cosets of
\S\ref{sec:numerators}, which is where \S\ref{sec:conditional-worth} picks the
second one up. The section returns to $G$ once, there, and only to say what the
restriction on $\omega$ is a restriction of.

\subsection{The problem without the no-carry conjunct}
\label{sec:conditional-small}

The miss condition on the Korselt locus, as \S\ref{sec:pw-problem} states it, is
a conjunction: the Egyptian sum takes its extreme value and every numerator is
divisible by four. The first conjunct is redundant at small $\omega$, and the
chain that removes it is six steps, all of them already proved.

The carry is non-negative and at most $\omega - 2$
(\S\ref{sec:korselt}). If $m$ divides every numerator and $m \mid N-1$, then
$m$ divides $C$. Hence if $\omega - 2 < m$, a non-negative multiple of $m$ below
$m$ can only be zero: writing $C = mk$ with $k \ge 1$ would give
$m \le mk = C \le \omega-2 < m$, so $C = 0$ ($\lv$
\lean{carry\_eq\_zero\_of\_dvd\_numerators\_of\_card}). At $m = 4$ and
$\omega \le 5$ the hypothesis $\omega - 2 < 4$ is the bound on the number of
prime factors, and the conclusion is that four divisibilities force the carry to
vanish ($\lv$ \lean{carry\_eq\_zero\_of\_four\_dvd\_numerators\_of\_card\_le\_five}).
And $C = 0$ is exactly $\sum_p v_p\,(N/p) = N-1$ ($\lv$
\lean{carry\_eq\_zero\_iff\_egyptianSum}) --- the identification that makes the
whole subsection possible, and it is \S\ref{sec:korselt}'s.

The hypothesis $N \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ in the fourth step is not a technicality.
For odd $x$ one has $4 \mid N(x)-1$ ($\lv$ \lean{four\_dvd\_N\_sub\_one\_of\_odd},
one line: $N = 2x-1$ with $x = 2u+1$ gives $N-1 = 4u$), and the section states
its results at $x$ odd for that reason. Without the congruence the redundancy
fails, and the failure is measured: below $10^{18}$ there are three Carmichael
numbers with $\omega = 5$ and every numerator divisible by four whose carry is
$2$, and all three are $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$
(\texttt{experiments/results/carry\_mod\_eight.txt}, the line
\texttt{omega=5: 22 numbers, C=0: 19, other carries: \{2: 3\}}); they are
necessarily on that side, since on the other side the fourth step would give
$C = 0$. The count is the script's; the congruence is the statement's.

\begin{definition}[P-W at five prime factors]
\label{def:pw-small}
$\lean{PWProblemSmall}$: for every bound $n_0$ there is a natural number
$n > n_0$ with $n \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, $n$ squarefree, $2 \le \omega(n) \le 5$,
$p-1 \mid n-1$ for every prime $p \mid n$, and integers $v_p$ with
$0 < v_p < p$, $p \mid v_p\,(n/p)+1$ and $4 \mid v_p$ --- and no condition on
the Egyptian sum.

$\lean{KorseltMissInfiniteSmall}$: the same statement at the parameter, with
$N = N(x)$ in place of $n$ and $\lean{AllZero}\,x$ in place of the numerators.
\end{definition}

The two predicates are the ones of \S\ref{sec:pw-problem} with one clause
deleted, and the subsection's whole claim is that the deleted clause was
redundant. So they are not a weakening of P-W but a restatement of it at
$\omega \le 5$: the definition is displayed as a pair, the way the problem was,
and what follows proves that the pair is one statement.

\begin{theorem}[A miss at five prime factors is one congruence]
\label{thm:five-factors}
\lv{} \lean{korselt\_allZero\_iff\_four\_dvd\_of\_card\_le\_five}
Let $x \ge 2$ be odd, put $N = N(x)$, and let $S$ be a finite set of primes
with $\prod_{p \in S} p = N$, $2 \le |S| \le 5$ and $p-1 \mid 2x-2$ for
$p \in S$. Let $v_p \in (0,p)$ be integers with
$p \mid v_p\,\lean{cof}(S,p)+1$. Then
\[
  \lean{AllZero}\,x
  \iff
  4 \mid v_p \ \text{ for every } p \in S .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The criterion's own form of the miss on this locus is \S\ref{sec:pw-problem}'s
Theorem~\ref{thm:pw-nocarry}: $\lean{AllZero}\,x$ holds if and only if
$\sum_{p \in S} v_p\,\lean{cof}(S,p) = N-1$ and $4 \mid v_p$ at every prime.

One direction is that second conjunct, directly.

For the other, assume $4 \mid v_p$ at every $p$. Since $x$ is odd,
$4 \mid N-1$, so the redundancy lemma applies at $m = 4$: the carry vanishes.
By the identification of the chain above, $C = 0$ is exactly
$\sum_{p \in S} v_p\,\lean{cof}(S,p) = N-1$ ($\lv$
\lean{egyptianSum\_eq\_sub\_one\_of\_four\_dvd\_numerators\_of\_card\_le\_five}
is that step with the definitions unfolded). That is the first conjunct.

So the two sides of \S\ref{sec:pw-problem}'s theorem coincide, and the
conjunction is the divisibility alone.
\end{proof}

Two corollaries are read off it and used below. First, a miss at $\omega \le 5$
forces the order condition $(W)$ of \S\ref{sec:korselt}: the carry vanishes, and
$C = 0$ is equivalent to $\lean{AntitoneIdem}$ ($\lv$
\lean{antitoneIdem\_of\_four\_dvd\_numerators\_of\_card\_le\_five}) --- so at
five prime factors the miss event lives on the same antitone-idempotent locus as
the atom sum and the closed forms, which is where the book has been since
Part~VI. Second, the criterion's own form of the problem at $\omega \le 5$ keeps
only the numerator condition: $\lean{AllZero}\,x$ holds if and only if there are
integers $v_p \in (0,p)$ with $p \mid v_p\,\lean{cof}(S,p)+1$ and $4 \mid v_p$
at every prime ($\lv$ \lean{allZero\_iff\_pw\_reduced\_at\_card\_le\_five}),
which is \S\ref{sec:pw-problem}'s arithmetic condition with the Egyptian-sum
conjunct deleted. That is the form in which the problem is one congruence.

\begin{theorem}[The two small forms are one statement]
\label{thm:small-iff}
\lv{} \lean{pwProblemSmall\_iff}
$\lean{PWProblemSmall}$ holds if and only if
$\lean{KorseltMissInfiniteSmall}$ does.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For the forward direction, let $n$ be a small-form member beyond the bound
$n_0$. It is $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$, so $n \ne 0$; it is at least $6$: a squarefree
 number with at least two prime factors has at least
two distinct primes, and the smallest pair of distinct primes is $2$ and $3$. So
$x = (n+1)/2$ is a natural number with $N(x) = 2x-1 = n$; this is the only place
in the section where the parameter and the number are interchanged, and it is
why the second predicate is stated at $x$ while the first is stated at $n$.

The six arithmetic clauses transfer along $N(x) = n$: the bound $n > n_0$, the
class modulo four, squarefreeness, the two bounds on $\omega(n)$, and Korselt's
condition $p-1 \mid n-1$.

For the last clause, apply the reduced form of the criterion at that $x$, in the
direction from the numerators to the pass, with the numerators the definition
provides; its hypotheses are the five clauses just transported, and its
conclusion is $\lean{AllZero}\,x$.

For the converse, let $x$ satisfy the second predicate beyond $n_0$, and put
$n = N(x)$. The same five clauses transfer the other way, and the reduced form
of the criterion, read in the other direction, produces the numerators. Hence
each predicate implies the other, and they differ by nothing but the letter of
the parameter.
\end{proof}

Three corollaries, each carrying its hypothesis in the signature, and the
section says so in each case: the mark is $\lvCond$ because the
\emph{statement} assumes the small problem, not because a proof has a gap.

First, the small form implies the full one ($\lvCond$ \lean{pwProblem\_of\_small}):
through \S\ref{sec:pw-problem}'s identification of the problem with the question
and Theorem~\ref{thm:small-iff}, a small miss is a miss. Second, it delivers the
same conclusion as the full problem from a strictly simpler hypothesis: for
every bound $n_0$ there is $x \ge 2$ with $N(x) > n_0$, $N(x)$ composite and
$\lean{AllZero}\,x$ ($\lvCond$
\lean{infinitely\_many\_counterexamples\_of\_pw\_small}, which is
\S\ref{sec:pw-hypothesis}'s corollary applied to the first). Third --- and this
is the section's strongest statement about P-W --- the $(1,2,5,10)$ family
composed with Theorem~\ref{thm:small-iff} gives
$\lean{KorseltMissInfiniteSmall}$ with Dickson's conjecture as the only
hypothesis ($\lvCond$ \lean{korseltMissInfiniteSmall\_of\_dickson\_1\_2\_5\_10}):
infinitely many small misses, outright. It is a composition of two results the
reader already has, one from \S\ref{sec:conditional-families} and one from this
subsection, and it is the reason the families were run into the small problem
rather than left in the general layer.

The restriction is not vacuous and it is not automatic, and one number shows
both. Let $N = 188461 = 7\cdot 13\cdot 19\cdot 109$, the Korselt number of
\S\ref{sec:korselt} whose invariant is $1$. Its numerators, in the order
$7, 13, 19, 109$, are $6, 6, 18, 80$ and its carry is $2$
(\texttt{experiments/results/carmichael\_numerator\_gcd.txt}, the row
\texttt{188461 [7, 13, 19, 109] v=[6, 6, 18, 80] carry=2 G=1}); three of the
four are not divisible by four and the last is. So $188461$ is not a miss, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:five-factors} is what says so: the theorem is an equivalence, and
this is the number on its false side at $\omega = 4$. Its role in the book is
elsewhere --- it is the instance that corrected the atom formula --- and here it
is doing something else, which is what a running example is for. That the
restriction is not vacuous is a measurement with a range: below $10^{18}$ every
Korselt miss has $\omega \le 5$, all $1266$ of them, and none has six or more
prime factors
(\texttt{experiments/results/pw\_carry\_congruence.txt}, field F3: the counts by
$\omega$ are $1148$, $99$, $19$, $0$ and then zero again). The figure carries no
marker; it is the census's, and \S\ref{sec:conditional-rate} is where the census
is read properly.

\subsection{What the hypothesis of the small problem is worth}
\label{sec:conditional-worth}

The small problem is a hypothesis in everything above, and it is worth saying
exactly what it assumes --- not what it would imply about the criterion, which
was the last subsection, but what it asserts about Carmichael numbers, which is
what a reader sceptical of the reduction will want to see. Three statements, all
unconditional, do that; they are the reason the section can call the restriction
a restriction and not a reformulation into something easier.

The first is that the lower bound $\omega \ge 2$ in the definition is really
$\omega \ge 3$, and the reason is Korselt's condition alone. A squarefree number
with exactly two prime factors is never Korselt: if $n = pq$ then
$p-1 \mid n-1 = (p-1)q + (q-1)$ forces $p-1 \mid q-1$, and symmetrically
$q-1 \mid p-1$, so $p = q$ and $n$ is not squarefree with two factors ($\lv$
\lean{not\_korselt\_of\_card\_two}). Consequently a squarefree Korselt number
with at least two prime factors has at least three ($\lv$
\lean{card\_ge\_three\_of\_korselt}), and the $\omega = 2$ branch of the small
problem is empty: no bound admits a member with exactly two prime factors
($\lv$ \lean{not\_pwSmall\_card\_two}). The problem can therefore be stated at
$3 \le \omega \le 5$ with nothing lost:

$\lean{SmallMissData}(n)$ is the arithmetic content of a single $n$ --- the class
modulo four, squarefreeness, Korselt, and numerators $v_p \in (0,p)$ with
$p \mid v_p\,(n/p)+1$ and $4 \mid v_p$ at every prime,
without the Egyptian sum --- and $\lean{PWProblemSmall}$ is the statement that
for every bound there is a larger $n$ with $2 \le \omega(n) \le 5$ and
$\lean{SmallMissData}(n)$ ($\lv$ \lean{pwProblemSmall\_iff\_data}); the same with
$3 \le \omega(n) \le 5$ is the same statement ($\lv$
\lean{pwProblemSmall\_iff\_data\_three}), by the two results of the paragraph
above.

The second is that the restricted problem splits at fixed $\omega$, and this is
a statement about infinitude rather than about members. The negation of ``for
every bound there is a larger member'' is ``all members beyond some bound fail''
($\lv$ \lean{not\_infinite\_iff\_bounded}, a two-line equivalence), and
$3 \le \omega \le 5$ is a finite union. So if the small problem holds, one of
its three fixed-$\omega$ halves holds: there is a $k \in \{3,4,5\}$ for which
infinitely many squarefree Korselt $n \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ with exactly $k$ prime
factors carry $\lean{SmallMissData}$ ($\lvCond$
\lean{exists\_fixed\_card\_of\_pwProblemSmall}). Each of the three disjuncts
requires infinitely many Carmichael numbers with exactly $k$ prime factors, and
that is not a statement the published constructions provide.

The third makes the last clause exact. A Carmichael number is $1 < n$,
composite, squarefree and Korselt ($\lean{IsCarmichael}$); being composite and
squarefree it has at least two prime factors, and with at least two it has at
least three, the case of two being excluded by the theorem above ($\lv$
\lean{card\_ge\_three\_of\_isCarmichael}).
Write $\lean{CarmichaelsSmallOneFour}$ for the statement that infinitely many
Carmichael numbers $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$ have $3 \le \omega \le 5$. Then the small
problem implies it ($\lvCond$
\lean{carmichaelsSmallOneFour\_of\_pwProblemSmall}): a small miss is a Korselt
number with $\omega$ between three and five, hence a Carmichael number in that
class, and the bound of the problem makes the infinitude. This is the
unconditional content of the hypothesis, and no theorem of
Alford--Granville--Pomerance \cite{AGP1994}, Wright \cite{Wright2013} or
Matom\"aki \cite{Matomaki2013Carmichael} type supplies it: the constructions
behind those counts produce Carmichael numbers whose number of prime factors
grows, whereas the hypothesis asks for infinitely many below six prime factors.

The link with $G$ is cited from \S\ref{sec:korselt} (Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion-four}). On a squarefree Korselt $N \equiv 1 \pmod 4$
the criterion misses if and only if $4 \mid G(N)$, with no bound on $\omega$
($\lv$ \lean{allZero\_iff\_four\_dvd\_residueGcd}, the identification of
\S\ref{sec:korselt} in the prime-factor language). Consequently P-W is the
statement that infinitely many squarefree Korselt $N \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ with
$\omega \ge 2$ have $4 \mid G(N)$ ($\lv$ \lean{pwProblem\_iff\_residueGcd}), and
the small problem is the same statement with $\omega \le 5$ added and nothing
else changed ($\lv$ \lean{pwProblemSmall\_iff\_residueGcd}; the two differ by
the bound on $\omega$ and by no other clause, $\lv$
\lean{pwProblem\_iff\_residueGcd\_without\_card}). Nothing is re-derived here:
the equivalence is Part~V's, the letters are \S\ref{sec:korselt}'s, and the
paragraph exists so that a reader who thinks in the invariant can see that the
restriction of this section is a restriction on $\omega$ and not a change of
problem.

\subsection{Why the reduction stops at six prime factors}
\label{sec:conditional-ceiling}

The redundancy of \S\ref{sec:conditional-small} is not an accident of the number
four. It is the first case of a bound, and the bound is sharp at the second.

The general statement is about a divisor of the numerators. Call $A$ an
\emph{atom-gcd} of the numerator set when $\lean{AtomGcdDvd}$ holds: $A$ divides
$N-1$ and divides $e-1$ for every one-prime atom $e$ of \S\ref{sec:korselt}.
Under a nonzero carry such an $A$ is at most $\omega - 2$ ($\lv$
\lean{atomGcd\_le\_card\_sub\_two}: $A \mid C$ by
$\lean{atomGcd\_dvd\_carry}$, $0 < C$ by non-negativity, and
$C \le \omega-2$); and for a positive $A$ the $2$-adic part $2^{v_2(A)}$ divides $A$ ($\lv$
\lean{two\_pow\_padicValNat\_le}, one line). Hence
\[
  2^{v_2(A)} \;\le\; A \;\le\; \omega - 2
  \qquad
  (\lv\ \lean{two\_pow\_v2\_atomGcd\_le}),
\]
and in particular $2^k \mid A$ forces $\omega \ge 2^k + 2$ ($\lv$
\lean{card\_ge\_of\_two\_pow\_dvd}). The carry is bounded by the number of prime
factors; a power of two dividing the atom-gcd is bounded by the carry; and the
two together make the number of prime factors grow with the power of two.

At $k = 2$ this reads: under a nonzero carry, $4 \mid A$ forces $\omega \ge 6$
($\lv$ \lean{card\_ge\_six\_of\_four\_dvd}). And the other direction at
$\omega \le 9$ closes the gap: if $4 \mid v_p$ at every prime then $4 \mid C$,
while $0 \le C \le \omega-2 \le 7$, so the carry is $0$ or $4$ ($\lv$
\lean{carry\_eq\_zero\_or\_four\_of\_four\_dvd\_numerators\_of\_card\_le\_nine}).
Together the two say that the $\omega \le 5$ redundancy of \S\ref{sec:conditional-small}
is the last one of its kind: at $\omega = 6$ a miss still needs $C = 0$, while a
number with $4 \mid v_p$ everywhere and $C = 4$ is a non-miss. The redundancy
does move one scale up --- at $\omega \le 9$ with $8 \mid N-1$ and $8 \mid v_p$
at every prime the carry is again $0$ ($\lv$
\lean{carry\_eq\_zero\_of\_eight\_dvd\_numerators\_of\_card\_le\_nine}) --- but
the hypothesis there is strictly stronger, and the number of the next display
fails it at one prime, so the $m = 8$ form sharpens the statement without
removing the ceiling.

\begin{theorem}[The ceiling at six prime factors]
\label{thm:six-factors}
\lv{} \lean{omega6\_instance}
Let $N_6 = 9\,505\,981\,178\,881\,201 = 7\cdot 241\cdot 283\cdot 1327\cdot 1873\cdot 8011$,
let $S_6$ be its prime support, $v_6$ the numerators
$4, 208, 220, 1176, 1704, 7948$ at those primes, and $e_6$ the atoms
$(p - v_6(p))\,(N_6/p)$. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\text{each } p \in S_6 \text{ is prime}, \qquad
  \prod_{p \in S_6} p = N_6, \qquad |S_6| = 6, \\
&p - 1 \mid N_6 - 1 \ \ (p \in S_6), \qquad
  C = 4, \qquad \sum_{p \in S_6} e_6(p) = N_6 + 1, \\
&\lean{AtomGcdDvd}\,S_6\,N_6\,4, \qquad
  N_6 \equiv 1 \pmod 4, \qquad
  \neg\,\lean{AntitoneIdem}\,S_6\,N_6 .
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The number, its support, the numerators and the atoms are the definitions
$\lean{N6}$, $\lean{S6}$, $\lean{v6}$ and $\lean{e6}$; the six factors are prime ($\lv$ \lean{primes\_N6},
each small enough for a finite check), their product is $N_6$ ($\lv$
\lean{prod\_N6}) and there are six of them ($\lv$ \lean{card\_S6}).

Korselt: $p-1 \mid N_6-1$ at each of the six ($\lv$ \lean{korselt\_N6}). So the
number is a Carmichael number, and the criterion's own locus is where the ceiling
is attained.

The numerators lie in $(0,p)$ at every prime ($\lv$ \lean{hv6}, $\lv$
\lean{hvlt6}) and satisfy $p \mid v_6(p)\,(N_6/p)+1$ ($\lv$ \lean{hvp6}); all
six are divisible by four by inspection, which is the hypothesis of the
subsection. The atoms are pinned by the CRT: any one-prime atom of $N_6$ equals
$N_6 - v_6(p)\,(N_6/p)$ ($\lv$ \lean{atom\_N6\_eq}, from
$\lean{atom\_eq\_sub\_mul}$ of \S\ref{sec:korselt}), and $e_6$ is that value
($\lv$ \lean{e6\_eq}).

The carry is $4$, and $4 = \omega - 2$ ($\lv$ \lean{carry\_N6}); so the atom sum
takes the value $(6-1-4)N_6 + 1 = N_6+1$ ($\lv$ \lean{sum\_atoms\_N6}, which is
\S\ref{sec:korselt}'s exact atom sum restated at $\omega = 6$ and $C = 4$,
$\lv$ \lean{sum\_atom\_eq\_n\_mul}). The atom-gcd is $4$: it divides $N_6-1$ and
each $e_6(p)-1$ ($\lv$ \lean{atomGcdDvd\_N6}); the number is $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$
($\lv$ \lean{N6\_mod\_four}); and the order condition $(W)$ fails ($\lv$
\lean{not\_antitoneIdem\_N6}), because $(W)$ would force the carry to vanish and
the carry is $4$.

The nine clauses together are the display. And the bound of the previous
paragraph is attained, not merely approached: the instance satisfies its
hypotheses and has $\omega = 6 = 2^2+2$ exactly ($\lv$
\lean{omega6\_attains\_bound}), so the ceiling is sharp.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The carry-four number at six prime factors]
\label{ex:omega6}
$N = 9\,505\,981\,178\,881\,201$ is the smallest of the $28$ rows of the
census below $10^{21}$ that have $\omega = 6$, every numerator divisible by four
and carry $4$
(\texttt{experiments/results/pw\_omega6\_f1\_list.txt}, whose first row it is;
the factorisation is the c21 census \cite{Pinch2007}). Its numerators at
$7, 241, 283, 1327, 1873, 8011$ are $4, 208, 220, 1176, 1704, 7948$, each in
$(0,p)$, and its carry is $4 = \omega-2$, so its atom sum is $N+1$ rather than
the $5N+1$ a vanishing carry would give. It is doing three things at once:
it is the case where the two conjuncts of the miss condition separate, it
attains the bound $\omega \ge 2^2+2$ of \S\ref{sec:conditional-ceiling} with
equality, and it is a Carmichael number, so the ceiling is attained on the
criterion's own locus and not off it. It also fails the $m = 8$ hypothesis of the
paragraph above at $p = 283$, since $220 \equiv 4 \pmod 8$.
\end{example}

Two statements of the same module make the separation exact, and they are worth
naming because one of them is the ceiling in the criterion's own letters. First,
the $\omega = 6$ analogue of Theorem~\ref{thm:five-factors} fails: there are a
set of six primes, a product $n$ and numerators in $(0,p)$ with
$p \mid v_p\,\lean{cof}(S,p)+1$ and $4 \mid v_p$ everywhere, whose Egyptian sum
is \emph{not} $n-1$ ($\lv$ \lean{card\_le\_six\_analogue\_fails}). So the
hypothesis $\omega \le 5$ of the small problem cannot be raised to $\omega \le 6$
in the numerator-only formulation without losing the equivalence with the miss
event. Second, at the number of the example the criterion \emph{detects}: with
$x_6 = \lean{x6} = (N+1)/2 = 4\,752\,990\,589\,440\,601$, so that
$N(x_6) = N_6$ ($\lv$ \lean{N\_x6}), one has
$\neg\,\lean{AllZero}\,x_6$ ($\lv$ \lean{not\_allZero\_x6}), although every
numerator of $N_6$ is divisible by four. A carry-four number is a
non-miss with the numerator condition of a miss, and that is the whole content of
the ceiling: at $\omega \le 5$ the two conditions collapse into one, at
$\omega = 6$ they separate, and the number where they separate is a Carmichael
number. The small problem implies P-W.

\subsection{The last numerator is forced, and the rate it predicts}
\label{sec:conditional-rate}

A miss at $\omega \le 5$ is a conjunction of $\omega$ divisibilities, but on the
no-carry locus it is one divisibility and $\omega-1$ consequences of it. The
mechanism is a lemma about sums, and the section states it because it is what
turns the census of this subsection from a list into a rate.

On the no-carry locus, $\sum_{p \in S} v_p\,\lean{cof}(S,p) = n-1$ with
$4 \mid n-1$. Suppose $\omega - 1$ of the numerators are divisible by four and
let $p_0$ be the remaining prime. Then $4 \mid n-1$ gives $4$ divides the sum;
the summand at $p_0$ is the sum minus the summands at the other primes, each of
which is divisible by four, so $4 \mid v_{p_0}\,\lean{cof}(S,p_0)$ ($\lv$
\lean{dvd\_of\_dvd\_sum\_of\_dvd\_all\_but\_one}, which needs only that the
cofactor at $p_0$ be coprime to the modulus); and the cofactor at $p_0$ is odd,
$n$ and every prime being odd, so it is a unit modulo four --- if
$\lean{cof}(S,p_0) = 2k+1$ then
$\lean{cof}(S,p_0)^2 - 4k(k+1) = 1$, a Bezout identity and no inversion to
compute. Hence $4 \mid v_{p_0}$ ($\lv$
\lean{four\_dvd\_of\_dvd\_sum\_sub\_one\_all\_but\_one}). The event
``$4 \mid v_p$ at every prime'' is therefore one congruence and not $\omega$
independent ones, which is why the congruence contributes $4^{1-\omega}$ and not
$4^{-\omega}$, and why the predicted density carries one factorial and not
$\omega$ of them.

What the census says about that prediction is a measurement with a range and a
script, in four sentences, and no figure in it carries a marker. First the
shape: below $10^{18}$, among Carmichael numbers $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$, the counts
of misses by $\omega$ are $1148$, $99$, $19$ and $0$ for $\omega = 3, 4, 5, 6$,
against $32\,749$, $40\,685$, $175\,417$ and $414\,660$ Carmichael numbers in
those classes; the prediction $4^{1-\omega}/(\omega-1)!$ applied to those
denominators gives $1023$, $106$, $29$ and $3$
(\texttt{experiments/results/pw\_carry\_congruence.txt}, field F3; the range is
$10^{18}$, the script \texttt{experiments/pw\_carry\_congruence.py}). Second the
split at six: with $4 \mid v_p$ everywhere the carry below $10^{18}$ is $0$ at
$\omega = 3$ (all $1148$ numbers) and at $\omega = 4$ (all $99$); at
$\omega = 5$ it is $0$ for $19$ numbers and $2$ for the three numbers
$\equiv 3 \pmod 4$ of \S\ref{sec:conditional-small}; and at $\omega = 6, 7, 8$
it is $4$ throughout, for $3$, $12$ and $2$ numbers respectively --- one carry
value per row and no exception
(\texttt{experiments/results/carry\_mod\_eight.txt}, the rows
\texttt{omega=6}, \texttt{omega=7}, \texttt{omega=8}). Third the census above
the range: Pinch's list of the $20\,138\,200$ Carmichael numbers below $10^{21}$
\cite{Pinch2007} contains $7\,648$ P-W members
(\texttt{experiments/results/pw\_survival\_bh.txt}, the full sample, its
parameter \texttt{PMAX} fixed at $10^7$; the dataset is the one whose hash is
\texttt{experiments/results/pw\_c21.sha256}). Fourth, these are
consistency checks on a model whose shape was fixed before the data were examined
(\texttt{experiments/results/pw\_prereg.txt}); the analysis is
\texttt{experiments/results/pw\_prereg\_analysis.txt}, and the summary there is
$13$ cells in $(10^{18}, 10^{21}]$ and two models, the first inside its predicted
interval in $11$ of the $13$. No figure in the four sentences is promoted to a
theorem; the only theorem in this neighbourhood is the next subsection's, and it
is about the model and not about the primes.

\subsection{The coupling bound}
\label{sec:conditional-grid}

The model behind those counts is a statement about boxes, and it has one theorem
in it. Its subject is the relation between two ways of reading a tuple of
fractions: the \emph{weight box} $\{1,\dots,q_i-1\}^r$, in which every coordinate
carries its own denominator, and the \emph{grid} $\{1,\dots,L\}^r$, in which all
of them share one. The theorem says the grid is a faithful model of the box for
the carry statistic, with an error the section can write down.

Name the objects first. For $m, L \in \N$ and $k \in \N$,
$\lean{coupled}(m,L,k) = \lceil mk/L \rceil$ is a natural number; $\lean{box}(r,L)$
is $\{1,\dots,L\}^r$; $\lean{vbox}(q)$ is $\{1,\dots,q_i-1\}^r$;
$\lean{jbox}(q,L)$ is $\{1,\dots,L/(q_i-1)\}^r$; the coordinatewise coupling is
$\lean{coupledTuple}(q,L,k)_i = \lean{coupled}(q_i-1,L,k_i)$; and
$\lean{residue}(m,L,k)$ is the index of $k$ inside the fibre of its coupled
weight. The hypothesis on the moduli is $q_i \ge 2$ with $q_i - 1 \mid L$, so
$L$ is a common multiple of the $q_i-1$. The two counts are
$\lean{carryCount}(q,c)$, the weight tuples whose coordinate sum of fractions
has floor $c$, and $\lean{gridCount}(r,L,c)$, the grid tuples whose sum has
floor $c$; the coordinate sum on a grid tuple is $\lean{sumQ}$.

The proof has eight steps, and the first two control the rounding.
$\lv$ \lean{coupled\_bounds} brackets the ceiling: for $L > 0$,
$L\lfloor a/L\rfloor \le a$ and $a < L\lfloor a/L\rfloor + L$ at
$a = mk + (L-1)$, which is the division algorithm written twice. Its
consequences are that $mk \le L\,\lean{coupled}(m,L,k)$ ($\lv$
\lean{coupled\_le\_mul}) and that the coupling lands in $\{1,\dots,m\}$ when
$m \ge 1$, $1 \le k \le L$ and $m \mid L$ ($\lv$ \lean{coupled\_mem}); so a coupled tuple
of a grid tuple is a weight tuple ($\lv$ \lean{coupledTuple\_mem}).

The third step is that the fibres are exact. Inside the fibre of its weight, $k$
is bracketed by $\lv$ \lean{fiber\_lo} and $\lv$ \lean{fiber\_hi}; the index lies in
$\{1,\dots,L/m\}$ ($\lv$ \lean{residue\_mem}); every $k$ is reconstructed from
its weight and its index, $k = (\lean{coupled}-1)(L/m) + \lean{residue}$ ($\lv$
\lean{residue\_reconstruct}); and the reconstruction is inverted by the coupling
($\lv$ \lean{coupled\_of\_reconstruct}). Consequently the index tuple of a grid
tuple is a tuple of indices ($\lv$ \lean{residueTuple\_mem}) and the tuple is
recovered from its coupled tuple and its index tuple ($\lv$
\lean{tuple\_reconstruct}).

The fourth step is the count on a fibre. For any predicate $P$ on weight tuples,
the grid tuples whose coupled weight satisfies $P$ number the $P$-filtered weight
tuples times $\prod_i L/(q_i-1)$ ($\lv$ \lean{card\_coupled\_fibre}); the proof
is the bijection $k \mapsto \bigl(\lean{coupledTuple}(q,L,k),\ (\lean{residue}(q_i - 1, L, k_i))_i\bigr)$
onto the product of the weight box with the box of indices, whose inverse is the
reconstruction of the third step.

The fifth step is the pointwise distance. For $1 \le k \le L$ and $m > 0$,
\[
  \left| \frac{\lceil mk/L\rceil}{m+1} - \frac{k}{L} \right| \;\le\; \frac{1}{m+1}
  \qquad
  (\lv\ \lean{coupled\_dist});
\]
with $m = q_i-1$, so that $m+1 = q_i$, this reads
$\lvert v_i/q_i - k_i/L \rvert \le 1/q_i$ coordinate by coordinate, and summing
the $r$ inequalities by the triangle inequality gives
$\lvert \sum_i v_i/q_i - \sum_i k_i/L \rvert \le \varepsilon$ with
$\varepsilon = \sum_i 1/q_i$ ($\lv$ \lean{coupled\_sum\_dist}).

The sixth step says where the two floors can disagree. If $\lfloor x\rfloor = c$
and $\lfloor y\rfloor \ne c$ while $\lvert x - y\rvert \le \varepsilon$, then
$y$ lies within $\varepsilon$ of $c$ or within $\varepsilon$ of $c+1$: there are
only two sides on which $y$ can leave the unit interval containing $x$ ($\lv$
\lean{floor\_ne\_mem\_windows}).

The seventh step bounds each of the two windows. A window of half-width
$\varepsilon$ around a centre holds at most $(2\varepsilon L + 1)L^{r-1}$ grid
points: slice on the last coordinate, whose fibre is an interval of integers, and
bound that interval by its length plus one ($\lv$ \lean{card\_Icc\_filter\_le},
$\lv$ \lean{card\_window\_le}).

The eighth step adds the two windows and normalises. The two counts differ by at
most the number of grid points in the union of the two windows, which is at most
$(4\varepsilon L + 2)L^{r-1}$; dividing by $L^r = L^{r-1}L$ gives
$4\varepsilon + 2/L$; and the count on the coupled side is converted to
$\lean{carryCount}(q,c)/\prod_i (q_i-1)$ by the fibre count of the fourth step
and the identity $\prod_i \bigl(L/(q_i-1)\bigr)\cdot\prod_i (q_i-1) = L^r$.

\begin{theorem}[The grid models the weight box]
\label{thm:carry-grid}
\lv{} \lean{carryGrid\_dist}
Let $r \ge 1$, $L \ge 1$ and $q : \mathrm{Fin}\,r \to \N$ with $q_i \ge 2$ and
$q_i - 1 \mid L$ for every $i$. Then for every $c \in \N$
\[
  \left|
    \frac{\lean{carryCount}(q,c)}{\prod_i (q_i-1)}
    -
    \frac{\lean{gridCount}(r,L,c)}{L^r}
  \right|
  \;\le\;
  4\sum_i \frac{1}{q_i} + \frac{2}{L} .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\varepsilon = \sum_i 1/q_i \ge 0$. The coupled count on the grid side is,
by the fourth step applied to the predicate whose floor is $c$,
\[
  A \;=\; \lean{carryCount}(q,c)\,\prod_i \frac{L}{q_i-1} ,
\]
and the grid count is $B = \lean{gridCount}(r,L,c)$ by definition.

The two sets differ only on tuples whose coupled floor is $c$ and whose own
floor is not, or the other way round. On such a tuple the two coordinate sums
are within $\varepsilon$ by the fifth step, so by the sixth step the tuple lies
in one of the two windows of the seventh step; hence
$\lvert A - B\rvert$ is at most the number of grid points in the union of the two
windows, which is at most $2(2\varepsilon L+1)L^{r-1}$.

Divide by $L^r = L^{r-1}L$: the quotient is at most $(4\varepsilon L + 2)/L =
4\varepsilon + 2/L$. What is divided is $\lvert A - B\rvert$, and $A/L^r$ is the
first term of the display by the normalisation
$\prod_i \bigl(L/(q_i-1)\bigr)\prod_i(q_i-1) = L^r$ of the fourth step; so the
display follows.
\end{proof}

The bound is $O(\sum_i 1/q_i + 1/L)$: for fixed $r$ and large moduli the grid is
a faithful model of the weight box to within a sum of reciprocals, which is the
sense in which the two counts agree. What the theorem does
\emph{not} say is that either count converges to the Eulerian numbers. The
passage from the grid count to the Eulerian law is not this theorem, it is not
proved anywhere in this development, and the census of \S\ref{sec:conditional-rate}
is a measurement and not a corollary of this display. The theorem is the one
piece of mathematics inside the heuristic, and it is about boxes; the reader who
wants the law should read the measurement as a measurement.

\subsection{Where the book stops}
\label{sec:conditional-stop}

P-W is now a statement about one congruence on a locus. At $\omega \le 5$ it is
$4 \mid v_p$ at every prime and nothing else: the no-carry conjunct is redundant
(Theorem~\ref{thm:five-factors}), the two forms of the restricted problem are one
statement (Theorem~\ref{thm:small-iff}), and the restriction is a restriction on
$\omega$ and not a change of problem (the $G$-form of the two, at the end of
\S\ref{sec:conditional-worth}). At $\omega \ge 6$ it is the
same congruence together with $C = 0$, the two are no longer redundant, and the
 number of
the ceiling is a Carmichael number where they separate
(Theorem~\ref{thm:six-factors}).

The conditional layer is a hypothesis and its count is a shape. Theorem~\ref{thm:bh-count}
gives a bound of the form $c'X^{1/4}/(\log X)^4$, whose factor is $0.011$ at $10^{18}$ and $1.39$ at
$10^{30}$, while the census stops at $10^{21}$ with $7\,648$ members in it, and
the grid bound says only that the model behind the census's shape is faithful to
within a sum of reciprocals.

The problem can be stated exactly, and it is not settled here. The open questions of \S\ref{sec:open} include this one, in
the sharpest form it has here --- the small problem of
\S\ref{sec:conditional-small} --- and none of them is answered: the condition is a congruence on the numerators of
a Korselt number with at most five prime factors, and whether infinitely many
such numbers exist is not a question this book can settle. That is where it stops.

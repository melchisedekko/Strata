A NUMBER THAT DECIDES A FAMILY OF TESTS
ALESSANDRO SGARBI
Abstract. For an odd integer N ≥ 3 let G(N ) be the greatest common divisor of r − 1 over the nonzero
Fermat residues r = aN −1 mod N , where a runs over all of Z/N Z, units and non-units alike (a gcd of zeros
being 0). Reading the Fermat residue modulo a fixed M does not separate N from the primes exactly
when M divides G(N ), so this one number decides a whole family of tests. We show that G(N ) = 0
exactly when N is prime, that at a composite it is positive and divides N (N − 1), and that G(pk ) = p for
k ≥ 2 and G(N ) = 1 on every other non-squarefree N . On squarefree N the invariant has a closed form
in the CRT numerators of N , the integers 0 < vp < p with p | vp N/p
∑︁ + 1: let the coset of vp be the set of
values vp bN −1 mod p over the integers b; then G(N ) is 1 unless p vp N/p = N − 1 and each vp is the
largest element of its coset, and in that case it is the gcd of all∑︁the elements of the cosets. On Carmichael
numbers each coset is {0, vp }, and G(N ) = gcdp vp whenever p vp N/p = N − 1. Finally G(N )2 ≤ N , so
the least modulus that detects a composite N is at most log2 N + 2. By direct computation, the smallest
odd composite with G(N ) > 1 that is not a prime power is 6601, with G(6601) = 2. The results are
formally verified in Lean 4 and Mathlib. None of this yields a practical primality test: the tests that G
decides read all N bases.

1. Introduction
Fermat’s congruence says that ap−1 mod p is 0 or 1 for every a when p is prime. Read
over all residues a of an odd composite N , units and non-units alike, the Fermat residue
r = aN −1 mod N usually takes other values, and a coarse reading of r already sees them.
Fix a modulus M and call N detected modulo M if some nonzero Fermat residue of N
is not congruent to 1 modulo M . At M = 2 this asks only for one even nonzero residue,
and it is enough for every odd composite below 6601, Carmichael numbers included: the
residues of 561 = 3 · 11 · 17 are 0, 1, 34, 154, 187, 375, 408, 528, and 34 is even. The residues
of 6601 = 7 · 23 · 41 are
0, 1, 575, 2255, 2829, 3773, 4347, 6027,
all odd but 0, so the test modulo 2 does not detect 6601. Below 6 · 104 it is the only odd
composite with this property (Remark 2.21).
The reason is a single number. For odd N ≥ 3 let G(N ) be the greatest common divisor
of r−1 over the nonzero Fermat residues r of N (Definition 2.1). A modulus M divides G(N )
exactly when every Fermat residue of N is 0 or congruent to 1 modulo M (Theorem 2.3),
so the test modulo M does not detect N exactly when M | G(N ) (Theorem 8.12): one
number decides the whole family of tests, and G(6601) = gcd(6600, 574, 2254, . . . ) = 2.
The equivalence holds by construction — the divisors of a gcd are the common divisors of
its terms — and it is stated as a theorem only because it is what turns the family of tests
into one number. The content of this paper is the computation and the bounds of G(N ).
Results. The invariant is 0 exactly on the primes (Theorem 2.7); on an odd composite it
is positive and divides N (N − 1) (Theorem 2.14); it equals p at a proper prime power pk
(Theorem 2.10) and 1 at every other non-squarefree N (Theorem 2.18). On squarefree N
everything is read from the integers 0 < vp < p with p | vp (N/p) + 1, one for each prime
p | N∑︁
, which we call the CRT numerators of N (Definition 3.10). Their weighted sum
Σ = p vp N/p is always −1 modulo N , so Σ + 1 = (C + 1)N for an integer C ≥ 0, the
carry (Definition 3.13); the sum carries when C > 0, that is, when Σ > N − 1.

What is classical and what is not. The CRT idempotents of Z/N Z, and Euler–Fermat

numerators vp , not the gcd(N, x) or gcd(N, e) of an element or an idempotent that the
literature uses.1 What we claim is the invariant G(N ) and the equivalence that ties it to
the tests, the exact value of the idempotent sum, the closed forms in numerators on every
squarefree N , and the bound G(N )2 ≤ N .
Two limits should be stated at the start. G is not a practical primality test: every
test it decides reads all N bases, at Θ(N ) modular exponentiations, and knowing that
M ∤ G(N ) does not tell which base detects N . And detecting Carmichael numbers is no
sign of depth here: it is the non-unit bases that detect them, and any test that reads the
non-units shares that property.

2. The invariant
This section defines the invariant, which reads only the Fermat residues r = r(N, m)
over all bases m ∈ [1, N ] — units and non-units alike. Among other things it proves that
G(N ) = 0 exactly for primes, that G(N ) always divides N (N − 1), and that G(pk ) = p.
2.1. The invariant and the criterion. We write r(N, m) for the Fermat residue of the
base m modulo N , the remainder of (m − 1)N −1 on division by N , and R for the set of
residues so attained as m runs over [1, N ]. Since m − 1 runs over [0, N ) exactly once,
R is the set of all residues aN −1 mod N : the bases are read over all of Z/N Z, units and
non-units alike, at the one exponent Fermat’s congruence is about.

Suppose first that M | G(N ), and fix m in range. If rm = 0 the first alternative holds.
Otherwise tm = |rm − 1| is divisible by M , hence so is rm − 1, which is the congruence
rm ≡ 1 (mod M ).

2.2. Primality is zero.
Theorem 2.6 (residueGcd_pos). Let N ≥ 3 be an odd composite. Then G(N ) > 0. [L]
Proof. By Proposition 2.4 there is a base m ∈ [1, N ] whose residue r is neither 0 nor 1.
Suppose G(N ) = 0. A gcd vanishes exactly when every term of its family vanishes, so
tm = 0 for this m. Since r ̸= 0, the term is |r − 1| = 0, that is, r = 1 — against the choice
of m. Hence G(N ) ̸= 0.
□

2.3. Prime powers: G(pk ) = p.
Theorem 2.8 (prime_pow_dvd_residueGcd). Let N ≥ 3 be odd and let N = pk with p
prime and k ≥ 1. Then p | G(N ). [L]
Proof. Here N − 1 = pk − 1, which is at least k. By Theorem 2.3 at M = p it suffices to
show that every attainable residue is 0 or 1 modulo p.
If p | m − 1 then pk | (m − 1)N −1 because the exponent N − 1 is at least k, so rm = 0. If
p ∤ m−1, then m−1 is a unit modulo the prime p and (m−1)p−1 ≡ 1 (mod p) by Fermat’s
congruence. Since p ≡ 1 (mod p − 1) we have p − 1 | pk − 1 = N − 1; raising the congruence
to the quotient (pk − 1)/(p − 1) gives (m − 1)N −1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Finally p | N , so reducing
modulo N does not change the value modulo p and rm ≡ (m − 1)N −1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Every
attainable residue is thus 0 or 1 modulo p, and Theorem 2.3 gives p | G(N ).
□
Theorem 2.9 (residueGcd_dvd_of_prime_pow). Let N ≥ 3 be odd and let N = pk with
p an odd prime and k ≥ 2. Then G(N ) | p. [L]
Proof. By Proposition 2.5 some base m ∈ [1, N ] attains the residue 1 + p. That residue is
nonzero, since p ≥ 3, so Lemma 2.2 applies and gives G(N ) | (1 + p) − 1 = p.
□
Theorem 2.10 (residueGcd_prime_pow). Let N ≥ 3 be odd and let N = pk with p an
odd prime and k ≥ 2. Then G(N ) = p. [L]
Proof. N is odd, and N = pk ; a power of 2 is even, so p is odd. Now G(N ) | p by
Theorem 2.9, while p | G(N ) by Theorem 2.8, which needs only k ≥ 1. By antisymmetry,
G(N ) = p.
□

2.4. Where G lives: G | N (N − 1).
Lemma 2.11 (residueGcd_dvd_idem_sub_one). Let N ≥ 3 be odd, and let v be an
integer with 2 ≤ v < N and v 2 ≡ v (mod N ). Then G(N ) | v − 1 in Z. [L]
Proof. Since 2 ≤ v we have v ≥ 0; let m = v + 1. Then 1 ≤ m ≤ N and m − 1 = v. An
idempotent is its own Fermat residue, so r(N, m) = v (the idempotent is an attainable
residue, as in Proposition 2.4). This residue is nonzero, so Lemma 2.2 gives G(N ) | v−1. □
Theorem 2.12 (residueGcd_dvd_sub_one). Let N ≥ 3 be odd and let N = d · e with
d, e > 1 coprime. Then G(N ) | N − 1. [L]
Proof. The split has an idempotent: some integer u with 2 ≤ u < N , u2 ≡ u (mod N ),
u ≡ 1 (mod d) and u ≡ 0 (mod e) (the construction of Proposition 2.4). Its complement
u′ = N + 1 − u is again idempotent modulo N — modulo N it is 1 − u and (1 − u)2 ≡ 1 − u
— and again satisfies 2 ≤ u′ < N (Pairing.idem_compl).
By Lemma 2.11, G(N ) divides both u−1 and u′ −1. Their sum is (u−1)+(u′ −1) = N −1,
so G(N ) | N − 1; both sides are natural numbers, so the divisibility holds in N as well. □

Theorem 2.18 (residueGcd_eq_one_of_not_squarefree). Let N ≥ 3 be odd, not

Lemma 2.19 (residueGcd_eq_one_of_add). Let N ≥ 3 be odd, and let ru , rv , rw be
three nonzero attainable residues with rw = ru + rv as integers. Then G(N ) = 1. [L]
Proof. Every residue lies in [0, N ), so the equation is an equality of integers — the sum is
not reduced modulo N . By Lemma 2.2, G(N ) divides ru − 1, rv − 1 and rw − 1, hence
divides (ru − 1) + (rv − 1) − (rw − 1) = −1. So G(N ) | 1, and G(N ) ≥ 0, whence
G(N ) = 1.

3. Carmichael numbers and the idempotent sum
Remark 2.21 left one number unexplained: at N = 6601 = 7 · 23 · 41 the invariant is 2
where the OEIS sequence returns 1. The explanation is that the residues of a Carmichael
number are not a set of powers but a lattice. A base m with m − 1 a unit modulo N
contributes the residue 1 and nothing else; every other residue comes from a base with m−1
sharing a prime factor with N , and all of them are idempotent: r2 ≡ r (mod N ). The
residue set of a squarefree Korselt number therefore collapses onto the 2ω CRT idempotents
of Z/N Z, and G(N ) — the gcd of the offsets r − 1 — becomes a question about that
lattice.
Throughout this section N is odd, squarefree and Korselt: p − 1 | N − 1 for every
prime p | N , which is Korselt’s condition [4] for N to be a Carmichael number. We write
S = {p : p prime, p | N },

ω = |S|,

so that N = p∈S p. The results below hold for every ω ≥ 2, and the Lean statements
carry that hypothesis where they need it.
∏︁

3.1. Unit bases on a Korselt number give 1.
Theorem 3.1 (korselt_unit_resid_eq_one). Let N be odd, squarefree and Korselt, and
let m be a base. If the residue r(N, m), read in Z/N Z, is a unit, then r(N, m) = 1. [L]
Proof. Every attainable residue of a squarefree Korselt number is idempotent: r2 ≡ r
(mod N ) for r = r(N, m) (Refutation.resid_idem_of_korselt'). Read in Z/N Z that
congruence is the equation r · r = r · 1. If r is a unit, cancel the common factor r to obtain
r = 1.
□
On a Carmichael number, then, the unit part of the residue set carries no local content
at all: whatever a prime ℓ contributes to G(N ) has to come from residues that are not
units.

3.2. The idempotent lattice decides G. An idempotent of Z/N Z is its own Fermat

3.3. Half the lattice is redundant.
Lemma 3.4 (dvd_compl_sub_one). Let M , A and e be integers with M | A − 1 and

6. Off the Korselt locus
Section 5 gave G(N ) exactly on every squarefree N — as the gcd of its numerator
cosets. That does not say how often it is 1. Off the Korselt locus and away from the
prime powers, where G(pk ) = p, it is 1 at every composite with at least two prime factors
below 3.1 · 108 except one (a measurement, recorded at the end of the section), and this
section exhibits the mechanisms that force the collapse: a coprime pair of attainable
offsets, coprime wrap counts, a local −1 in one prime’s power image, a 2-adic mismatch
between two primes, and, unconditionally, the least prime whose p − 1 misses N − 1. Then
it records the smallest number at which none of them is available.
Throughout, N ≥ 3 is odd and G(N ) is the invariant of Definition 2.1: the gcd of the
offsets r − 1 over the nonzero Fermat residues r = r(N, m) as the base m runs over [1, N ].
Two facts from Section 2 are used throughout: G(N ) divides every offset (Lemma 2.2),
and a modulus M divides G(N ) exactly when every attainable residue is 0 or 1 modulo
M (Theorem 2.3).
6.1. The bridge: a pair of coprime offsets. Every collapse in this section is read
through one theorem, and it needs nothing beyond the definition of G: two nonzero
residues whose offsets are coprime collapse the invariant, because a Bézout combination of
two multiples of G(N ) is 1.
Theorem 6.1 (residueGcd_eq_one_of_coprime_offsets). Let N ≥ 3 be odd, and let
1 ≤ mu ≤ N and 1 ≤ mv ≤ N be bases whose residues ru = r(N, mu ) and rv = r(N, mv )

Proof. By Lemma 2.2, G(N ) divides each of the two offsets ru − 1 and rv − 1 in Z.

a (ru − 1) + b (rv − 1) = 1.

G(N ); so G(N ) | 1, and a natural number dividing 1 is 1.

does, with no unitary split, no idempotent and no primality hypothesis in sight. Two

□

6.3. Parity at a semiprime. The first mechanism that needs only the modulus 2 is
parity. The invariant divides the offset r − 1 of every nonzero residue, so an even residue

7. The exponent
Section 6 studied G(N ) away from the Korselt locus and found it 1 wherever one of its
mechanisms applies. One number has been fixed since G was defined, and no definition
chose it: the exponent N − 1 inside the Fermat residue r = r(N, m). It comes from
Fermat’s congruence; the definition of G, a gcd of offsets r − 1 over the nonzero residues,
does not require it. This section lets the exponent vary. Write re (N, m) for the residue of
the base m at the exponent e, and Ge (N ) for the same gcd; then the exponents at which
Ge (N ) = 0 for a prime N (the prime direction) are exactly the multiples of N − 1, each of
them gives a Ge (N ) divisible by G(N ), and e enters the value only through gcd(e, λ(N )).
At a saturated exponent nothing local is left: the value is the numerator gcd of Section 4,
or 1 under a carry. Since a Korselt N has N − 1 saturated, the closed form of Section 4 is
the saturated value, and after this section the exponent is no longer a parameter of the
definition.
7.1. The exponent as a parameter. The residue carried N − 1 because Fermat’s
congruence does. The two definitions below free it, and the result after them checks that
nothing was added.

8. Size, and what the invariant decides
Section 7 freed the exponent inside the Fermat residue and showed that the invariant
does not follow it: every exponent that keeps the prime direction gives a Ge (N ) that is
a multiple of G(N ), Ge sees e only through gcd(e, λ(N )), and at a saturated exponent
the value is read off the numerators with nothing local left to sweep. What remains of
G(N ) — the gcd of the offsets r − 1 over the nonzero residues r = r(N, m), over all bases
m ∈ [1, N ] — is therefore a plain arithmetic function of N , and the questions left
√ about it
are numerical. This section answers the first of them. The invariant is at most N ; it lies
strictly below the least prime factor of a squarefree N ; once N has three prime factors it
is at most the cube root; and consequently the least modulus that does not divide G(N )
is at most log2 N + 2. Only then, with the size in hand, does the article take up the finite
computation it started from — the reduction of the Fermat residue modulo a fixed M ,
over every base — and identify what that reduction decides: it does not detect N exactly
when M fails to divide G(N ).
8.1. How large is the invariant? Every size statement below is a statement about one
of the three shapes a composite can have. A prime power has the closed form G(pk ) = p of
Section 2; a squarefree number reduces to its least prime factor, through the one estimate
of Section 6; and a number that is neither has G = 1. The first bound is the sharp
statement of that trichotomy.

8.3. What the invariant decides. Everything so far reads G(N ) as a number: a gcd
of offsets, bounded, small. The article opened on a different object, the reduction of the
Fermat residue modulo a fixed modulus, and this is where the two meet. Fix M , let
1 ≤ m ≤ N be a base, and call m a witness at M for N when its Fermat residue r(N, m)
is nonzero and not congruent to 1 modulo M . The reduction reports N composite as soon
as one base is a witness, and accepts N only if it finds none; this is the idealised rung-M
reduction (the reductions at the powers of two, M = 2j , are the rungs of the ladder), and
the only thing to fix about it is the shape of “not a witness”.

Read with the valuation this is ν2 (G(N )) ≤ ν2 (N − 1), hence, through Theorem 8.28,

Theorem 8.32 (exists_witnessMod_two_pow_of_not_dvd). Let N ≥ 3 be odd and
composite, let j be a natural number with 2j ∤ N − 1. Then some base is a witness at 2j
for N . [L]
Proof. Had 2j divided G(N ), then Theorem 8.31 would make it divide N − 1, against the
hypothesis. So 2j ∤ G(N ), and the contrapositive reading of Theorem 8.12 produces a
witness.
□
This is the dyadic face of Theorem 8.24, and the only form the concrete instances need,
since it asks for no valuation. One shift past the two-part of N − 1 is therefore one rung
past every modulus that finds no witness at N , and that is a schedule.

Proof. One shift past the two-part of N − 1 gives 2ν2 (N −1)+1 ∤ N − 1: if it divided N − 1,

Lemma 8.35 (noWitnessMod_prime_pow). Let M be an odd prime, let x ≥ 2 and k ≥ 2
be natural numbers with N (x) = 2x − 1 = M k . Then the rung-M reduction finds no
witness for N (x). [L]
Proof. Here N = M k with M an odd prime and k ≥ 2, so G(N ) = M by Theorem 2.10.
In particular M | G(N ), and Theorem 8.12 turns that divisibility into the statement that
no base is a witness at M .
□
Theorem 8.36 (odd_prime_constant_modulus_fails). Let M be an odd prime and let
B be a natural number. Then there is a composite N > B whose rung-M reduction finds
no witness. [L]
Proof. Put k = B + 2 and N = M k , realised as N = N (x) for x = (M k + 1)/2. Since
B + 2 < 2B+2 ≤ M B+2 we have N > B, and a power of a prime with exponent k ≥ 2 is
composite. Lemma 8.35 then gives the absence of a witness at M .
□
This is the unconditional form: there are arbitrarily large composites at which an odd
prime modulus finds no witness. The corresponding statement on the squarefree locus
needs a hypothesis of Dickson type and is not proved here or in this article; the conditional
version is in [9]. What the proof uses about N is only the closed form G(M k ) = M of
Theorem 2.10; on any other shape, whether M finds no witness is a statement about N
and not about M , and Section 6 is where that is treated.
8.7. Two closed moduli. The divisibility criterion of Theorem 8.24 takes M as given
and asks only that it miss N (N − 1). Two choices of M can be written down in closed
form, one trivially — M = N + 1 exceeds every residue, so the reduction loses nothing
and the criterion is the Fermat test on all bases — and one by a search, and both give an
exact criterion — not an estimate, and with no reference to G(N ).

Corollary 8.38 (prime_iff_no_witnessMod_succ). Let N ≥ 3 be odd. Then N is prime

Proof. Write N = k + 3. Then
N (N − 1) = (N + 1)(N − 2) + 2,
so a divisor N + 1 of N (N − 1) would divide 2; but N + 1 > 2, so that is impossible.

Definition 8.39 (minNonDivisor). Let K ≥ 1. The least non-divisor of K is the least
natural number m ≥ 2 that does not divide K:
minNonDivisor(K) = min{ m ≥ 2 : m ∤ K }.
The set it minimises is nonempty — K + 1 lies in it, since K + 1 ≥ 2 and K + 1 > K

properties are all that is used: it is at least 2 (two_le_minNonDivisor), and it does not
divide K (minNonDivisor_not_dvd).

Theorem 8.37 applies at M = minNonDivisor(K).

8.8. The mod-4 criterion, read on the invariant. The criterion this paper takes as its
starting point is defined in [8]; what is used here is its form on residues. Write N = 2x − 1
and let κ = x − 1 for even x and κ = x for odd x. For each base m ∈ [1, N ] the criterion
reduces U (m) = κ r(N, m) mod N and reports the value 0 when U (m) is 0 or odd and
2 otherwise (Residue.f_eq_zero_iff [L] ); it finds no witness for N when the value is 0
at every base of the window (AllZero). On a squarefree Korselt N with N ≡ 1 (mod 4)
that criterion and the invariant are the same object.

8.9. What the criterion costs. One limitation remains. The schedules are explicit, but
the reduction this section is about examines every base of the window: its definition quantifies over all m ∈ [1, N ] (Lemma 8.11), and each base costs one modular exponentiation.
So the reduction is Θ(N ) modular exponentiations whatever the modulus — making the
modulus small, as Theorem 8.25 does, does not make the criterion cheap. In particular
the invariant is not a primality test, and the divisibility reading of Theorem 8.12 does not
make it one: what that equivalence does is say which moduli see a composite, not how to
find one without sweeping the bases. Nor does the divisibility reading revive the reduction
at the fixed modulus 4: that one is false, its failure being 4 | G(2508013) (Corollary 8.42).
The threshold growth of Theorem 8.29 and the shapes with two prime factors are left to

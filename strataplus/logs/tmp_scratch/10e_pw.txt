
\section{Problem P-W: the Counterexamples with the Criterion Removed}
\label{sec:pw}

The previous section ended on an identification. The members of the family of
2009 are the rows of the Demjanenko matrix read modulo two, their character sums
are the generalised Bernoulli numbers $B_{1,\xi}$, and the formula was computing
a classical object. That identification says what the formula reads; it does not
say where the criterion has no witness.

At some Carmichael numbers the criterion is zero at every base. On a squarefree
$N \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ that is Korselt --- $p-1 \mid N-1$ for every prime $p \mid N$
--- with at least
two prime factors, it happens at some and not at others, and whether it happens at
infinitely many is not a question about the criterion at all. This section takes
the criterion out of its own question. On that locus the vanishing is an arithmetic
condition on the numerators of one congruence: every
$v_p \equiv -(N/p)^{-1} \pmod p$ divisible by four, and their Egyptian sum
carrying nothing. Written that way the question names no residue, no base and no
modulus, and it is open; nothing in this section decides it.

The section proves that the two forms of the question are the same statement; it certifies
eight members with kernel-checked data; and it proves that a
member propagates along its residue class and that one whole family ---
$\bigl((b-1)m+1,\; bm+1,\; (b+1)m+1\bigr)$ for even $b$ --- is decided by three
 divisibilities once its three forms are prime. Each of those is a theorem in the strict sense, unconditional and
marked $\lv$ alone; the hypothesised form of the question is one subsection at the
end, where every statement carries its hypothesis in its signature.

The same problem is stated in \cite{Companion-korselt-misses} layer by layer: the
unconditional reduction to at most five factors and the certified instances, then what a
Dickson-type hypothesis adds. Here the order is
the question first, then the certified evidence, then the hypothesis. The eight
members of
\S\ref{sec:pw-members} are what this development certifies and not a census: no
count of members, and no claim that the table is complete, appears anywhere
below.

The section reads as its argument runs. \S\ref{sec:pw-condition} writes the vanishing
as an arithmetic condition and gives the carry its floor;
\S\ref{sec:pw-problem} states the problem in its two forms and proves them the
same statement; \S\ref{sec:pw-members} certifies eight members, by size;
\S\ref{sec:pw-propagate} proves that a member propagates along its class and
checks that the classes of this development are admissible;
\S\ref{sec:pw-evenb} decides one family in full; \S\ref{sec:pw-hypothesis} says
what a hypothesis would add and stops; \S\ref{sec:pw-close} says what is not
decided.

\subsection{The vanishing is an arithmetic condition}
\label{sec:pw-condition}

Throughout, $x \ge 2$ and $N = N(x) = 2x-1$. The locus is the one on which the criterion
is zero at every base: $N$ squarefree, $N \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, at least two prime factors,
and $p-1 \mid N-1$ for every prime $p \mid N$, which is Korselt's criterion
\cite{Korselt1899} read at $N$. The criterion's own value at $N$ and the base $m$
is $\ffn{x}{m} = \Efn{x}{m} \bmod 4$, and the criterion \emph{vanishes identically}
at $N$ when $\ffn{x}{m} = 0$ at every base $m$, that is when $\lean{AllZero}\, x$ holds
(\S\ref{sec:definitions}). For $p \mid N$ the \emph{cofactor} of $p$ in $N$ is
$\lean{cof}$ of \S\ref{sec:wrap}; at a prime factor of a squarefree number it is
the quotient $N/p$, and the prose writes $N/p$ from here on.

A \emph{numerator} at $p$ is an integer $v_p$ with
\[
  0 < v_p < p,
  \qquad
  p \;\Big|\; v_p\,\frac{N}{p} + 1 .
\]
One exists at every prime of every finite set of primes, and the reason is a
unit. For a finite set $S$ of primes and $p \in S$, the cofactor
$\lean{cof}(S,p)$ is not divisible by $p$: if it were, $p$ would divide a product
of primes none of which is $p$. So $\lean{cof}(S,p)$ is invertible modulo $p$,
$-\lean{cof}(S,p)^{-1}$ is a class modulo $p$, its least non-negative
representative is non-zero and below $p$, and the divisibility holds by
construction:
\[
  \exists v : \N \to \Z,\ \forall p \in S,\
  0 < v_p < p \ \wedge\ p \mid v_p\,\lean{cof}(S,p) + 1
  \qquad
  (\lv\ \lean{exists\_numerators}).
\]
The numerator is moreover unique: two integers in $(0,p)$ with $p \mid v\,c+1$
coincide, because their difference is a multiple of $p$ of absolute value below
$p$ ($\lv$ \lean{resid\_unique}). So ``the numerator at $p$'' is a property of $N$
and not of a choice, and a condition on the numerators is a condition on $N$.

That is what makes the next display a statement about arithmetic.

\begin{theorem}[The vanishing is an arithmetic condition]
\label{thm:pw-at}
\lv{} \lean{allZero\_iff\_pw\_at}
Let $x \ge 2$ and $N = N(x)$. Suppose that $N \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, that $N$ is
squarefree, that $\omega(N) \ge 2$, and that $p-1 \mid N-1$ for every prime
$p \mid N$. Then the criterion vanishes identically at $N$ if and only if there
are integers $v_p$, indexed
by the primes $p \mid N$, with
\[
  0 < v_p < p, \qquad p \;\Big|\; v_p\,\frac{N}{p} + 1, \qquad 4 \mid v_p
  \qquad (p \mid N),
\]
and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pw-at}
  \sum_{p \mid N} v_p\,\frac{N}{p} \;=\; N-1 .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
All three ingredients are \S\ref{sec:korselt}'s, and the hypothesis $N \equiv 1
\pmod 4$ is what lets them be read here. First, $x$ is odd: $N = 2x-1 \equiv 1$
modulo four gives $2x \equiv 2$, hence $x \equiv 1$ modulo two. Second, the prime
factors of $N$ are prime, their product is $N$ because $N$ is squarefree, and
$p-1 \mid 2x-2 = N-1$ is the Korselt hypothesis, so the set
$S = \lean{primeFactors}\, N$ carries the data the earlier section's statements
ask for. Third, on a squarefree Korselt number the criterion passes at every base
exactly when $4 \mid G(N)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion-four} of
\S\ref{sec:korselt}); and at a fixed set of numerators, $M \mid G(N)$ is
equivalent to $M \mid v_p$ for every $p$ once the numerators do not carry
($\lv$ \lean{korselt\_dvd\_residueGcd\_iff\_numerators}), while if they do carry
then $G(N) = 1$ ($\lv$ \lean{korselt\_residueGcd\_noCarry}, second conjunct).

Suppose first that the criterion vanishes identically at $N$, so $4 \mid G(N)$.
Take the canonical numerators
$v_p = -(N/p)^{-1}$ of the display above. If they satisfy \eqref{eq:pw-at}, the
numerator criterion at $M = 4$ turns $4 \mid G(N)$ into $4 \mid v_p$ for every
$p$, which is the second clause. If they do not, the no-carry theorem's second
conjunct gives $G(N) = 1$, contradicting $4 \mid G(N)$; so that case does not
arise, and the two clauses hold as stated.

Conversely, let $v_p$ be integers with the three properties of the display and
with the sum \eqref{eq:pw-at}. The numerator criterion at $M = 4$ reads them back
as $4 \mid G(N)$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion-four} turns that divisibility
into a pass at every base.
\end{proof}

The two clauses are the traces of the two objects the criterion reads.
$4 \mid v_p$ is the trace of the coefficient and of the report modulo four: the
coefficient of 2009 is $2^{-1}$ modulo $N$ up to sign, and the value it produces
is a residue modulo four, so what survives of it on the Korselt locus is a
divisibility of the numerators by four. Equation~\eqref{eq:pw-at} is the trace of
the residue: divided by $N$ it reads $\sum_{p \mid N} v_p/p = 1 - 1/N$, so the
numerators are the coefficients of a partial-fraction expansion of $1 - 1/N$ whose
denominators are the primes of $N$. Neither clause mentions $f$,
$E$, a base or a modulus; both are statements about $N$ and its prime factors.
That is the whole content of the phrase in the title.

The second clause deserves the name the book already gave it. The \emph{carry} of
\S\ref{sec:korselt} is the integer $C$ with
$\lean{egyptianSum}(S,v) = (C+1)N-1$ (\eqref{eq:carry} there, $\lv$
\lean{egyptianSum\_eq\_carry\_mul}), and \eqref{eq:pw-at} says $C = 0$. The form
in which the condition is handed over is a floor. Since
$p \cdot \lean{cof}(S,p) = \prod_{q \in S} q$ ($\lv$ \lean{mul\_cof}), each term
of the sum is $v_q/q = v_q \lean{cof}(S,q)/N$, so the sum of the fractions
$v_q/q$ over $q \in S$ is $C+1-1/N$; omitting one term $v_p/p$ leaves
\[
  \sum_{q \in S,\ q \ne p} \frac{v_q}{q}
  \;=\; C + \Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{N} - \frac{v_p}{p}\Bigr),
\]
and the bracket lies strictly between $0$ and $1$ because $0 < v_p < p$ and
$p < N$. Hence
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pw-carry}
  C \;=\; \Bigl\lfloor \sum_{q \in S,\ q \ne p} \frac{v_q}{q} \Bigr\rfloor
  \qquad
  (\lv\ \lean{carry\_eq\_floor}),
\end{equation}
for every $p \in S$, and in particular
\[
  C = 0 \iff \sum_{q \in S,\ q \ne p} \frac{v_q}{q} < 1
  \qquad
  (\lv\ \lean{carry\_eq\_zero\_iff\_sum\_lt\_one}).
\]
So ``carries nothing'' is not a bookkeeping condition but an inequality between
fractions, for any one omitted prime. That is the form in which the condition is
a statement about numbers and not about a filter, and the reason the problem can
be handed to a reader who has never seen the criterion.

One number shows what a member is not. A Korselt number
congruent to $1$ modulo four is not automatically one at which the criterion
vanishes identically: at $N = 188461$ the
invariant is $1$ ($\lv$ \lean{residueGcd\_188461\_eq\_one},
\S\ref{sec:korselt}), hence $4 \nmid G$ and the criterion has a witness at it. The members
of P-W are not what a Korselt number usually is, and the sentence is worth its
space because the opposite reading --- that the criterion has no witness at
Carmichael numbers --- is
the first one a reader forms, and it is false.

\subsection{The problem, and its two forms}
\label{sec:pw-problem}

\begin{definition}[Problem P-W]
\label{def:pw-problem}
\lean{PWProblem}
For every bound $n_0$ there is a natural number $n > n_0$ such that
$n \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, $n$ is squarefree, $\omega(n) \ge 2$, $p-1 \mid n-1$ for
every prime $p \mid n$, and there are integers $v_p$ with $0 < v_p < p$,
$p \mid v_p\,(n/p) + 1$ and $4 \mid v_p$ for every $p \mid n$, whose Egyptian sum
is exactly one less than the number:
\[
  \sum_{p \mid n} v_p\,\frac{n}{p} \;=\; n-1 .
\]
\end{definition}

Read clause by clause, the definition has six clauses, and not one mentions the
criterion. The congruence $n \equiv 1$ modulo four is the class on which the
criterion is decided by a divisibility; squarefreeness and $\omega(n) \ge 2$ are
the shape of a Korselt number above the semiprimes; $p-1 \mid n-1$ is Korselt's
criterion itself, which is a statement about $n$ alone; and the last two clauses
are the arithmetic condition of Theorem~\ref{thm:pw-at}, which
Theorem~\ref{thm:pw-nocarry} states in the criterion's own letters, the second of
them being $C = 0$, that is \eqref{eq:pw-carry} read with the floor at zero. The
letter $n$ is not
the $N(x)$ of the rest of the book: it is any natural number, and the parameter
$x$ comes back only in the second form.

A \emph{member} is a number with the six clauses and without the bound. Write
$\lean{PWData}(n)$ for that predicate; then P-W says that for every $n_0$ there is
$n > n_0$ with $\lean{PWData}(n)$, and the two predicates are the same statement
($\lv$ \lean{pwProblem\_of\_pwData}, which is the definition read). The section
writes ``$n$ carries P-W data'' from here on, and every member of
\S\ref{sec:pw-members} is certified in that form.

\begin{definition}[The criterion zero at infinitely many Korselt numbers]
\label{def:korselt-miss-infinite}
\lean{KorseltMissInfinite}
For every bound $n_0$ there is an $x \ge 2$ with $N(x) > n_0$ such that
$N(x) \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, $N(x)$ is squarefree, $\omega(N(x)) \ge 2$,
$p-1 \mid N(x)-1$ for every prime $p \mid N(x)$, and $\lean{AllZero}\, x$.
\end{definition}

The two definitions have the same five arithmetic clauses; they differ in the
letter of the number and in the last clause, which the second compresses into
$\lean{AllZero}\, x$. That is the criterion's own form of the question: the
parameter is $x$, the number is $N = 2x-1$, and the condition on the numerators
is the predicate the earlier Parts were about.

Between the two forms sits one identity, and it is the one the earlier sections
state in their own letters: for an arbitrary finite set $S$ of primes with
product $N$ and an arbitrary vector of numerators, a pass at $N$ is exactly the
arithmetic condition.

\begin{theorem}[The criterion's own form of the condition]
\label{thm:pw-nocarry}
\lv{} \lean{korselt\_allZero\_iff\_noCarry\_four\_dvd}
Let $x \ge 2$ be odd, put $N = N(x)$, and let $S$ be a finite set of primes with
$\prod_{p \in S} p = N$, $|S| \ge 2$ and $p-1 \mid 2x-2$ for every $p \in S$.
Let $v_p \in (0,p)$ be integers with $p \mid v_p\,\lean{cof}(S,p)+1$ for every
$p \in S$. Then the criterion vanishes identically at $N$ if and only if
\[
  \sum_{p \in S} v_p\,\lean{cof}(S,p) \;=\; N-1
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  4 \mid v_p \quad (p \in S).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The product over a finite set of primes is squarefree, and $x$ odd makes
 $N \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, so the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion-four} are
 met: on the Korselt locus the pass is the divisibility $4 \mid G(N)$, and it
 suffices to determine when four divides the invariant. Split on the sum.

If the sum is $N-1$, the numerator criterion at $M = 4$
($\lv$ \lean{korselt\_dvd\_residueGcd\_iff\_numerators}) gives that $4 \mid G(N)$
holds exactly when $4 \mid v_p$ for every $p \in S$, which is the second clause.

If the sum is not $N-1$, the second conjunct of the no-carry theorem
($\lv$ \lean{korselt\_residueGcd\_noCarry}) gives $G(N) = 1$, so $4 \mid G(N)$
fails and the right-hand side fails with it, its first clause being false.

Composing the two cases, the pass is equivalent to the conjunction of the display
in both directions.
\end{proof}

Now the two definitions are the same statement, and the proof is a change of
letter.

\begin{theorem}[The problem is the question with the criterion removed]
\label{thm:pw-iff}
\lv{} \lean{pwProblem\_iff}
Problem~\ref{def:pw-problem} holds if and only if
Definition~\ref{def:korselt-miss-infinite} does.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let P-W hold and let $n_0$ be a bound. Take $n > n_0$ with the six clauses. The
number is odd, since $n \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, and $n \ge 2$ --- indeed $n \ge 6$,
since $n$ has at least two prime factors. Put $x = (n+1)/2$; then $x \ge 2$ and
$N(x) = 2x-1 = n$.

The five arithmetic clauses rewrite along that equality: $N(x) \equiv 1$ modulo
four, $N(x)$ squarefree, $\omega(N(x)) \ge 2$, and $p-1 \mid N(x)-1$ at every
prime factor. For the last clause, apply Theorem~\ref{thm:pw-at} in the direction
from the arithmetic condition to the pass, at this $x$, to the numerators the
first definition provides: the hypotheses of that theorem are the five clauses
just transported, and its conclusion is $\lean{AllZero}\, x$.

Conversely, let Definition~\ref{def:korselt-miss-infinite} hold and let $n_0$ be a
bound. Take $x$ with $N(x) > n_0$ and the five clauses, $\lean{AllZero}\, x$
among them, and put $n = N(x)$. The same five rewrites give the five arithmetic
clauses for $n$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:pw-at} in the other direction turns the
pass into the existence of numerators with the two last properties.

Hence each statement implies the other, and they differ by nothing but the letter
of the parameter.
\end{proof}

The theorem is short and it is not a formality. Problem~\ref{def:pw-problem}
quantifies over $n$ and never over $x$; Definition~\ref{def:korselt-miss-infinite}
quantifies over $x$ and carries the criterion's own predicate. That the two are
the same statement is exactly the claim that the problem \emph{is} the question
with the criterion removed, and the proof is nothing but the change of letter,
which is the point. In one clause: this is where the criterion of 2009 leaves the
book. The object it defined is gone from the statement, the arithmetic it was
reading is not.

\subsection{The certified members}
\label{sec:pw-members}

Eight numbers carry kernel-checked P-W data, and the first of them is proved from
scratch, clause by clause, so that the pattern the others repeat is visible once.

\begin{theorem}[The member at $m = 22\,146$]
\label{thm:pw-instance-12510}
\lv{} \lean{pw\_instance\_1\_2\_5\_10}
The number
\[
  n \;=\; 24\,055\,615\,898\,561\,844\,361
  \;=\; 22\,147 \cdot 44\,293 \cdot 110\,731 \cdot 221\,461
\]
is a member: $n \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, $n$ is squarefree, $\omega(n) = 4$, $p-1 \mid
n-1$ at its four primes, and the numerators
\[
  v \;=\; (3\,076,\ 14\,764,\ 46\,140,\ 24\,604)
\]
lie in $(0,p)$, are divisible by four, and satisfy \eqref{eq:pw-at}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The number is its primes. The four factors are the primes $22\,147$, $44\,293$,
$110\,731$ and $221\,461$ ($\lv$ \lean{S\_1\_2\_5\_10\_primes}), the finite set
$S = \lean{S\_1\_2\_5\_10} = \{22\,147,\,44\,293,\,110\,731,\,221\,461\}$ they
form has cardinal four ($\lv$ \lean{S\_1\_2\_5\_10\_card}), its product
is $n$ ($\lv$ \lean{S\_1\_2\_5\_10\_prod}) and $n$ is defined as that product
($\lv$ \lean{n\_1\_2\_5\_10}, $\lv$ \lean{n\_1\_2\_5\_10\_factorization}); so
$S = \lean{primeFactors}\, n$ ($\lv$ \lean{primeFactors\_n\_1\_2\_5\_10}).

The three shape clauses are computations. The number is $1$ modulo four
($\lv$ \lean{n\_1\_2\_5\_10\_mod\_four}); it is squarefree, being a product of
four distinct primes ($\lv$ \lean{n\_1\_2\_5\_10\_squarefree}); and its length is
four, which is at least two.

Korselt holds at the four primes ($\lv$ \lean{n\_1\_2\_5\_10\_korselt}). Here
$n-1 = 22\,146 \cdot Q$ with $Q = 1\,086\,228\,479\,118\,660 = 20 \cdot
54\,311\,423\,955\,933$, and the four numbers $p-1$ are $a_i \cdot 22\,146$ for
$a = (1,2,5,10)$, the four entries of the family. The four divisibilities are
therefore $1 \mid Q$, $2 \mid Q$, $5 \mid Q$ and $10 \mid Q$: the first is the
empty statement and the other three follow from the single fact that $Q$ is a
multiple of $20$.

The numerators are the four numbers of the display ($\lv$ \lean{v\_1\_2\_5\_10}):
each is positive ($\lv$ \lean{v\_1\_2\_5\_10\_pos}), each is below its prime
($\lv$ \lean{v\_1\_2\_5\_10\_lt}), and each is a multiple of four
($\lv$ \lean{v\_1\_2\_5\_10\_four\_dvd}).

The four congruences are the four cofactors
\begin{gather*}
  \lean{cof}(S, 22\,147) = 1\,086\,179\,432\,815\,363, \qquad
  \lean{cof}(S, 44\,293) = 543\,101\,977\,706\,677, \\
  \lean{cof}(S, 110\,731) = 217\,243\,733\,900\,731, \qquad
  \lean{cof}(S, 221\,461) = 108\,622\,357\,428\,901
\end{gather*}
($\lv$ \lean{cof\_22147}, $\lv$ \lean{cof\_44293}, $\lv$ \lean{cof\_110731},
$\lv$ \lean{cof\_221461}), each the product of the other three primes, and the
four products $v_p \lean{cof}(S,p) + 1$ are
\begin{gather*}
  3\,341\,087\,935\,340\,056\,589, \qquad 8\,018\,357\,598\,861\,379\,229, \\
  10\,023\,625\,882\,179\,728\,341, \qquad 2\,672\,544\,482\,180\,680\,205,
\end{gather*}
with the four quotients exhibited at each prime ($\lv$
\lean{v\_1\_2\_5\_10\_dvd}).

The Egyptian sum is exactly one less than the number,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pw-egyptian}
  \sum_{p \in S} v_p\,\lean{cof}(S,p)
  \;=\; 24\,055\,615\,898\,561\,844\,360 \;=\; n-1
\end{equation}
($\lv$ \lean{v\_1\_2\_5\_10\_sum}). The six clauses are then one statement
($\lv$ \lean{pw\_instance\_1\_2\_5\_10}), and the same number is a member in the
predicate's own form ($\lv$ \lean{pwData\_1\_2\_5\_10}).
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The member at $m = 22\,146$, in full]
\label{ex:pw-member}
The arithmetic of the theorem is four divisibilities, one per prime,
\[
  22\,147 \mid 3\,076 \cdot 1\,086\,179\,432\,815\,363 + 1, \qquad
  44\,293 \mid 14\,764 \cdot 543\,101\,977\,706\,677 + 1,
\]
\[
  110\,731 \mid 46\,140 \cdot 217\,243\,733\,900\,731 + 1, \qquad
  221\,461 \mid 24\,604 \cdot 108\,622\,357\,428\,901 + 1,
\]
whose quotients, in the order of the primes, are
\[
  150\,859\,616\,893\,487, \qquad 181\,029\,905\,376\,953,
\]
\[
  90\,522\,309\,761\,311, \qquad 12\,067\,788\,378\,905 ;
\]
and the four numerators divide by four as $769$, $3\,691$, $11\,535$ and
$6\,151$. Every clause is a finite check, and the two that matter are the last
two: all four numerators are multiples of four, and their Egyptian sum does not
carry. That is the pattern the rest of the section repeats at scale.
\end{example}

\subsection{What propagates along a class}
\label{sec:pw-propagate}

A family is a tuple $a = (a_1,\dots,a_k)$ of distinct positive integers, and its
numbers at $m$ are the products
\[
  \lean{famNk}(a,m) \;=\; \prod_{i} (a_i m + 1),
  \qquad
  \lean{famQk}(a,m) \;=\; \frac{\lean{famNk}(a,m) - 1}{m},
\]
the second written as a telescoping sum $\sum_i a_i \prod_{j<i} (a_j m + 1)$, an
integer by construction ($\lv$ \lean{prod\_range\_telescope}, $\lv$
\lean{famNk\_eq}); the module also has the extension $\lean{famExt}$ of a
$k$-tuple by zero, so that a $k$-term family is a function on $\N$. At $k = 4$ the
section writes $\lean{famN}$ and $\lean{famQ}$ for the same two functions
($\lv$ \lean{famN\_eq}, $\lv$ \lean{famQ\_eq\_famQk}), and both are congruent
along a class: $m \equiv m_0 \pmod M$ implies
$\lean{famNk}(a,m) \equiv \lean{famNk}(a,m_0)$ and the same for the quotients
($\lv$ \lean{famNk\_modEq}, $\lv$ \lean{famQk\_modEq}, and $\lv$
\lean{famN\_modEq}, $\lv$ \lean{famQ\_modEq} at $k = 4$). A number of the family
also dominates its parameter: $m < \lean{famNk}(a,m)$ as soon as one $a_i$ is
positive ($\lv$ \lean{lt\_famNk}, $\lv$ \lean{lt\_famN}), which is what makes a
family with arbitrarily large prime members produce arbitrarily large members of
P-W.

The point of a class is that the data of one point propagate to all of it.

\begin{theorem}[A member propagates along its class]
\label{thm:pw-propagate}
\lv{} \lean{pwData\_family\_propagate\_k}
Let $k \ge 2$, let $a_1,\dots,a_k$ be distinct positive integers, let
$m \equiv m_0 \pmod M$ with $m \ge m_0$, and suppose that integers $w_i > 0$,
$\alpha_i$, $\beta_i$, $u_i$ and $L \ne 0$ satisfy
\[
  u_i w_i = L, \qquad 4 w_i \mid M, \qquad a_i \mid M, \qquad 4 \mid M ,
\]
and, at the base point $m_0$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pw-data}
  \prod_i (a_i m_0+1) \equiv 1 \pmod 4, \qquad
  a_i \mid \lean{famQk}(a,m_0), \qquad
  4 w_i \mid \alpha_i m_0 + \beta_i ,
\end{equation}
\[
  0 \le \alpha_i \le w_i a_i, \qquad
  0 < \alpha_i m_0 + \beta_i < w_i\,(a_i m_0 + 1),
\]
together with the polynomial identity
\[
  \sum_i u_i\,(\alpha_i X + \beta_i) \prod_{j \ne i} (a_j X + 1)
  \;=\; L\,\Bigl( \prod_i (a_i X + 1) - 1 \Bigr)
  \qquad (X \in \Z).
\]
Then, if all the numbers $a_i m + 1$ are prime, $n = \lean{famNk}(a,m)$ carries
P-W data.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The class is a translation, and every arithmetic condition of the display is
stable under it. Write $m = m_0 + M j$ with $j \ge 0$; then
$\alpha_i m + \beta_i = (\alpha_i m_0 + \beta_i) + \alpha_i M j$, which is still
divisible by $4 w_i$ because $4 w_i \mid M$.

The numerators are the quotients $v_i = (\alpha_i m + \beta_i)/w_i$. They are
integers, because $w_i$ divides $\alpha_i m + \beta_i$; each is a multiple of
four, because $4 w_i$ divides that number; each is positive, because
$\alpha_i m_0 + \beta_i > 0$ and $\alpha_i M j \ge 0$; and each is below
$a_i m + 1$, because $\alpha_i \le w_i a_i$ together with
$\alpha_i m_0 + \beta_i < w_i (a_i m_0+1)$ gives
$\alpha_i m + \beta_i < w_i (a_i m + 1)$.

The class modulo four is the base point's, since $4 \mid M$ divides $m - m_0$ and
$\lean{famNk}$ is congruent along the class; so the product is $1$ modulo four.

Korselt holds at every $a_i m + 1$. For $a_i \mid M$ divides $m - m_0$, so
$\lean{famQk}(a,m) \equiv \lean{famQk}(a,m_0)$ modulo $a_i$, and the base point's
divisibility gives $a_i \mid \lean{famQk}(a,m)$; multiplying by $m$ and using
$\lean{famNk}(a,m) - 1 = m\,\lean{famQk}(a,m)$ gives
$a_i m \mid \lean{famNk}(a,m) - 1$, which is $p - 1 \mid n - 1$ at
$p = a_i m + 1$.

The Egyptian sum is the polynomial identity at $X = m$. Since
$u_i(\alpha_i m + \beta_i) = L v_i$, the left side is
$L \sum_i v_i \prod_{j \ne i}(a_j m+1)$, the right side is $L(n-1)$, and $L \ne 0$
cancels.

It remains to assemble. Apply the generic lemma below to the $k$ primes
$a_i m + 1$: they are distinct, since $a$ is injective and $m > 0$ --- and
$m > 0$ because $a_i m + 1$ would be $1$ at $m = 0$, which is not prime --- and
the divisibility, the bounds, the class modulo four, Korselt and the sum are the
five steps above.
\end{proof}

The generic lemma is where the divisibility $p \mid v_p \lean{cof}(S,p) + 1$ is
shown not to be a hypothesis but a consequence of the sum, and it is the reason
the section needs a lemma about $k$ primes at all.

Let $k \ge 2$, let $P_1,\dots,P_k$ be distinct primes, and let $v_i \in (0,P_i)$
be multiples of four with $\sum_i v_i \prod_{j \ne i} P_j = \prod_i P_i - 1$.
Then the product carries P-W data ($\lv$ \lean{pwData\_of\_fin}), and the proof
is five readings of one sum.

First, the prime factors of the product are the $P_i$: the product over the image
of $P$ is the product over the index set, so
$\lean{primeFactors}(\prod_i P_i)$ is the image of $P$ ($\lv$
\lean{primeFactors\_prod\_fin}), and the cofactor at $P_i$ is the product of the
others ($\lv$ \lean{cof\_image\_fin}), the two erase products being the ones the
module writes out at $k = 4$ and $k = 3$ ($\lv$ \lean{prod\_erase\_fin4}, $\lv$
\lean{prod\_erase\_fin3}).

Second, the divisibility is not assumed. Split the sum at the index $i$: the term
$v_i \prod_{j \ne i} P_j$ equals the whole product minus the other terms minus
one, and both subtracted groups are multiples of $P_i$, the first because $P_i$
is a factor of the product and the second because every one of its summands
contains a factor $P_i$. Hence $P_i \mid v_i \prod_{j\ne i}P_j + 1$.

Third, the product is squarefree, being a product of pairwise coprime
squarefree numbers. Fourth, its length is $k \ge 2$, and its class modulo four is
the hypothesis. Fifth, Korselt and the Egyptian sum transport along the identity
of prime factors. The four clauses are then P-W data.

At $k = 4$ the theorem above is stated in the same hypotheses with
$\lean{famN}$ and $\lean{famQ}$ (\lv \lean{pwData\_family\_propagate}), and that
form is a corollary of the general one (\lv
\lean{pwData\_family\_propagate\_of\_k}), which is what the rest of the section
uses. The polynomial identity is written with the erase products as
if-then products ($\lv$ \lean{prod\_erase\_eq\_prod\_ite}) when a machine
evaluates it, and by hand it is the partial-fraction identity the data encode.

Five classes carry the section's families, and the data of each are the tuple of
\eqref{eq:pw-data} read at its own base point.

For $a = (1,2,5,10)$ the class is $m \equiv 546 \pmod{720}$
($\lean{a\_1\_2\_5\_10}$), the data are $w = (36,3,12,9)$,
$\alpha = (5,2,25,10)$, $\beta = (6,0,30,-24)$, $u = (1,12,3,4)$ and $L = 36$,
and they reproduce the member of \S\ref{sec:pw-members} at $m = 22\,146$ ($\lv$
\lean{pw\_instance\_1\_2\_5\_10\_via\_family}). For $a = (1,2,6,9)$ the class is
$m \equiv 5\,670 \pmod{10\,080}$ ($\lean{a\_1\_2\_6\_9}$), with $w = (40,7,5,56)$,
$\alpha = (9,4,12,45)$, $\beta = (10,0,20,-238)$, $u = (7,40,56,5)$ and
$L = 280$. For $a = (5,7,15,21)$ the class is $m \equiv 4\,056 \pmod{26\,880}$
($\lean{a\_5\_7\_15\_21}$), with $w = (64,32,32,64)$, $\alpha = (155,63,105,21)$,
$\beta = (56,-40,232,-440)$, $u = (1,2,2,1)$ and $L = 64$. For
$a = (2,3,10,15,30)$ the class is the whole residue class $m \equiv 12\,780
\pmod{21\,840}$ ($\lean{a\_2\_3\_10\_15\_30}$), with $w = (182,28,14,52,7)$,
$\alpha = (18,9,90,45,30)$, $\beta = (8,4,-16,228,-24)$, $u = (2,13,26,7,52)$,
$L = 364$ and the identity $\lean{hpoly\_2\_3\_10\_15\_30}$; the class theorem is
$\lean{pwData\_2\_3\_10\_15\_30}$, which is a statement about every member of the
class and not a sample of it. And $a = (1,24,115,384,480)$ is used at the single
point $m = 28$ ($\lean{a\_k5}$), through the lemma with
$m_0 = m$, as \S\ref{sec:pw-members} records.

A family that is not admissible cannot have infinitely many prime members, and
admissibility is the local condition a Dickson-type hypothesis needs. The
restricted forms of the class are $f_i(t) = a_i (Mt + m_0) + 1$, and
$\lean{AdmissibleProg}(a, m_0, M)$ says that for every prime $p$ there is a $t$
with $p \nmid \prod_i f_i(t)$.

The criterion for it is a counting argument in $\Z/p\Z$. Suppose $a_i \mid M$,
$\gcd(M, \lean{famNk}(a,m_0)) = 1$, and every prime $p \le k$ divides $M$. Then
the tuple is admissible ($\lv$ \lean{admissibleProg\_of}), and the proof has two
halves. If $p \mid M$ then every form is congruent to $a_i m_0 + 1$ modulo $p$, so
at $t = 0$ the product is $\lean{famNk}(a,m_0)$, which is coprime to $M$ and
hence to $p$. If $p \nmid M$ then $p > k$, since otherwise $p \le k$ would put
$p \mid M$; each form is affine in $t$ with slope $a_i M \not\equiv 0$ modulo $p$,
so it has exactly one root modulo $p$; the roots are at most $k$ in number, and
$k < p$, so together they cannot cover $\Z/p\Z$; a $t$ outside them makes no form
vanish. One $t$ for each prime is the definition.

The four classes of this development are admissible, and the checks are finite.
The hypothesis of the criterion is supplied by $30 \mid M$ and the fact that every
prime $p \le 5$ divides $30$ ($\lv$ \lean{small\_primes\_dvd}); then
$m \equiv 546 \pmod{720}$ ($\lv$ \lean{admissible\_1\_2\_5\_10}),
$m \equiv 5\,670 \pmod{10\,080}$ ($\lv$ \lean{admissible\_1\_2\_6\_9}),
$m \equiv 4\,056 \pmod{26\,880}$ ($\lv$ \lean{admissible\_5\_7\_15\_21}) and
$m \equiv 12\,780 \pmod{21\,840}$ ($\lv$ \lean{admissible\_2\_3\_10\_15\_30})
are admissible, each by one computation. What admissibility is for is exactly
this and no more: it is the local condition a hypothesis of Dickson type needs,
and a family that fails it has a fixed prime dividing every member of the class,
so its members are prime at most where one of them equals that prime. The
hypothesis itself is not proved anywhere in the book, and
\S\ref{sec:pw-hypothesis} is where it is assumed.

\subsection{One family in full}
\label{sec:pw-evenb}

The families above are four isolated tuples. One family of them is infinite in
its parameter and decided exactly, and it is the one whose three forms are
consecutive: for even $b \ge 2$ put
\[
  p_1 = (b-1)m+1, \qquad p_2 = bm+1, \qquad p_3 = (b+1)m+1,
  \qquad n = p_1 p_2 p_3 ,
\]
and ask when $n$ carries P-W data. Write $b = c+1$, so that $c \ge 1$ is odd, and
$c = 2e+1$.

\begin{theorem}[The even-$b$ family]
\label{thm:pw-even-b}
\lv{} \lean{pwData\_even\_b\_iff}
Let $b \ge 2$ be even and $m \ge 1$, and suppose that $p_1$, $p_2$ and $p_3$ are
prime. Then $n = p_1p_2p_3$ carries P-W data if and only if
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:evenb-class}
  \operatorname{lcm}(8,b) \mid m, \qquad
  2m \equiv -3 \pmod{b-1}, \qquad
  2m \equiv 3 \pmod{b+1} .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The two directions are separate theorems, and the second is the one with content:
the first says that the class is sufficient, the second that it is necessary.

\emph{The class is sufficient} ($\lv$ \lean{pwData\_even\_b}). Assume
 \eqref{eq:evenb-class}; it forces $b < m$ ($\lv$ \lean{evenB\_lt\_of\_class}). From the lcm both $8 \mid m$ and $b \mid m$;
write $m = 8r$ and $m = (c+1)q$, so that $m > b \ge 2$ gives $m \ge c+2$. The
three candidates
\[
  v_1 = \frac{p_1 - c^2}{2}, \qquad v_2 = (c+1)^2, \qquad
  v_3 = \frac{p_3 - (c+2)^2}{2}
\]
are integers: $p_1 - c^2 = 8K_1$ with $K_1 = (2e+1)r - e(e+1)/2$ and
$p_3 - (c+2)^2 = 8K_3$ with $K_3 = (2e+3)r - (e+1)(e+2)/2$, using $m = 8r$,
$c = 2e+1$ and the fact that $e(e+1)$ is even. Each lies in the interval it
must: $K_1, K_3 > 0$ is $m > c+2$, so $v_1$ and $v_3$ are positive, $v_2$ is
positive, $v_1 < p_1$ and $v_3 < p_3$ because $c^2$ and $(c+2)^2$ are positive,
and $v_2 < p_2$ because $(c+1)^2 < (c+1)m+1$. They are all multiples of four:
$v_1 = 4K_1$, $v_3 = 4K_3$, and $v_2$ is the square of the even number $c+1$.
Korselt is a polynomial identity in the parameter: $n-1 = m Q$ with
\[
  Q = 3c+3 + (3c^2+6c+2)\,m + c(c+1)(c+2)\,m^2 ,
\]
read in the three ways
\begin{gather*}
  Q = c\bigl(3 + (3c+6)m + (c+1)(c+2)m^2\bigr) + (2m+3), \\
  Q = (c+1)\bigl(3 + (3c+3)m + c(c+2)m^2\bigr) - m, \\
  Q = (c+2)\bigl(3 + 3cm + c(c+1)m^2\bigr) + (2m-3),
\end{gather*}
so the three divisibilities of \eqref{eq:evenb-class} are exactly
$p_i - 1 \mid n-1$ at $c$, $c+1$ and $c+2$. Every factor is $8r$ times something
plus one, hence $1$ modulo four, and so is the product. The Egyptian sum is the
polynomial identity
\[
  \bigl(p_1-(b-1)^2\bigr)p_2p_3 + 2b^2 p_1p_3 + \bigl(p_3-(b+1)^2\bigr)p_1p_2
  \;=\; 2\,(n-1)
  \qquad
  (\lv\ \lean{evenB\_egyptian}),
\]
which holds for every $b$ and $m$ and is twice the sum
$v_1p_2p_3 + v_2p_1p_3 + v_3p_1p_2$. The generic lemma at the three primes then
packages the data.

\emph{The numerators are forced} ($\lv$ \lean{evenB\_forced\_core}). In the class,
with $c+1 \mid m$ and $m > 0$, any integers $v_1 \in (0,p_1)$, $v_2 \in (0,p_2)$
and $v_3 \in (0,p_3)$ with $p_i$ dividing $v_i$ times the product of the other two
factors plus one satisfy
\[
  2v_1 = p_1 - c^2, \qquad v_2 = (c+1)^2, \qquad
  2v_3 = p_3 - (c+2)^2 \ \ \text{if} \ \ c+1 < m .
\]
Write
$m = (c+1)q$, so $m \ge c+1$ and $m$ is even, and put
$V_1 = c(e+1)q - 2e(e+1)$ and $V_3 = (c+2)(e+1)q - 2(e+1)(e+2)$; then
$2V_1 = p_1 - c^2$ and $2V_3 = p_3 - (c+2)^2$. The number $V_1$ lies in
$(0,p_1)$ and $p_1 \mid V_1 p_2p_3 + 1$, because $2(V_1p_2p_3+1)$ is an explicit
multiple of $p_1$ and $p_1$ is odd, so the factor $2$ cancels in a divisibility by
an odd prime ($\lv$ \lean{dvd\_of\_dvd\_two\_mul}); and the numerator in $(0,p)$
is unique ($\lv$ \lean{resid\_unique}). Hence $v_1 = V_1$, $v_2 = (c+1)^2$, and
$v_3 = V_3$ once $m > c+1$.

\emph{The class is necessary} ($\lv$ \lean{evenB\_class\_of\_pwData}). Assume the
P-W data, extract Korselt at the three primes and the numerators $v$, and write
$b = c+1$. Since $n-1 = mQ$ with the $Q$ above and $p_i - 1 = c, c+1, c+2$ times
$m$, Korselt at the three primes gives $c \mid Q$, $c+1 \mid Q$ and $c+2 \mid Q$;
the three readings of $Q$ turn those into $c \mid 2m+3$, $c+1 \mid m$ and
$c+2 \mid 2m-3$, which is \eqref{eq:evenb-class} with $b$ in place of $c+1$. The
numerators are the forced ones, by the previous paragraph, and its third
conclusion needs $m > c+1$: if $m = c+1$ then $c+2 \mid 2m-3 = 2c-1$ and
$c+2 \mid 2(c+2)-5$ give $c+2 \mid 5$, which no odd $c \ge 1$ allows. Finally
$4 \mid v_1$: with $c = 2e+1$ and $m = 2s$ one has $v_1 = cs - 2e(e+1)$, and
$4 \mid 2e(e+1)$ because $e(e+1)$ is even, so $4 \mid cs$ with $c$ odd, that is
$4 \mid s$ and $8 \mid m$. Together with $b = c+1 \mid m$ this is
$\operatorname{lcm}(8,b) \mid m$.

The equivalence is the two directions composed. Its side condition $b < m$ is not
an extra hypothesis: the class forces it, since $m = b$ would give $b+1 \mid 5$
and $8 \mid b$ at once, and no even $b \ge 2$ satisfies both ($\lv$
\lean{evenB\_lt\_of\_class}). In the class the witnesses are moreover unique:
every vector of numerators at the three primes equals
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:evenb-witness}
  \Bigl(\frac{p_1-(b-1)^2}{2},\;\; b^2,\;\; \frac{p_3-(b+1)^2}{2}\Bigr)
\end{equation}
($\lv$ \lean{evenB\_v\_forced}), which is the previous paragraph read with the
cofactors of $n$ at its three prime factors identified ($\lv$ \lean{evenB\_cof},
$\lv$ \lean{evenB\_mem}).
\end{proof}

A member's numerators are determined by a cofactor's class and by nothing else,
and the coordinate is the one Chernick's family already used in Part~III. Let
$n = p m$ with $p$ prime, $p \nmid m$, $m > 1$, $p-1 \mid n-1$, and let
$v \in (0,p)$ satisfy $p \mid vm+1$; put $j = (m-1)/(p-1)$, the cofactor's $j$
and not $(n-1)/(p-1)$. Then $j \ge 2$, $p \nmid j-1$, and
$v(j-1) = 1 + sp$ with $0 \le s \le j-2$ ($\lv$ \lean{jp\_lemma}). The proof is
three congruences: $p-1 \mid m-1$ follows from $p-1 \mid pm-1$, so $j$ is an
integer and $m = 1+j(p-1) \equiv 1-j$ modulo $p$; $j \equiv 1$ would make $m \equiv
0$, against $p \nmid m$; and $vm \equiv -1$ with $m \equiv 1-j$ gives
$v(j-1) \equiv 1$, the bounds on $s$ coming from $1 \le v \le p-1$ and $j \ge 2$.
On P-W data the cofactor of $p$ is $n/p$, so every member's numerator at every
prime obeys those conclusions ($\lv$ \lean{jp\_of\_pwData}). The lemma is stated
here and not used below: its content is that a member's numerators are pinned by
the cofactors, which is what makes the forced formula of \eqref{eq:evenb-witness}
a formula and not a choice.

The family meets the Chernick numbers of Part~III at $b = 2$. There the three
forms are $m+1$, $2m+1$, $3m+1$, which at $m = 6k$ are
$\lean{Chernick.P}\,k$, $\lean{Chernick.Q}\,k$ and $\lean{Chernick.R}\,k$, and
their product is $N(\lean{Chernick.xk}\, k)$
($\lv$ \lean{evenB\_two\_chernick}, $\lv$ \lean{Chernick.N\_xk} of
\S\ref{sec:chernick}); and the class \eqref{eq:evenb-class} at $b = 2$ is exactly
$m = 6k$ with $4 \mid k$ ($\lv$ \lean{evenB\_two\_class}), which is the
hypothesis of \S\ref{sec:chernick}'s classification of the Chernick numbers at
which the criterion has no witness. Hence, for $4 \mid k$ and the three primes,
the Chernick number carries
P-W data ($\lv$ \lean{pwData\_chernick}). Read with \S\ref{sec:chernick}, the two
sections say the same thing from opposite sides: there $4 \mid k$ is what makes the
criterion vanish identically at a
Chernick number, here $4 \mid k$ is what puts it in the class whose members
are P-W members. The middle numerator of the family has the same reading: with
$j_2 = (p_1p_3-1)/(p_2-1)$ one has $(j_2-1)b^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{p_2}$, that is
$v_2 = b^2$ is $(j_2-1)^{-1}$ at $p_2$ ($\lv$ \lean{evenB\_j2\_crosscheck}).

\begin{example}[The member with seven prime factors]
\label{ex:pw-seven}
The row of the table without a family is also the one member with more than five
prime factors:
\[
  718\,132\,609\,376\,191\,497\,601
  \;=\; 17 \cdot 61 \cdot 113 \cdot 1\,009 \cdot 2\,017 \cdot 8\,677 \cdot 347\,041,
\]
with numerators $(4,4,12,388,140,1\,196,400)$ at the seven primes, all divisible
by four. It is certified directly, not through a class
($\lv$ \lean{pw\_instance\_d7}), and it is the row that shows the members are not
confined to the four- and five-term families of
\S\ref{sec:pw-propagate}. Its size is in the band $(10^{18}, 10^{21}]$, and no
minimality is claimed for it.
\end{example}

\subsection{What a hypothesis would add}
\label{sec:pw-hypothesis}

Nothing above decides P-W. What a hypothesis would buy is stated here in the form
of corollaries that carry the hypothesis in their signature, and this is the form
alone: what the hypothesis \emph{is}, and what the problem reduces to under it, is
the next section's subject.

For a four-tuple $a$ and a class $m \equiv m_0 \pmod M$,
$\lean{DicksonFor}(a,m_0,M)$ says that for every bound $B$ there is $m > B$ in the
class with all four $a_i m + 1$ prime, and $\lean{DicksonForK}$ says the same for
$k$ forms. Both are definitions of a proposition, neither is proved anywhere in
the book, and the conjecture behind them is Dickson's \cite{Dickson1904}, refined
by Bateman--Horn \cite{BatemanHorn1962}.

With the data of a class the problem follows: if $\lean{DicksonFor}$ holds for a
class whose propagation data are those of \eqref{eq:pw-data}, then P-W holds
($\lvCond$ \lean{pwProblem\_of\_dicksonFor}), because for a bound $n_0$ one takes
$m$ in the class with $m > \max(n_0, m_0)$ and all four forms prime, and the
product $\lean{famNk}(a,m)$ exceeds $m$, hence $n_0$, and carries P-W data by
\S\ref{sec:pw-propagate}.

The four classes of \S\ref{sec:pw-propagate} are instances, one line each:
$m \equiv 546 \pmod{720}$ ($\lvCond$ \lean{pwProblem\_of\_dickson\_1\_2\_5\_10}),
$m \equiv 5\,670 \pmod{10\,080}$ ($\lvCond$
\lean{pwProblem\_of\_dickson\_1\_2\_6\_9}), $m \equiv 4\,056 \pmod{26\,880}$
($\lvCond$ \lean{pwProblem\_of\_dickson\_5\_7\_15\_21}), and the same first
statement re-derived through the general-$k$ lemma ($\lvCond$
\lean{pwProblem\_of\_dickson\_1\_2\_5\_10\_via\_k}), plus the five-term family
$m \equiv 12\,780 \pmod{21\,840}$ with $\lean{DicksonForK}$ in the signature
($\lvCond$ \lean{pwProblem\_of\_dickson\_2\_3\_10\_15\_30}, through
$\lean{lt\_famNk}$). Each of the six is a conditional statement and none of them
is $\lv$: the hypothesis is in the signature, and the unconditional problem is
untouched by them.

One corollary needs no class at all: from P-W itself, for every $n_0$ there is
$x \ge 2$ with $N(x) > n_0$, $N(x)$ composite and $\lean{AllZero}\, x$ ($\lvCond$
\lean{infinitely\_many\_counterexamples\_of\_pw}), by Theorem~\ref{thm:pw-iff} and
the fact that $\omega(N(x)) \ge 2$ forbids primality, the prime factors of a prime
being a single one. That is what P-W would be worth to the criterion --- the
statement that its counterexamples are not a finite accident --- and it is the
last sentence of the section that assumes anything.

\subsection{What is certified, and what is not decided}
\label{sec:pw-close}

What the section has certified is a list and not a theory. The vanishing on the Korselt
locus is the arithmetic condition of Theorem~\ref{thm:pw-at}; the two forms of
the question are the same statement (Theorem~\ref{thm:pw-iff}); eight members
carry kernel-checked data, and Table~\ref{tab:pw-members} is what this
development certifies and not a census; a member propagates along its class
(Theorem~\ref{thm:pw-propagate}); the four classes of the development are
admissible; and the even-$b$ family, at prime $p_1$, $p_2$ and $p_3$, is decided exactly by
 the three divisibilities of \eqref{eq:evenb-class}
 (Theorem~\ref{thm:pw-even-b}).

What the section has not decided is whether P-W holds. No statement of it says
that there are infinitely many numbers of that kind; the seven corollaries of
\S\ref{sec:pw-hypothesis} all carry a hypothesis in their signature, and
$\lean{PWProblem}$ itself is a definition, not a claim. The table is finite, and
the one infinite family the section treats does not settle the problem either: it
characterises each Chernick-class member, and it does not say that the class
contains infinitely many prime triples, which is Dickson's conjecture at
$b = 2$ and not a theorem.

So the counterexamples of Parts~III--VI reduce to one problem, stated without
the criterion --- six clauses about a squarefree number, its numerators and an
Egyptian sum --- and the problem is open. It names none of the parameters of the
formula of 2009.
